Principles & Framework of Law of Evidence in Tanzania

Topic 1: Introduction to Law of Evidence in Tanzania

1. Fundamental Concepts of Evidence

Evidence presented in a court of law must be duly proved and legally admissible. For example, a documentary instrument generally requires appropriate certification or stamping where mandated by law to be accepted as evidence.

Statutory Provision: Section 3(1) of the Evidence Act provides the statutory interpretation and legal definition of "evidence" as denotes the means by which an alleged matter of fact, the truth of which if submitted to investigation, is proved or disproved; and without prejudice to the preceding generality, includes statements and admissions by accused persons;

To establish proof, the law provides procedural mechanisms to summon witnesses and adduce testimony, enabling the court to evaluate whether the evidence is sufficient to meet the required standard of proof.

Legal Evaluation & Correction:

Note on "Have evidence without evidence?": In evidentiary jurisprudence, facts may be established without formal physical or oral evidence through Judicial Notice (facts so notorious that proof is unnecessary) or formal Admissions made by parties prior to or during proceedings.

2. Classification and Types of Evidence

The principal classifications of evidence adduced before judicial tribunals include:

  • Oral Evidence: Evidence given viva voce (by word of mouth) by a competent witness speaking on matters within their personal knowledge. Under statutory exceptions, oral evidence may be adduced through written affidavits, or via interpreters/symbols where a witness cannot speak.
  • Documentary Evidence: Statements expressed or described upon any material substance by means of letters, figures, or marks, intended to be used for recording permanent communications.
  • Direct Evidence: Evidence that directly proves a fact in issue without requiring inferences or presumptions (for example, testimony of an eyewitness who directly perceived the event).
  • Circumstantial Evidence: Evidence of relevant facts from which the existence of a fact in issue can be logically inferred. It relies on a chain of connected facts to arrive at a legal conclusion.
  • Real Evidence: Physical objects or material items produced for the inspection of the court that directly relate to the issue (for example, weapons, clothing, tools).
  • Primary Evidence: The original document or object itself produced for inspection by the court, considered the highest quality of evidence.
  • Secondary Evidence: Evidence admissible in the absence of primary evidence (for example, certified copies, mechanical duplicates, or oral accounts of the contents of a document).
  • Electronic Evidence: Data generated, stored, or transmitted via electronic or digital devices (for example, computer logs, mobile records, digital media, digital signatures).
  • Opinion Evidence: Evidence provided by an expert or layperson regarding inferences drawn from facts, where the court requires specialized expertise or judgment.
  • Exculpatory Evidence: Evidence that tends to clear an accused individual of guilt or liability in criminal or civil proceedings.

3. Historical Development of the Law of Evidence

The law of evidence evolved from primitive mechanisms such as trials by ordeal, combat, or customary rituals toward structured adversarial systems. Historical legal traditions, including medieval practice and Islamic legal systems (which historically emphasized specific witness qualifications and categories like Hadd), shaped formal rules governing witness competency and corroboration.

System Models: Modern evidence systems operate primarily under the Adversarial system (common law) or the Inquisitorial system (civil law tradition).

4. Why Legal Practitioners Study the Law of Evidence

  1. To ascertain and establish the truth of facts in dispute before a judicial tribunal.
  2. To evaluate the admissibility, relevance, and probative weight of different forms of evidence.
  3. To formulate effective legal strategies and ensure proper judicial decision-making.
  4. To meet the standard of proof required to secure a conviction or civil judgment.
  5. To understand the statutory and case law framework governing evidentiary procedures in Tanzania.
  6. To appreciate the role of evidence in shaping legal outcomes and ensuring justice.

Topic 2: Relevancy and Admissibility of Facts

1. Foundations of Relevancy and Admissibility

Under Section 3(1) of the Law of Evidence Act [Cap. 6 R.E. 2023], a "fact" is not limited merely to tangible objects that can be seen or touched. It includes any mental condition or state of mind of which any person is conscious, such as feelings, emotions, or intentional actions demonstrated in court.

General Principle: Not all facts are admissible as evidence in court. Only facts that are legally relevant and admissible under the Act may be adduced as evidence. If a fact lacks relevancy or fails the legal test of admissibility, it cannot be received as evidence.

Determination of Relevancy [Section 3(1)]

"Fact in Issue" refers to any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature, or extent of any right, liability, or disability asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding necessarily follows.

"Relevancy": One fact is said to be relevant to another when the one is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of the Evidence Act relating to the relevancy of facts.

Legal Evaluation (Adversarial System & Judicial Discretion):

In an adversarial legal system, the parties initially determine which facts to present as relevant. However, the judge or magistrate retains ultimate judicial discretion to rule on whether a piece of evidence is relevant and legally admissible. When a witness's testimony contradicts previous statements, the court assesses credibility (impeachment of witness under Sections 154–157 TEA) before deciding the weight of the evidence.

2. Admissibility and General Rules of Relevancy

Admissibility is a question of law. Some facts may be logically relevant but legally inadmissible due to exclusionary rules (for example, hearsay or privileged communications). Conversely, facts must first be relevant to be admitted.

General Rule of Relevancy (Section 7 TEA)

  1. Evidence may be given in any suit or proceeding of the existence or non-existence of every fact in issue and of such other facts as are declared to be relevant by the Act, and of no others.
  2. Parties can argue the existence or non-existence of facts in issue or relevant facts.
  3. Evidence adduced must be strictly confined to the relationship with the fact in issue or facts declared relevant.
  4. Only evidence recognized and permitted by law is admissible.

3. Facts Forming Part of the Same Transaction (Res Gestae - Section 8 TEA)

Facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the **same transaction** are relevant, whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places.

  • Contemporaneity Test: Spontaneous statements or acts must be substantially contemporaneous with the fact in issue to exclude the possibility of fabrication or afterthought.
  • Hearsay Exception: Statements forming part of the res gestae are admissible as exceptions to the rule against hearsay, provided they are uttered while the act is occurring or immediately thereafter.
Case of: Republic v. Bedingfield (1879) 14 Cox CC 341
Facts:
The accused Henry Bedingfield had a prior relationship with the deceased woman. They were having some disputes in their relationship. Bedingfield allegedly harboured resentment towards the woman due to her refusal to fulfil a proposal made by the defendant and her desire to end their relationship. This led him to issue violent threats, including threats to kill her. The victim ran a laundry business and had two assistants helping her. The night before the incident, the deceased informed local policemen about the threats and requested them to keep watch over her house. Early the next morning, Bedingfield visited her home which was unusual for him. They spent some time together and after a while, he left for a store. The woman’s two assistants came and saw her lying on the floor senseless. The defendant returned to the house and entered her room while her assistants were in the yard. A few moments later, the victim came out of her house screaming, holding her throat, which was bleeding from a deep cut. She reportedly told her assistants, “Oh, aunt, see what Bedingfield has done to me!” Within a few moments, she succumbed to her injuries on the spot.
Issues:
1. Is the statement made by the deceased before her death admissible as evidence?
2. Does the statement fall under the exception of “Res Gestae”?
Arguments:
1. Though the prosecution did not bring the dying statement directly, they initially submitted it as evidence in the court. They argued that, according to the witnesses, the victim was so frightened when she was giving the statement. Therefore, the prosecution tried to impose the doctrine of res gestae against the defendant.
2. A question was raised by the defence lawyer that, the incident could have been a suicide rather than murder. But according to the medical experts, the wound of her throat was extensive, measuring three to four inches and had severed the trachea and the jugular vein. Consequent they ruled out suicide as a possibility.
1. The defendant argued that “res gestae” requires that the statement be part of the act itself, not something said afterwards. Res gestae requires spontaneity and an immediate connection to the crime, without any opportunity for reflection or fabrication. Since the victim made the statement after leaving the crime scene, it was not part of the continuous transaction of the event.
2. The prosecution did not reintroduce the victim’s statement as a dying declaration after medical testimony was given. The defence highlighted that, for a dying declaration to be admissible, the declarant must have a full understanding of their imminent death. Since there was no proof that the victim believed she was dying when making the statement, it failed to meet the legal criteria for admissibility.
Decisions:
Lord Cockburn CJ ruled that the statement was inadmissible, as it was made after the attack had ended. The Court also noted that the statement was not admissible as a dying declaration because the deceased did not express a belief that she was about to die. Moreover, there was no evidence that she had lost all hopes of recovery from the injury when making the statement. Besides, he stated that it could not be considered part of the res gestae since it was not made during the incident but afterwards, giving room for possible fabrication. Despite this, other evidence strongly pointed to the defendant’s guilt. The nature of the victim’s injuries and the position of the razor suggested an intentional attack rather than an accident or self-inflicted harm. Cockburn CJ emphasized to the jury that it was the deceased woman, not the accused, who ran out, seemingly to raise an alarm or make a complaint. Based on these factors, the Court found Bedingfield guilty of murder, and he was sentenced to death.
Relevant Principle :
Doctrine of Res Gestae : When something is so closely connected with the main fact that the two can be considered parts of the same transaction, it is called res gestae and is admissible as evidence. Res gestae is an exception to the rule against hearsay.
Case of: Ramadhani Ismail v. The Crown (1945) 7 Z.L.R. 36
A young child, having been raped, went crying straight to her home (only 2 or 3 houses away), had a conversation with her parents, and then led her father to the house where she had been assaulted. On arrival at the accused's house, she said 'That is the Bwana'. It was held that this utterance COULD NOT be considered part of the res gestae when it comes to proving the truth of the matter.

The appellant appealed from a conviction of murder. The deceased had been killed with a dagger. Evidence was admitted that the deceased's brother was assaulted with a dagger and uttered threats against the deceased (words spoken by appellant: 'I have finished you and I am going to show your brother'). 5. Fact showing motive,intention, preparation and subsequent conduct (Section 10 TEA)

Under Section 10 of the Evidence Act, any fact is relevant which shows or constitutes a motive or preparation for any fact in issue or relevant fact.

  • Motive vs. Intention: Intention relates to the immediate purpose or act desired, whereas motive refers to the ultimate ulterior reason or emotion inducing a person to act.
  • Preparation: Acts or measures undertaken prior to committing an offense (for example, buying a weapon, purchasing poison) are relevant if connected to the fact in issue.
  • Subsequent Conduct: Conduct of any party or accused person following an offense such as absconding, fabricating false evidence, or destroying clues is admissible as relevant conduct under Section 10(2).

5. Facts Necessary to Explain or Introduce Relevant Facts (Section 11 TEA)

Facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact are admissible in so far as they are necessary for that purpose. Section 11 covers:

  1. Facts necessary to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  2. Facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  3. Facts establishing the identity of any person or thing whose identity is relevant (for example, identification parades).
  4. Facts establishing the time or place at which any fact in issue or relevant fact occurred.
  5. Facts showing the relation of parties by whom any such fact was transacted.

Section 11 of the Evidence Act declares relevant facts that are intended either to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact (such as names, dates, and places, which may be admissible).

  • Introductory or Explanatory Facts: Names, descriptions, places, and times are admissible under sub-section (1).
  • Supporting or Rebutting Inferences: It admits facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  • Identity of Person or Object: Identity may be established using identification parades, DNA, or direct witness testimony.
  • Fixing Time and Place: Facts which fix the exact time and place when or where an event happened.
  • Relationship of Parties: Facts showing the relationship of parties (e.g., cohabitation, employment, agency).

Procedure & Rules Requirement for Identification Parade in Tanzania

Identification parades in Tanzania are governed by police procedural guidelines under P.G.O. 232 (Police General Orders) read together with Form 3 (P.F. 186):

  1. The officer shall register the number of persons attending the parade (minimum 8 persons of similar age, height, and general appearance).
  2. He/she will register the number of witnesses under Form 3 / P.F. 186.
  3. The officer-in-charge of the case shall take no part in conducting the parade.
  4. The officer conducting the parade shall inform the suspect of his right to put on parade identification options (e.g., choice of position or presence of advocate/friend).
  5. The place of the parade must have good light.
  6. The suspect may put on the clothing he was wearing when the offense was committed during the parade.
  7. The officer conducting the parade will finally check entries in the identification parade register and sign in the space provided.
Omary Hussein @ Ludanga & Another v. Republic (Criminal Appeal No. 547 of 2017) [2021] TZCA 543
Facts:
On March 16, 2013, Sister Mary Shobana (the victim) and her driver, Erick Justine Kimanguka (PW7), were accosted by two armed assailants outside the gate of Notredame School in Njiro, Arusha. The assailants shot the victim in the leg, ordered PW7 to lay face-down on the steering wheel, and stole TZS 30,000,000/= before escaping on a motorcycle. Following an informant's tip, the appellants were arrested at a hotel. PW7 identified the 1st appellant during an identification parade. Cautioned statements were recorded where the appellants allegedly admitted involvement, though they later disowned them during trial claiming torture. The Resident Magistrates' Court convicted both appellants of armed robbery under section 287A of the Penal Code and sentenced them to 30 years imprisonment, a conviction upheld by the High Court.
Issues:
1. Whether the 1st appellant was properly identified by PW7 at the scene and during the identification parade.
2. Whether the appellants' cautioned statements and the identification parade register were properly admitted into evidence.
3. Whether the prosecution's failure to call crucial witnesses (the ballistic expert and the victim) entitled the court to draw an adverse inference.
4. Whether the offence of armed robbery was proved beyond reasonable doubt.
Court's Holding:
Identification Vitiated: The 1st appellant was not properly identified because PW7 failed to give a detailed physical description of the suspect to the police prior to participating in the identification parade. Exhibits Expunged: Although the identification parade register (Exhibit P6) and cautioned statements (Exhibits P1 & P5) were admitted, they were never read out in open court. This failure constituted a fatal, incurable procedural irregularity under section 388 of the Criminal Procedure Act (CPA), rendering them of no evidential value. Adverse Inference Drawn: The prosecution failed without sufficient reason to call the ballistic expert (to link the 2nd appellant's pistol to the crime scene) and the victim, Sister Mary Shobana. Under section 143 of the Evidence Act, the Court drew an adverse inference that their evidence would have been unfavorable to the prosecution. Verdict: With the identification evidence vitiated, key exhibits expunged, and adverse inferences drawn, no evidence remained to sustain the conviction. The Court allowed the appeal, quashed the convictions, set aside the 30-year sentences, and ordered the immediate release of both appellants. Precedents Applied

6. Conspiracy & Common Intention (Section 12 TEA)

Section 12 TEA: Statements or actions referring to common intention. Anything said, done, or written by a conspirator in reference to their common design is a relevant fact against each of the persons believed to be so conspiring.

Principle of Agency: This section builds upon the agency principle—treating every individual who is involved in a conspiracy as an agent of another co-conspirator.

Two Essential Conditions Required to Apply Section 12:
  1. There must be prima facie / sufficient evidence of an agreement between those parties (conspiracy).
  2. There was a common intention by the parties to execute their plan at the time the act or statement was made.

7. Defense of Alibi & Inconsistent Facts (Section 13 TEA)

Section 13 TEA: Facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact, or if by themselves or in connection with other facts they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable.

Definition of Alibi: A form of defense whereby the accused attempts to prove that he/she was elsewhere when the crime in question was committed.

Procedural Requirements for Alibi Defense in Tanzania

  • Notice of Alibi: Governed by Section 200(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act [Cap. 20 R.E. 2023]. The accused must give notice of intention to rely on an alibi during preliminary hearing or before prosecution closes its case.
  • Failure to Plead Early: Failure to give timely notice allows court to draw adverse inference or disallow late alibi evidence without leave.
  • Examples of Supporting Evidence: Eyewitness testimony, time-stamped pictures/video footage placing the accused at another location, bank/ATM receipts, GPS log data.

8. Damages and Customary Rights (Sections 14 & 15 TEA)

Facts Relevant in Suits for Damages (Section 14 TEA)

In suits in which damages are claimed, any fact which will enable the court to determine the amount of damages which ought to be awarded is relevant (whether to mitigate or aggravate damages).

Facts Affecting Existence of Right or Custom (Section 15 TEA)

Where the existence of any right or custom is in question, the following are relevant:

  • Any transaction by which the right or custom was created, claimed, modified, recognized, asserted, or denied.
  • Particular instances in which the right or custom was claimed, recognized, exercised, or disputed.

9. Facts Showing State of Mind / Body & System (Sections 16 & 17 TEA)

State of Mind or Feeling (Section 16 TEA)

Facts showing the existence of any state of mind (such as intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, rashness, ill-will, or good-will) or state of body/bodily feeling are relevant when the existence of such state of mind or body is in issue.

John Makindi v. Republic: Evidence of similar transactions/previous conduct is admissible to show knowledge, intention, or state of mind, but cannot be used as sole proof of guilt for the specific charged offense.

System / Similar Occurrences (Section 17 TEA)

When there is a question whether an act was accidental or intentional, or done with particular knowledge, the fact that such act formed part of a series of similar occurrences is relevant.

  • Must be read together with Section 16 (state of mind).
  • Requires a specific line of defense alleging lack of intention, accident, or mistake.
  • The similar acts must be closely related in nature and time to show a systematic course of conduct.

Four Basic Points of Section 17
1. Rebuttal of Defenses: Intends to allow evidence of facts that rebut and refute defenses of mistake, accident, or other innocent intentions.
2. Prerequisite Defense: Requires a specific line of defense to be raised by the accused before evidence of similar occurrences can be tendered.
3. Requirement of Similarity: The acts sought to be introduced in evidence must be similar to those under inquiry.
4. Sufficiency of Acts: A single similar act is sufficient to prove knowledge and rebut accident or mistake; however, a single act is not necessarily adequate to prove a systematic course of conduct.

Conclusion:​When the accused asserts that the act occurred accidentally or without guilty knowledge, Section 17 applies to admit evidence of similar occurrences to disprove that assertion.

Topic 3: ADMISSIONS & HEARSAY

1. The General Rule of Direct Evidence & Hearsay

General Rule (Section 62 TEA): Evidence must be direct. Oral evidence must, in all cases whatever, be direct (that is, given by a witness who saw, heard, or perceived the fact himself).

Exceptions: Where evidence is permitted or given by someone else, it constitutes an exception to the rule against hearsay evidence (for example, Admissions, Confessions, and Res Gestae).

Definition of Admission (Sections 19–27 TEA): An admission is a statement oral, electronic, or documentary which suggests any inference as to any fact in issue or relevant fact, made by any of the persons and under the circumstances specified under the Act.

Note on Admissions: For a statement to qualify as a relevant admission under Sections 19–27 of the TEA, it should generally not be in the self-serving interest of the maker when tendered.

2. Proof and Usage of Admissions (Section 23 TEA)

General Rule under Section 23 TEA: An admission may be proved against the person who makes it (or his representative in interest), but it cannot be proved by or on behalf of the person who makes it, except in specific statutory instances.

  • Prohibition against Self-Serving Statements: A party is not permitted to bring evidence of his or her own admission to support their own case.
  • Legal Maxim / Rationale: No person should be allowed to benefit from their own wrongdoing or self-fabricated statements (Nemo ex suo dolo interdictur).

Statutory Exceptions under Section 23(a)–(c) TEA

An admission may be proved by or on behalf of the person making it under the following exceptions:

  1. Section 23(a): When the statement is of such a nature that, if the person making it were dead, it would be relevant as between third persons under Section 34 TEA (Statements by persons who cannot be called as witnesses).
  2. Section 23(b): When the admission consists of a statement of the existence of any state of mind or body, made at or about the time when such state of mind or body existed, and is accompanied by conduct rendering its falsehood improbable.
  3. Section 23(c): If the statement in question is relevant otherwise than as an admission (that is, admissible under any other provision of the law).

3. Who Can Make Admissions? (Sections 20–22 TEA)

According to Section 20 of the Evidence Act, admissions can be made by:

  • Parties to the proceeding.
  • Agents authorized by parties.
  • Parties suing or being sued in a representative character (when the statement was made while holding that character).
  • Persons who have a proprietary or pecuniary interest in the subject matter.
  • Persons from whom the parties to the suit have derived their interest.
Specific Statutory Provisions:
  • Section 21 TEA: Statements made by persons whose position must be proved as against a party to the suit are admissions if they would be relevant in a suit brought by or against them.
  • Section 22 TEA: Statements made by persons to whom a party to the suit has expressly referred for information in reference to a matter in dispute are admissions.

4. Oral Admissions, Written Documents & Civil Cases

Oral Admissions as to Contents of Documents (Section 24 TEA)

General Rule: Oral admissions as to the contents of a document are not relevant, unless and until the party proposing to prove them shows that he is entitled to give secondary evidence of the contents under statutory rules, or unless the genuineness of a document produced is in question.

Admissions in Civil Cases (Section 25 TEA)

In civil cases, no admission is relevant if it is made either upon an express condition that evidence of it is not to be given (for example, "without prejudice" negotiations), or under circumstances from which the court can infer that the parties agreed together that evidence of it should not be given.

Evidential Effect of Admissions (Section 26 TEA)

Section 26 TEA: Admissions are not conclusive proof of the matters admitted, but they may operate as an estoppel to preclude a party from denying what they previously stated.

Case: Slatterie v. Pooley (1840) 6 M. & W. 664; 151 E.R. 579
Facts
The plaintiff sued the defendant on a covenant contained in a deed to indemnify him against certain debts. A dispute arose regarding whether a particular debt was listed in a schedule attached to a composition deed. The plaintiff sought to prove the contents of the document by introducing evidence of an oral admission made by the defendant, in which the defendant admitted that the debt was indeed included in the schedule. The plaintiff did so without producing the original deed or establishing the legal foundation (for example, loss or destruction of the original) required to introduce secondary evidence.
Legal Issue
Whether an out-of-court admission made by a party regarding the contents of a written document is admissible as primary evidence against that party, without accounting for the non-production of the original written document under the Best Evidence Rule.
Holding:
The Court held that a voluntary admission made by a party against their own interest regarding the contents of a written document is admissible as primary evidence against that party. The party offering the admission is not required to produce the original document or account for its absence before introducing the admission.
Rationale:
Self-Interest Premise: What a party voluntarily admits to be true against their own interest is reasonably presumed to be true.
Exemption from Best Evidence Rule: The court created an exception to the strict Best Evidence Rule, asserting that admissions operate as primary evidence rather than secondary evidence. Therefore, procedural requirements for accounting for missing originals do not apply when a direct admission of a party is offered.

Hearsay vs. Admissions / Res Gestae

If evidence is brought into court for the sole purpose of establishing the truth of the matter stated, it will generally be excluded as hearsay. However, if it is introduced for another legal purpose—such as proving an admission, confession, or res gestae—it is admissible as an exception to the hearsay rule.

TOPIC 4: CONFESSIONS

1. Meaning of Confession

Refers to the statement that is voluntarily made by the accused person which admits or incriminates the criminal liability by the accused, and this statement is made in the presence of a person authorized to administer confession. For a statement to amount to a confession under Tanzanian law, it must be an unambiguous, direct admission of guilt containing all essential elements of the offense charged not merely a series of exculpatory or neutral statements.

(Constitutes Admission of actus reus and element of mens rea).

2. Admissibility & Evidentiary Value

Section 27(3)(b) of TEA: Confession is considered to be one of the best evidence in criminal proceedings because an accused freely agreed that they were liable.

Principle: No reasonable person would make a statement against their own interest unless it is a truthful matter.

Generally, confession statements are admissible if they were made voluntarily (S. 27 of TEA). However, in instances where the law permits the court to admit a confession obtained through threat, if it is in the opinion of the court that the confession contains nothing but the truth of the matter.

3. Voluntariness and Court Position

While it is true to law that the condition precedent for the admissibility of confession is its voluntary nature, the confession is not automatically admissible simply because it resulted from threat or promise where the inducement or threat was of such a nature as was likely to cause an untrue admission of guilt.

Plea-bargain in Criminal Procedure Act is similar to confession, but this does not constitute the amount of threat or promise which influences the accused to accept or to admit the liability of the offence.

    5. Guiding Principles in Confessions

    Case law has established several rules that assist the court when dealing with confession statements:

    Core Guiding Principles:

    1. A confession is presumed to be obtained voluntarily except where an objection is made by an accused.
    2. Where an accused makes an objection to the admissibility of a confession, they may do so on the ground that the confession was not made voluntarily, or it was not made at all, or it was made contrary to the Law.
    3. An objection to the admissibility of a confession must be done when or before a confession was admitted in court.
    4. The court cannot conduct an inquiry to test the voluntariness of a confession on its own motion.
    5. Whenever a confession is objected to at the right moment on the issue of voluntariness or whether the statement was recorded or not, the court must conduct a trial-within-a-trial or inquiry to determine the objection.
    6. In the trial-within-a-trial or inquiry, the court should only deal with whether the accused person made the statement at all or whether it was obtained voluntarily.

    6. Repudiated and Retracted Confessions

    • Retracted Confession: Refers to a statement where a person admits making the recorded statement, but claims it was not made voluntarily.
    • Repudiated Confession: Refers to a confession where the accused person argues that they never made such a statement at all.
    When there is retraction or repudiation, a conviction can be ruled using the repudiation or retraction, but the court should only do so after it has warned itself on the danger of acting on such confessions. It also requires that the court should satisfy itself that the confession is nothing but the truthfulness of the matter.
    Case: Muganyize Peter Michaer and 3 Others v. Republic, Criminal Appeal No. 144 of 2022
    Facts
    The Incident: On October 13, 2012, at Kitangiri in Ilemela District, Mwanza, Regional Police Commander Liberatus Barlow was shot dead at point-blank range while in his vehicle with PW1. The assailants stole his radio call, ignition keys, and PW1’s phone.
    Confessions Recorded: Upon arrest, the 1st (Muganyizi), 2nd (Magige), and 3rd (Abdallah) appellants made cautioned statements (Exhibits P33, P32, and P25) confessing their participation in a robbery spree that led to the shooting. The 1st appellant admitted to pulling the trigger, while the 3rd appellant implicated the 4th appellant.
    Retraction & Objections: At trial, all appellants repudiated their statements, claiming they were tortured or forced to sign. The 3rd appellant argued his statement was recorded past the statutory 4-hour limit under Section 50 of the CPA, and that the trial judge improperly ruled on this procedural objection within a trial-within-a-trial without assessors.
    Retraction & Objections:x The High Court relied on the confessions, eyewitness identification, and matching fingerprints to convict all four appellants of murder, sentencing them to death. They appealed to the Court of Appeal.
    Issue
    Whether the 3rd appellant's cautioned statement was recorded outside the prescribed statutory period.
    Whether determining its admissibility in a trial-within-a-trial without assessors vitiated the trial.
    Whether the retracted/repudiated confessional statements of the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd appellants were reliable and sufficient to ground their convictions.
    Whether the confession of the 3rd appellant could be used to convict the 4th appellant under Section 33 of the Evidence Act.
    Holding:
    On Procedural Validity: The 3rd appellant's statement was recorded within time because the hours spent traveling between search locations were excluded under Section 50(2)(a) of the CPA. Hearing procedural objections during a trial-within-a-trial without assessors was an immaterial irregularity that caused no prejudice.
    On Retracted Confessions: Repudiated confessions can ground a conviction without corroboration if the court warns itself of the danger and is satisfied the statements are true. The confessions were voluntary, detailed, and truthful.
    On Co-Accused Confessions: Under Section 33(2) of the Evidence Act, a co-accused’s confession cannot be the sole basis for a conviction, but the 3rd appellant's confession properly convicted the 4th appellant because it was corroborated by independent fingerprint evidence.
    Rationale:
    Exclusion of Investigation Time: Under Section 50(2)(a) of the CPA, time spent conveying a suspect to various crime locations for search and recovery (such as retrieving the radio call from a cesspit) is excluded from calculating the 4-hour restraint limit.
    Substantive Truth over Technicality: A free and voluntary confession is the best evidence because it comes from the accused. The statements contained minute details about the sequence of robbery events that only actual perpetrators could know.
    Role of Assessors: The judge holds absolute discretion on legal admissibility under Section 169(1) of the CPA. Excluding assessors on points of law during a trial-within-a-trial does not invalidate proceedings if the accused was granted a fair hearing.
    Requirement of Corroboration for Co-Accused: Section 33 allows taking a co-accused's confession into consideration against others. While Section 33(2) prohibits basing a conviction solely on a co-accused's confession, the 4th appellant's conviction was lawful because the confession was supported by matching fingerprint impressions recovered from the deceased's car.

    Facts
    The High Court of Tanzania at Tabora convicted Lutamla Basu (the appellant) of murder under Section 196 of the Penal Code and sentenced him to death by hanging. He was alleged to have murdered Maulid Ramadhan Mpalasinge on February 19, 2000. The sole evidence incriminating him was his own police confession (Exhibit P2), in which he claimed he was hired to commit the murder by Isanzule Mayeka (the deceased’s former son-in-law).
    Procedurally, the trial judge committed multiple irregularities: Discharged the assessors immediately after selection to conduct a "trial-within-a-trial" before the main trial commenced or any defence objection was raised. Told the assessors directly during summing up that the prosecution failed to prove the case against Mayeka, effectively signaling a verdict before receiving their independent opinions. Mayeka was acquitted, leaving the appellant's retracted/repudiated confession uncorroborated.
    Issue
    Whether the trial judge's directive to the assessors regarding the co-accused's innocence prejudiced the appellant or invalidated the trial.
    Whether the trial judge erred in the timing, initiation, and scope of conducting the trial-within-a-trial.
    Whether a retracted/repudiated confession alone—lacking independent corroborative evidence—is sufficient to sustain a murder conviction.
    Holding:
    Improper Summing Up: The trial judge erred by revealing his decision on the co-accused to the assessors. However, because the assessors independently returned "not guilty" opinions for both accused persons, no failure or miscarriage of justice was occasioned to the appellant.
    Irregular Trial-Within-a-Trial: Conducting a trial-within-a-trial before the main trial begins is procedural error. However, since the assessors ultimately had the opportunity to cross-examine the witness regarding the confession during the main trial, it did not prejudice the outcome.
    Evidential Weight of Confession: The appeal was allowed, the conviction quashed, and the death sentence set aside. The court refused to order a retrial due to the complete absence of corroborative evidence.
    Principles Developed:
    Procedure for Trial-Within-a-Trial: A trial-within-a-trial cannot precede the main trial. It must occur within the main trial only after the prosecution introduces a caution statement and the defence formally objects on grounds of involuntariness (under Section 27 of the Evidence Act).
    Assessor Independence: During summing up, trial judges must refrain from disclosing their own views or making remarks that could influence the assessors' independent opinions. The judge should only state their findings after receiving the assessors' opinions.
    Corroboration of Retracted Confessions: A conviction cannot safely stand on the sole basis of a retracted or repudiated confession without independent corroborative evidence, particularly where a co-accused implicated in the same statement is acquitted.

    Absence of Strict Time Limit: The Court held that no statutory provision prescribes a specific time limit within which an accused person must be taken before a Justice of the Peace to record an extra-judicial statement.
    Sufficiency of Uncorroborated Confession: The Court affirmed that a court can enter a conviction based on an uncorroborated repudiated or retracted confession, provided it is fully satisfied that the confession is truthful and was made voluntarily.
    Principles Developed:
    Flexibility in Timing for Extra-Judicial Statements: Although delay in taking an accused person to record an extra-judicial statement before a Magistrate or Justice of the Peace must be evaluated for voluntariness, delay alone does not automatically invalidate the statement in the absence of a fixed statutory deadline.
    Weight of Repudiated/Retracted Confessions: Corroboration of a retracted or repudiated confession is a rule of prudence and practice rather than an absolute rule of law. Where a court is entirely satisfied beyond reasonable doubt regarding the voluntariness and truthfulness of the confession, a conviction may lawfully stand without independent corroborating evidence.

    Conditions for Extra-Judicial Statements:

    1. Time and date of the arrest.
    2. The place of the arrest.
    3. The place the accused person was kept before being taken to the Justice of the Peace.
    4. It must show whether there was any threat or promise that persuaded the accused to give the statement.
    5. A caution that the statement can be used as evidence against the maker.

    Topic 2: Hearsay Evidence & Dying Declaration

    1. The General Rule of Direct Evidence & Hearsay

    General Rule (Section 67 TEA): Evidence must be direct. Oral evidence must, in all cases whatever, be direct (i.e., given by a witness who saw, heard, or perceived the fact himself).

    Exceptions: Where evidence is permitted or given by someone else, it constitutes an exception to the rule against hearsay evidence (for example, Admissions, Confessions, and Res Gestae).

    Section "67.–(1) Oral evidence shall, in all cases whatever, be direct; that is to say, if it refers to-
    (a) a fact which could be seen, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he saw it;
    (b) a fact which could be heard, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he heard it;
    (c) a fact which could be perceived by any other sense, or in any other manner, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he perceived it by that sense or in that manner;
    (d) an opinion or to the grounds on which that opinion is held, it must be the evidence of the person who holds that opinion or, who holds it on those grounds:
    Provided that, the opinions of experts expressed in any treatise commonly offered for sale, and the grounds on which such opinions are held, may be proved by the production of such treatise if the author is dead or cannot be found, or has become incapable of giving evidence, or cannot be called as a witness without an amount of delay or expense which the court regards as unreasonable.
    (2) Where oral evidence refers to the existence or condition of any material thing other than a document, the court may, if it thinks fit, require the production of such material thing for its inspection".
    According to section 3 of TEA, the term oral evidence means "all statements which the court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses being physically present at the time of making the statement or by use of other means of communication including teleconference or video conference, in relation to matters of fact under inquiry;"

    I. Hearsay Evidence & "Out of Court" Statements

    Definition: Hearsay is a piece of evidence provided by a third party to the matter, which was within the event that occurred, and not someone who heard it from another person (s. 62(1)).

    The General Rule: The rule is against hearsay generally because entertaining hearsay may lead to an unfair hearing or proceeding, resulting in the conviction of an innocent person or acquittal of a guilty person.

    Elements of an Out-of-Court Statement (s. 62(2))

    1. An out-of-court location (anything said outside the four walls of the courtroom).
    2. A statement (oral, written, or non-verbal such as head nods or thumbs up/down).
    3. Offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted.

    II. Statement

    An assertion that is made by a human being, it can be orally, written (such as a letter or email), or non-verbal (head nods, head shake,thumbs up and down).

    5. Exceptions to Hearsay: Dying Declarations (D.D.)

    Definition: A statement of a person who died is admissible as evidence if it relates to the cause of death of that person (s. 34 TEA).

    Key Conditions for Admissibility of Dying Declaration:

    • Cause of Death: The statement must relate to the cause of death of the declarant, not someone else's death.
    • Admissibility Scope: Under Common Law, D.D. is only admissible in cases of murder or manslaughter when the person was in actual expectation of death. In Tanzania, D.D. is admissible irrespective of whether the person was in expectation of death or not.
    • Completeness: If a statement constituting a D.D. is incomplete, the general rule is that it is inadmissible.
    • Verification: The court must exercise caution when relying on a D.D., examining the capacity, memory, and circumstances under which it was made.
    Case: R. v. Abedi bin Kasanga (1938) 5 E.A.C.A. 120
    Facts
    The appellant, Abedi bin Kasanga, was charged with and convicted of the murder of Sowedi. The prosecution relied on a dying declaration made by another individual, Bushiri. In his dying statement, Bushiri stated that he had been instigated by Abedi bin Kasanga to shoot a person named Ali. Bushiri explained that he had actually shot Sowedi by mistake, believing him to be Ali. The trial court admitted Bushiri's statement as evidence against Abedi bin Kasanga.
    Issue
    Whether a dying declaration made by a deceased person regarding the circumstances leading to another person's death (rather than their own) is admissible in evidence under the law governing dying declarations.
    Holding:
    The Eastern Africa Court of Appeal (EACA) held that Bushiri’s dying declaration was inadmissible. A dying declaration is only admissible to prove the cause of death or circumstances resulting in the death of the declarant himself, not to prove the cause of death of a third party.
    Principles Developed:
    Scope of Dying Declarations: To be relevant and admissible under statutory rules governing dying declarations (for example, Section 34(a) of the Tanzania Evidence Act), the statement must relate strictly to the cause of death of the person making the statement or to the circumstances of the transaction that resulted in their own death.
    Inadmissibility for Third-Party Deaths: A statement made by a dying person cannot be admitted as a dying declaration to establish liability or cause of death in the murder trial of a different victim.
    Case: Pius Jasunga s/o Akumu v. Reginam, (1954) 21 E.A.C.A. 331
    Facts
    The appellant was charged and convicted of murder. The core evidence relied upon by the trial court to convict him was an oral statement made by the deceased shortly before his death (a dying declaration), naming the appellant as his assailant. The defence challenged the conviction on appeal, arguing that it was unsafe to convict an accused person solely on the uncorroborated dying declaration of a deceased individual.
    Issue
    Whether a court can safely base a criminal conviction for murder solely on a dying declaration without independent corroboration.
    Holding:
    The Eastern Africa Court of Appeal (EACA) held that while a dying declaration is legally admissible, it is generally unsafe to base a conviction on an uncorroborated dying declaration. Because the maker of the statement cannot be cross-examined to test their veracity, memory, or capacity to observe the attacker, independent corroborating evidence is required.
    Principles Developed:
    Rule of Prudence on Dying Declarations: As a rule of practice and caution, a court should not uphold a conviction based on a dying declaration unless it is supported by satisfactory corroborative evidence linking the accused to the crime.
    Exception (Impossibility of Mistake): Corroboration may only be dispensed with where the surrounding circumstances establish beyond doubt that the deceased could not have been mistaken regarding the identity of their assailant (for example, where the parties were intimately known to each other, in broad daylight, and in close proximity immediately prior to the attack).

    Key Case Authorities on Dying Declaration:

    • R. v. Abed Kasanga (1938) – D.D. must strictly relate to the cause of death of the maker.
    • Pius Jasunga s/o Akumu v. Reginam (1954) 21 EACA – Court observed that a Dying Declaration taken by asking leading questions to the deceased is admissible, but the court must exercise caution.
    • R. v. Mitchell – The court held that the declaration must be the actual words of the deceased.

    1. Introduction to Documentary Evidence

    Types of Documents

    any writing, handwriting, typewriting, printing, photostat, photography, computer data and every recording upon any tangible thing, any form of communication or representation including in electronic form, by letters, figures, marks or symbols or more than one of these means, which may be used for the purpose of recording any matter provided that recording is reasonably permanent and readable;

    1. It communicates a message.
    2. It uses readable or identifiable letters, figures, or symbols.

    Case: R v. Daye [1908] 2 KB 333
    Facts
    In a criminal proceeding, a witness was served with a subpoena duces tecum requiring him to produce a sealed document (a bank deposit envelope containing an agreement or instrument) that had been deposited with him for safe custody. The witness refused to produce the document, contending that a sealed envelope or paper whose written contents were not fully visible or known did not constitute a "document" in the legal sense, and that he was bound by a duty of confidentiality to the depositors.
    Issue
    What constitutes a "document" for the purposes of evidence.
    Whether a written paper or legal instrument enclosed in a sealed container can be ordered for production under a subpoena duces tecum.
    Holding:
    The High Court of Justice (King’s Bench Division) held that the sealed paper was a document and ordered its production. The court affirmed that any written paper or record capable of providing evidence qualifies as a document, regardless of the material on which it is inscribed or whether it is enclosed in a sealed wrapper.
    Principles Developed:
    Legal Definition of a Document: Justice Darling defined a document as "Any written thing or print capable of being evidence is properly described as a document. It is immaterial on what material writing is inscribed." The court established that the material on which the writing is inscribed (whether paper, parchment, wood, or stone) is immaterial, provided it records information that can serve as evidence in court.
    Duty to Produce Sealed Documents: A person holding a document under a seal or confidentiality agreement cannot refuse a court order (subpoena duces tecum) to produce it, as the court's authority to inspect evidence overrides private custodian agreements.

    3. Proof of Documents and the Best Evidence Rule

    The Way of Proving Private vs. Public Documents

    General Rule: The contents of a document are proved by the original document itself (Primary Evidence).

    Exception to the General Rule: Under Section 92 of TEA (Certified copies of public documents), a custodian of a public document is given power to certify a copy, and a court will afford certified copies the status of the original.

    The Best Evidence Rule

    This is the doctrine that emphasizes the use of superior evidence, which is more reliable and less susceptible to error.

    Procedure in Court:

    1. As a matter of procedure, before a document is tendered in court, a witness must first identify the document, normally by giving facts as to why this is the document they consider relevant to the case/issue.
    2. The Court must determine the genuineness and ascertain that the document is an original version. If it is not an original document, the court will prohibit admissibility unless it falls under an exception provided by TEA.
    3. If there are any legal requirements attached to the document, the same must be shown to have been met.
      • Example 1: A Will must be attested by a person who has authority.
      • Example 2: A Lease Agreement required by law to be registered must be registered, or it cannot be used as an official document.
      • Example 3: A document must be stamped (like a Tax Document by TRA stamp); if un-stamped, requirements are not met and the document is inadmissible.

    By virtue of Section 76 of TEA, any document required by law to be attested must be proved by calling an attesting witness (Proof of execution of document required by law to be attested).

    4. Primary and Secondary Evidence

    Proof of Content of Documents

    According to Section 68 of TEA, the contents of documents may be proved either by Primary or Secondary Evidence.

    Primary Evidence (Section 64 of TEA)

    Primary Evidence refers to introducing the original document itself. A document may be primary for one purpose and secondary for another purpose (for example, an executory document to prove evidence which is not signed).

    Case: Lucas v. Williams & Sons [1892] 2 QB 113
    Facts
    The plaintiff, an artist, brought an action for infringement of copyright under the Fine Arts Copyright Act 1862, alleging that the defendants had unlawfully sold unauthorized photographic copies of his painting. At trial, the original painting was not produced in court. Instead, the plaintiff tendered a photograph of the original painting alongside the infringing copy sold by the defendants to prove copyright infringement. The defendants objected, arguing that under the "best evidence rule," the prosecution/plaintiff was required to produce the original painting itself to establish primary evidence.
    Issue
    Whether a photograph or secondary representation can be admitted to establish the existence and identity of the original work.
    WWhether a party claiming copyright infringement of a painting must produce the original painting in court (as primary evidence).
    Holding:
    The Court of Appeal (England and Wales) held that the production of the original painting was not legally required to prove infringement. The court ruled that primary evidence refers to evidence that the law requires to be produced first, but secondary evidence (such as a photograph or witness testimony) is admissible to identify the subject matter where a proper explanation or context is given.
    Principles Developed:
    Primary vs. Secondary Evidence Distinction: Lord Esher, M.R., clarified the foundational distinction regarding documentary evidence: "Primary evidence is the evidence which the law requires to be given first; secondary evidence may be given in the absence of that better evidence when a proper explanation of its absence is given." ​ Identification of Works of Art: The court established that the best evidence rule does not compel the physical production of heavy, valuable, or fixed physical objects (such as paintings or statues) in court. The identity and similarity of a visual work can be established through photographic copies or oral testimony without producing the original masterpiece.

    The law under Section 69 of TEA provides circumstances under which primary evidence is determined:

    • Section 69(2): Where a document is executed in several parts, each part is primary evidence of the document.
    • Section 69(3): Instances where a document is executed in counterpart, each counterpart is primary evidence against the party executing it.
    • Section 69(4): Where a number of documents are all made by one uniform process (for example, printing, lithography, photography), each is primary evidence of the contents of the rest; but where they are all copies of a common original, they are not primary evidence of the contents of the original.

    Secondary Evidence (Proof of Content)

    Secondary evidence is permitted where there are sufficient grounds. The law provides a list of secondary evidence under Section 71 / Exception under Section 73 of TEA:

    1. When the original is in the possession or power of:
      1. The opposite party;
      2. A person out of reach / not subject to court process; or
      3. A person legally bound to produce it, who does not produce it after notice.
    2. When the existence, condition, or contents of the original have been proved to be admitted in writing by the person against whom it is offered or by his representative in interest.
    3. When the original has been lost or destroyed. Before invoking this, the party must prove that the original evidence existed prior to destruction, and prove how the loss/destruction took place.
    4. If the original is not easily movable.
    5. If the original is a public document.
    6. If the document is one where the law permits certified copies of primary evidence.
    7. If the original consists of numerous accounts or other documents which cannot conveniently be examined in court.

    Note: As a matter of procedure, it is up to the parties to object to the admissibility of secondary evidence in proceedings, because the general rule allows it only under statutory exceptions.

    Topic 7: Evidence of Opinion & Character

    General Rule: It is the duty of the Court to form an opinion on certain facts. Forming an opinion is not a witness's duty. If a witness offers an opinion in evidence, that opinion generally is not supported or accepted in court. Opinions are formed after evaluating facts to arrive at conclusions.

    Rationale / Exception: Jurisprudence recognizes that the law is not all-knowledgeable across every specialized field. Sometimes the law itself requires assistance. Where special knowledge or particular skill is required, the law permits a person possessing those skills/knowledge to assist the court by giving opinion evidence to help the court arrive at its decision.

    A. Expert Opinion (Section 52 of TEA)

    An expert is a person who has adequate experience, knowledge, or skills that qualify him to give an opinion. He does not necessarily need an academic certificate; the court considers the extent and period of his practical experience.

    Under Section 52 of the Tanzania Evidence Act (TEA), the court allows the opinion of an expert when required to form an opinion upon a point of foreign law, science, art, identity of handwriting, or finger/other impressions.

    Evidential Weight of Expert Opinion: The court is not bound to accept an expert opinion unless the expert provides the grounds and reasoning for that opinion.

    Case: Haider Abdi v. Rex (1933) 1 T.L.R. (R) 519
    Facts
    In a criminal prosecution in the High Court of Tanganyika (now Tanzania), the prosecution relied heavily on the testimony of an expert witness to prove a critical point of fact against the accused, Haider Abdi. During trial, the expert merely stated his ultimate conclusion to the court without explaining the underlying technical data, observations, or scientific process he used to reach that conclusion. The trial court convicted the accused based on this summary opinion.
    Issue
    Whether an expert witness's opinion is admissible and sufficient to support a conviction if the expert merely states a final conclusion without disclosing the facts, reasons, or grounds upon which that opinion is based.
    Holding:
    The High Court held that the conviction could not stand on such evidence alone. An expert's bare conclusion, stripped of its supporting grounds and scientific rationale, carries minimal evidential value. The court emphasized that the ultimate judge of fact is the court, not the expert witness.
    Principles Developed:
    Requirement of Grounds for Expert Opinion: An expert witness must not merely state a bare conclusion; he must provide the court with the underlying facts, observations, and logical grounds that led to his opinion.
    Court as the Ultimate Evaluator: The primary purpose of expert evidence is to provide the court with specialized scientific or technical criteria so that the judge can independently form their own conclusion. A court cannot delegate its fact-finding duty by blindly accepting an unreasoned expert opinion.

    Section 53 read together with Section 55 of TEA: Facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if they support or are inconsistent with the opinions of experts.

    B. Proof of Handwriting (Section 54 of TEA)

    Handwriting may be proved in court through the following modes:

    1. By calling the writer himself to give evidence.
    2. By the evidence of a witness who saw the person write.
    3. By the evidence of an opinion of a person acquainted with the handwriting.
    4. By a handwriting expert.
    5. By the court comparing the document in question with another admitted signature/handwriting.
    6. By the court directing the person to write in court for comparison (Section 88 / Section 81 of TEA).

    C. Non-Expert Opinions / Opinion of Lay Persons

    • Section 55 of TEA: Opinion as to existence of a general custom or right; opinions of persons who would be likely to know are relevant. (Read together with Section 18(b) and Section 34(1)).
    • Section 56 of TEA: Opinion as to usages, tenets, and meaning of words used in particular districts or by particular classes.
    • Section 57 of TEA: Opinion on relationship between persons. When the court has to form an opinion as to the relationship of one person to another, the opinion expressed by conduct of a family member or person having special means of knowledge is relevant. Note: Opinion on relationship alone is not sufficient to prove a marriage or legal presumption without additional proof.
    • Section 58 of TEA: Whenever the opinion of any living person is relevant, the grounds on which his opinion is based are also relevant. Opinions not grounded on facts are inadmissible. A witness must give the grounds for his opinion (for example, from literature, practice, or observation).

    2. Evidence of Character

    Definition & Statutory Meaning (Section 62 of TEA)

    Character refers to the inner quality that distinguishes one person from another. Under Section 62 of TEA, "character" includes both Reputation and Disposition: but, except as provided in sections 59 and 61, evidence may be given only of general reputation and general disposition, and not of particular acts by which reputation or disposition were shown.

    • Reputation: What others say about a person (general community estimation).
    • Disposition: The inherent tendencies or inner qualities showing a person's true nature.

    Scope: Evidence may only be given of general reputation and general disposition, not of specific acts by which reputation or disposition has been shown.

    A. Character Evidence in Civil Cases

    General Rule (Section 59(1) of TEA): In civil cases, evidence of character is irrelevant to prove probability or improbability of conduct.

    Exceptions:

    1. Where character is itself a fact in issue (for example, Defamation suits).
    2. Section 59(2) of TEA: In civil cases, evidence of character is admissible if it affects the amount of damages (for example, torts involving negligence, breach of promise of marriage, or defamation).

    B. Character Evidence in Criminal Cases

    In criminal cases, the law distinguishes between Good Character and Bad Character:

    1. Good Character (Section 60 of TEA):
    In criminal proceedings, the fact that the person accused is of good character is relevant and admissible.

    2. Bad Character (Section 56 of TEA):
    General Rule: Evidence of bad character of an accused person is inadmissible.

    Reasons for Excluding Bad Character Evidence:

    • It creates high prejudice against the accused person that outweighs real evidence.
    • It opens a "Pandora’s box" of collateral issues, wasting court time.
    • Risk of wrongful conviction based on past conduct rather than present facts.

    Exceptions Where Bad Character Becomes Admissible (Section 61(1) of TEA):

    1. Where the accused gives evidence of his good character (he opens the door/Pandora's box, allowing the prosecution to rebut with bad character).
    2. Where bad character is itself a fact in issue.
    3. For sentencing purposes / mitigation and aggravation after conviction has been entered.
    Case: Makin v. Attorney-General for New South Wales [1894] AC 57
    Facts
    John and Sarah Makin (husband and wife) were tried in New South Wales for the murder of an infant, Horace Amber Murray. The child had been given to the Makins to foster in exchange for a small sum of money, but the child subsequently vanished. The child’s body was later found buried in the backyard of a house previously occupied by the couple. To prove that the death was not accidental, the prosecution adduced evidence showing that the bodies of several other infants who had similarly been taken in by the Makins for small payments—were found buried in the yards of other houses previously occupied by the couple. The Makins were convicted of murder and appealed, arguing that evidence of these other buried infants (similar fact evidence) was inadmissible and highly prejudicial.
    Issue
    Whether evidence showing that an accused person committed other similar offenses (or that other similar dead bodies were found on their past premises) is admissible in a criminal trial.
    Whether it must be excluded under the rule prohibiting evidence of bad character/past misconduct.
    Holding:
    The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council dismissed the appeal and upheld the conviction. The Council held that while evidence of past misconduct cannot be introduced merely to show that the accused is a person of bad character with a propensity to commit crimes, such evidence is admissible if it is logically relevant to an issue before the court such as proving design, intent, system, or rebutting a defense of accident or mistake.
    Principles Developed:
    The Rule Against Propensity Evidence: Evidence tending to show that the accused has committed other crimes or possesses a bad character is strictly inadmissible if its sole purpose is to show that the accused is a bad person likely to have committed the crime charged.
    The Similar Fact / Relevancy Exception: Evidence of other similar acts or occurrences is admissible if it is relevant to a specific issue in the case, such as: Rebutting a defense of accident, mistake, or innocent intention. Establishing a systematic pattern or plan of criminal conduct (system). Proving the mens rea or guilty knowledge of the accused.
    Case: Republic v. Katilimala [1977] LRT n. 38
    Facts
    In a criminal trial before the High Court of Tanzania, the prosecution sought to rely on evidence regarding the accused person's bad character and past criminal record. The prosecution attempted to introduce these prior bad acts to demonstrate a likelihood or probability that the accused committed the specific offense for which he was currently being tried.
    Issue
    Whether evidence of an accused person's bad character or previous criminal convictions is admissible to serve as primary proof of guilt or establish criminal liability during a trial.
    Holding:
    The High Court held that evidence showing the bad character or past convictions of an accused person cannot be used as primary proof to establish guilt during trial. Such evidence is strictly excluded from the prosecution’s main case to protect the accused from unfair prejudice and to ensure that the verdict rests entirely on reliable, facts-in-issue evidence.
    Principles Developed:
    Inadmissibility of Bad Character as Primary Proof: Evidence of an accused’s bad character or previous criminal record cannot be adduced to show that the accused is predisposed to committing crimes or is likely to have committed the offense charged.
    Protection Against Prejudicial Injustice: The rule shields the accused from trial by prejudice, ensuring that past misconduct does not sway the court's evaluation of the specific facts establishing the current offense. Bad character evidence remains barred unless the accused opens the door by presenting evidence of good character, or unless it falls under narrowly defined statutory exceptions (or is introduced for sentencing post-conviction).

    3. Facts Which Need Not Be Proved

    It is not always true that a party must prove everything in court. Facts which the court will not require evidence to prove can be grouped into 3 main categories:

    1. Matters of Judicial Notice (Sections 63 & 64 of TEA): Facts so common, notorious, or easily verifiable that the court accepts them without formal proof. Section 64(a)–(i) enumerates facts of which the court must take judicial notice (for example, statutes, territorial limits, official seals).
    2. Admitted Facts (Section 65 of TEA): Facts admitted by parties before or at the hearing need not be proved.
    3. Presumptions of Law: Facts presumed by operation of statutory law.

    Topic 8: Burden of Proof & Standard of Proof

    Burden of Proof & Standard of Proof

    Burden of proof refer to the obligation that the party to the proceeding law to prove their claim. As the matter of law there is no court or judgment will awarded without first conviced that the algetion rised before him or hewr are true or otherwise.

    A. Fundamentals of Evidence

    The Law of Evidence is founded on three cardinal principles:

    1. Relevance: Evidence must have a legal and logical connection to the facts in issue.
    2. Admissibility: A matter of law determining whether evidence is permitted under statutory rules.
    3. Weight of Evidence: The degree of probative persuasion that the court attaches to the evidence to satisfy the burden of proof.

    B. Types of Burden of Proof

    Burden of proof refers to the legal obligation of a party to establish a fact in issue. Expressed by the Latin maxim Onus Probandi.

    • 1. Evidential Burden of Proof (Passing the Shifting Onus):
      Refers to the obligation to adduce sufficient evidence on a particular issue to raise a triable fact. It can shift back and forth between parties during trial (Section 119 of TEA).
    • 2. Legal / Persuasive Burden of Proof:
      Refers to the ultimate obligation imposed on a party to establish his whole case to the required standard of proof to avoid judgment against him (Section 117 of TEA). The legal burden in criminal cases never shifts from the prosecution.
    • In civil cases, a person has discharge in the standard of required/Balance of probabilities.
    • In criminal case, a party (prosecution) should prove their case beyond reasonable doubt.

    C. Standard of Proof

    Refers to the degree or threshold of persuasion required to discharge the burden of proof (Section 3(2) of TEA).

    1. Standard in Civil Cases: Balance of Probabilities (Section 3(2) of TEA)
    The plaintiff must adduce evidence that carries more weight than the evidence of the defendant. If the evidence is evenly balanced, the party bearing the legal burden fails to discharge it.

    2. Standard in Criminal Cases: Beyond Reasonable Doubt (Section 3(2) of TEA)
    The prosecution must prove both actus reus and mens rea beyond reasonable doubt. The accused has no constitutional or legal duty to prove his innocence.

    Case: Hopt v. Utah 120 U.S. 430 (1887)
    Facts
    Frederick Hopt was indicted and tried in the Territory of Utah for murder in the first degree. Following several appeals and retrials, the case reached the U.S. Supreme Court on a writ of error. Among several procedural and evidentiary challenges raised by the defence, Hopt contested: The trial court's instructions to the jury defining "reasonable doubt". The admissibility of expert medical opinion from a physician regarding the direction from which a fatal blow was delivered. The impartiality of certain jurors who had formed impressions of the case through newspaper reports.
    Issue
    What constitutes a proper judicial instruction to the jury regarding the standard of proof "beyond reasonable doubt" in a criminal trial?
    Is the opinion of a medical expert admissible when determining physical facts observed during a post-mortem examination?
    Holding:
    The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed Hopt's conviction, holding that the trial judge's instruction on reasonable doubt was clear, adequate, and accurate. The Court also upheld the trial judge’s rulings on the admissibility of the physician's testimony and the selection of jurors.
    Principles Developed:
    The "Important Matters of Life" Test for Reasonable Doubt: Justice Field articulated a classic benchmark for reasonable doubt: If the evidence in a criminal action is of such a convincing character that jurors would unhesitatingly act upon it in the weightiest and most important affairs of their own lives, they may be said to have no reasonable doubt respecting the guilt of the accused.
    Definition of Reasonable Doubt: A reasonable doubt is not a mere speculative, imaginary, or possible doubt, but an actual, substantial doubt arising from a candid, impartial consideration of all the evidence in the case.
    Admissibility of Medical Opinions on Physical Injuries: A qualified physician who performs a post-mortem examination may offer an opinion as to the nature, angle, and direction of a fatal blow inflicted upon the deceased, as such opinions are based on specialized observation and scientific knowledge.
    Case: Francis Marende v. Republic [1988] TLR 26
    Facts
    The appellant, Francis Marende, was convicted in the magistrate's court of a criminal offense based primarily on circumstantial evidence and eyewitness testimony. He appealed to the High Court of Tanzania, challenging both his conviction and sentence. The core of his appeal rested on the argument that the prosecution had failed to establish his guilt to the required legal standard, and that the evidence presented against him was insufficient to exclude reasonable doubt.
    Issue
    Whether the trial court erred in convicting the appellant, and whether the prosecution discharged its legal burden of establishing guilt beyond reasonable doubt in accordance with Section 3(2) of the Tanzania Evidence Act.
    Holding:
    The High Court allowed the appeal and quashed the conviction. The court held that where a conviction is based on evidence that leaves open reasonable doubt or fails to conclusively link the accused to the crime, the legal burden remains unfulfilled, and the accused must be given the benefit of the doubt.
    Principles Developed:
    Standard of Proof Beyond Reasonable Doubt: The case reinforces the foundational principle in Tanzanian criminal jurisprudence that the prosecution bears the ultimate legal burden of proving every element of the offense (actus reus and mens rea) beyond a reasonable doubt.
    Benefit of the Doubt: If the court, after examining all adduced evidence, is left with a genuine and reasonable doubt regarding the accused's guilt, that doubt must strictly be resolved in favor of the accused person, resulting in an acquittal.
    Case: Joseph John Makune v. Republic [1986] TLR 44 (CA)
    Facts
    The appellant, Joseph John Makune, a bank official, was charged and convicted under the Economic and Organized Crime Control Act for occasioning pecuniary loss to a specified authority (the National Bank of Commerce). The charge arose after he authorized the encashment of two high-value cheques without verifying whether the drawer’s account at a distant branch had sufficient funds. During trial, issues arose regarding whether he had properly exercised managerial discretion and whether the burden rested on him to prove the existence of adequate funds at the relevant time. He appealed both his conviction and sentence to the Court of Appeal of Tanzania.
    Issue
    On whom does the burden of proof lie in criminal prosecutions, and what is the standard of proof required by law?
    Can an accused person be convicted on the basis of weaknesses in their defense, or must a conviction rest solely on the strength of the prosecution's case?
    Holding:
    The Court of Appeal of Tanzania emphasized the strict division of proof in criminal trials, holding that the trial court erred in shifting any legal burden onto the accused regarding elements of the offense. An accused person cannot be convicted due to gaps or weaknesses in their defense; conviction must rest entirely on the prosecution proving its case beyond reasonable doubt.
    Principles Developed:
    The Cardinal Principle on Burden of Proof: In criminal matters, the legal burden rests squarely on the prosecution to prove every element of its case; no duty is cast on the accused to establish their innocence.
    Limited Exceptions to the Rule: The court noted that exceptions where the burden shifts to the defense are extremely rare and strictly created by law such as when an accused raises the affirmative defense of insanity, which must be proved on a balance of probabilities.
    Standard of Proof & Strength of the Prosecution Case: The standard of proof is strictly beyond reasonable doubt. A court cannot rely on a weak defense to plug deficiencies in the prosecution’s evidence.

    Burden on the Accused Person (Section 121 of TEA):
    Where an accused person raises specific defenses (for example; insanity, alibi, self-defense, intoxication), the burden is on him to raise reasonable doubt or satisfy the court on a balance of probabilities regarding that specific defense.

    Intoxication: This defence suggests that there was no mens rea (guilty mind) at the time of committing the act. Section 14 of the Penal Code covers intoxication as a defence. Under these circumstances, the accused must prove the fact that they suffered from temporary insanity induced by intoxication. However, this in no way relieves the prosecution of its burden to prove the requisite intention of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt.

    Insanity (Section 13, Penal Code)

    General Rule: The law presumes that every person is of sound mind (i.e., aware of the nature and consequences of their actions). Therefore, the defense bears the evidentiary burden to produce evidence establishing insanity.

    Evidentiary Threshold: The evidence produced by the defence does not need to establish insanity beyond all reasonable doubt, but must be sufficient to challenge the prosecution’s case and raise a reasonable doubt as to the accused's mental state.

    Defence of Alibi

    Overview: Suggests that at the time the alleged crime took place, the accused was elsewhere and physically could not have committed the act.

    Burden of Proof (Section 117, Tanzania Evidence Act / TEA): Raising an alibi does not shift the ultimate burden of proof from the prosecution to the accused. The accused only needs to adduce sufficient evidence showing that the assertion might be true. The prosecution must then disprove the alibi beyond a reasonable doubt.

    Procedure & Statutory Provisions:

    • Procedure for raising an alibi: Section 200(4) of the Criminal Procedure Act (CPA).

      • 200 (4) Where an accused person intends to rely upon an alibi in his defence, he shall give to the court and the prosecution notice of his intention to rely on such defence before the hearing of the case.

    Strict Compliance with Arraignment Procedures: It is mandatory for trial records to explicitly reflect that the charge was read over and explained to the accused in a language they understand, followed by a recorded plea. Writing generic internal directives such as "Let the accused be reminded of his charge" does not create a presumption of compliance and vitiates trial proceedings.
    Non-Reading of Exhibits Exception: The general rule requiring exhibit contents to be read aloud in court to avoid expungement does not apply if a witness testifies comprehensively on the exhibit's contents and the accused is afforded a full opportunity to cross-examine.
    Evidentiary Weight and Burden of Alibi: While an accused person has no duty to prove their innocence, an accused relying on a defense of alibi must demonstrate it on a balance of probabilities. If no prior notice or particulars of an alibi are provided pursuant to Section 194 of the CPA, the court may exercise its discretion to give the alibi defense no weight.
    Standard for Ordering a Retrial: A retrial is justified when an original trial was defective or illegal (for example, defective arraignment) and ordering one serves the interests of justice without giving the prosecution an unfair opportunity to patch up an insufficiency of evidence.

    3. Presumptions under the Law of Evidence

    Definition & Nature

    A presumption is an inference—either affirmative or disaffirmative of the truth or falsity of a doubtful fact or proposition—drawn by a process of probable reasoning from something proven or granted.

    In the law of evidence, it represents the possibility of arriving at a conclusion based on probable reasoning. It constitutes a rule of law, statutory or judicial, by which proof of a basic fact gives rise to the existence of a presumed fact, allowing the court to reach a conclusion unless rebutted.

    Reasons for Legal Presumptions

    • Settling Disputes: Useful in resolving matters where direct evidence is insufficient.
    • Efficiency & Time-Saving: Eliminates the need to present formal proof for universally accepted or standard factual scenarios.
    • Administrative Convenience: Simplifies proof regarding the execution and genuineness of legal documents.
    • Exclusive Knowledge: Applies when knowledge of a fact lies exclusively within one party's domain.
    • Policy & Social Justice: Promotes public policy and legitimacy (e.g., presumption of legitimacy of children born in wedlock).

    Types of Presumptions (Tanzania Evidence Act - TEA)

    Under the TEA, presumptions are categorized into three main statutory forms:

    1. Presumption of Fact (May Presume - Section 4 TEA): Inferences naturally drawn from common human experience, observation, and usages. Courts have discretion to presume or require proof.
    2. Rebuttable Presumption of Law (Shall Presume - Section 5 TEA): Mandatory inferences that the law requires the court to make until disproved by evidence.
    3. Irrebuttable / Conclusive Presumption of Law (Conclusive Proof - Section 6 TEA): Inferences that the law does not allow to be challenged or disproved by opposing evidence.

    Key Provisions on Presumptions

    1. Presumptions of Fact / Rebuttable Presumptions (TEA):

    • Section 122 TEA: General presumption of facts (court may presume existence of certain facts).
    • Section 96 TEA: Presumption regarding books, maps, and charts.
    • Section 95 TEA: Presumption regarding certified copies of foreign judicial records.
    • Section 97 TEA: Presumption regarding telecommunication messages.
    • Section 99 TEA: Presumption regarding documents 20 (or 30) years old.
    • Sections 116–117 TEA: Presumption of continuous life and death.
    • Section 121 TEA: Presumption of legitimacy of a child born during marriage.
    • Section 93 TEA: Presumption regarding genuineness of certified copies.

    2. Presumptions under the Penal Code (PC):

    • Section 13 PC: Presumption of Sanity (every person is presumed sane).
    • Section 15(3) PC: Presumptions regarding sexual capacity/intent.
    • Section 9 PC: Presumption of Knowledge of Law (ignorance of the law is no excuse).
    • Section 15 PC: Presumption of incapacity of a child under ten years (doli incapax).

    3. Presumptions under the Law of Marriage Act (LMA):

    • Presumption of Marriage: Cohabitation of spouses (John Kilakue v. Iddi Siko).
    • Presumption of Death: Absence for 7 years without news (Salum Omar Hamadi v. Fatuma Kituu).

    4. Irrebuttable Presumptions of Law (Conclusive Proof):

    • Final judgments, orders, or decrees of courts after the expiration of the statutory appeal period.
    • Incapacity of a child under 7 (or 10 depending on jurisdiction) years to commit a criminal offence (Section 15 PC).

    Topic 9. General Principles of Witness Competency & Compellability

    Meaning of Competency & Compellability

    Competency: Refers to the legal capacity or qualification of a person to take the stand and give evidence in a court of law.

    Compellability: Refers to whether a competent witness can be lawfully forced or required by the court to give evidence under penalty of law (e.g., contempt of court under Section 114(1) of the Penal Code).

    General Rule: A person cannot give evidence in court until their competency is established. Competency is a condition precedent to giving sworn or affirmed testimony.

    Classification of Witnesses

    Witnesses generally fall into three statutory categories:

    1. Competent and Compellable: Persons who have the legal capacity to testify and can be lawfully compelled to take the stand.
    2. Competent but Not Compellable: Persons who possess legal capacity but cannot be forced to testify against their will or against specific parties (e.g., spouses in certain criminal proceedings).
    3. Incompetent Witnesses: Persons whom the court determines cannot understand the nature of an oath/affirmation or cannot give rational answers due to tender age, extreme old age, disease (of body or mind), or other mental incapacities.

    Test of Competency (Section 127(1), TEA)

    Under Section 127(1) of the Tanzania Evidence Act (TEA), all persons are competent to testify unless the court considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions put to them, or from giving rational answers to those questions, by reason of:

    • Tender age (child witness)
    • Extreme old age
    • Disease of body or mind (unsound mind/lunacy)
    • Any other cause of the same kind

    2. Competency in Special Cases

    Child Witnesses / Tender Age (Section 127(2)–(5), TEA)

    Voire Dire Examination: When a child of tender age is called as a witness, the court must conduct a preliminary examination (voire dire) to ascertain two fundamental issues:

    1. Whether the child understands the nature of an oath (possesses religious/moral understanding of telling the truth).
    2. If not, whether the child possesses sufficient intelligence to understand the duty of speaking the truth and can give rational answers.

    Unsworn Evidence of a Child (Section 127(2), TEA): If the child does not understand the nature of an oath but understands the duty to tell the truth, their unsworn evidence may be received. However, such evidence requires corroboration as a matter of law/prudence (Godfrey Wilson v. Republic).

    Persons of Unsound Mind & Lunatics (Section 127(5), TEA)

    A person of unsound mind or a lunatic is not incompetent unless their mental condition prevents them from understanding questions or giving rational answers. A lunatic may testify during a lucid interval when their mental capacity is restored (R. v. Hill (1951)).

    Dumb and Deaf Witnesses (Section 128, TEA)

    A witness who is unable to speak (dumb) or hear (deaf) may give evidence in any manner in which they can make it intelligible, such as by writing or by signs made in open court. Evidence so given is deemed to be oral evidence.

    Requirement for an Interpreter: Where signs are used, an expert or qualified interpreter must be sworn to interpret the signs correctly (Salum v. Republic (1951) E.A.C.A.).

    Husband and Wife (Spousal Competency & Privilege - Section 130, TEA)

    General Rule: Spouses are competent witnesses, but in criminal proceedings, a spouse is generally non-compellable to give evidence against the accused spouse, subject to statutory exceptions.

    Exceptions under Section 130(1), TEA: A spouse is competent and compellable for the prosecution in cases involving:

    • Offences against the person or property of the spouse or children.
    • Offences under Chapter XV of the Penal Code (sexual offences/offences against morality).
    • Offences involving bigamy or under the Law of Marriage Act.

    Duty of Court: Under Section 130(3) TEA, the court must warn a spouse called as a prosecution witness of their right to refuse to give evidence (Akechi v. Republic (1971)).

    Concubines / Cohabitants: The privilege does not apply to unmarried cohabitants or concubines; they are fully competent and compellable (R. v. Kibaya).

    Accomplice Evidence (Section 142, TEA)

    An accomplice is a competent witness against an accused person. A conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds upon the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice, though courts apply the rule of caution requiring corroboration (DPP v. Aliballah Nsyohe & Another).

    3. Privilege of Witnesses (Sections 133–141, TEA)

    Privileged Communications & Information

    Certain witnesses are protected or exempted from answering specific questions or disclosing particular communications in court:

    • Judges and Magistrates (Section 133 TEA): Cannot be compelled to answer questions regarding their judicial conduct or knowledge obtained in court except upon special order of a higher court.
    • Information as to Commission of Offences / Informers (Section 137 TEA): Police officers or public officers cannot be compelled to disclose the sources of information regarding the commission of an offence.
    • Professional Communications / Advocate-Client Privilege (Sections 134–136 TEA):
      • No advocate is permitted to disclose communications made by a client or advice given in the course of professional employment without the client's express consent.
      • Exceptions: Communications made in furtherance of an illegal purpose/crime, or facts observing fraud or crime committed since the commencement of employment (Butler v. Board of Trade (1971)).
    • Official Records / Public Interest Immunity (Section 132 TEA): Protection of unpublished official records relating to affairs of state where disclosure would injure public interest.

    4. Order and Stages of Examination of Witnesses

    Order of Production (Section 144 TEA & Section 145 TEA)

    The order in which witnesses are produced is regulated by the law and practice relating to civil and criminal procedure, and in the absence of such provision, by the discretion of the court.

    Court's Discretion on Admissibility (Section 145(1) & (2) TEA): The judge/magistrate decides the admissibility of evidence and may ask the party proposing to give evidence in what manner the alleged fact, if proved, would be relevant.

    The Three Stages of Examination (Section 146, TEA)

    1. Examination-in-Chief (Section 146(1) TEA):

      The examination of a witness by the party who calls them. The main purpose is to lay before the court all material facts within the witness's personal knowledge to support the party's case.

      General Rule on Leading Questions: Leading questions (questions suggesting the answer desired) must not be asked in examination-in-chief without the permission of the court (Section 149 TEA / Section 150 TEA), except on introductory/undisputed matters.

    2. Cross-Examination (Section 146(2) TEA):

      The examination of a witness by the adverse party.

      Purposes of Cross-Examination:

      • To test the accuracy, credibility, and general integrity of the witness.
      • To shake the witness's credit by injuring their character.
      • To elicit facts favorable to the cross-examining party's case.

      Leading Questions: Leading questions are permitted in cross-examination (Section 151 TEA).

    3. Re-Examination (Section 146(3) TEA):

      The examination of a witness, subsequent to cross-examination, by the party who called them.

      Scope: Re-examination must be directed strictly to the explanation of matters referred to in cross-examination. New matters cannot be introduced without leave of the court.

    5. Practical Rules & Advocacy Techniques for Examination

    Key guidelines for advocates during witness examination in trial advocacy:

    • Never ask a question without a clear purpose: Ensure every question aims to establish a relevant fact or challenge credibility.
    • Maintain control during Cross-Examination: Use short, concise leading questions rather than open-ended queries that allow the witness to explain away contradictions.
    • Modulate tone and demeanor: Adapt voice and pacing to suit the witness and the court environment.
    • Protect your witness during Re-Examination: Use re-examination selectively to clarify ambiguities created during cross-examination, not to re-ask examination-in-chief questions.
    • Improper & Harassing Questions (Sections 156–161 TEA): The court has statutory power to forbid questions that are indecent, scandalous, or intended to insult/annoy the witness.

    Topic 10. Credibility of Witnesses & Weight of Evidence

    Credibility & Weight of Evidence

    Weight of Evidence: Refers to the probative value or persuasive strength that the court attaches to the evidence presented by a witness.

    Credibility: Refers to the truthfulness, reliability, and trustworthiness of a witness. The court will not trust or rely on evidence without first assessing the credibility of the witness.

    Factors for Determining Credibility

    Courts evaluate witness credibility based on several key factors:

    • Witness's demeanor and conduct in court
    • Powers of observation, memory, and perception
    • Knowledge of the facts in issue
    • Presence of bias, interest, or partiality
    • Consistency and rationality of the testimony given

    Impeachment of Witness Credibility (Section 157, TEA)

    Impeaching credit means attacking or challenging the credibility of an adverse witness to show they are unworthy of belief. Under the Law of Evidence, the credit of a witness may be impeached in the following ways:

    1. By Cross-Examination: Testing accuracy, truthfulness, memory, and shaking credit by injuring character (Sections 150–153 TEA).
    2. By Independent Evidence (Section 157, TEA):
      • Calling witnesses who testify that they believe the witness to be unworthy of credit from their general reputation.
      • Proving that the witness has been bribed or has accepted the offer of a bribe.
      • Proving former statements inconsistent with any part of their evidence which is liable to be contradicted.
    3. By Former Inconsistent Statements (Section 147 / Section 154, TEA): Cross-examining a witness on previous statements made in writing or reduced to writing without such writing being shown to them initially.
    4. By Demeanor in Court: The judge or magistrate observes the witness's deportment, hesitation, or attitude in the witness box (Homburg [1916]).

    2. Hostile Witness & Court Powers

    Hostile Witness (Section 163 & Section 164, TEA)

    Definition: A hostile witness is one who displays a definite animus or unwillingness to tell the truth on behalf of the party calling them, or who unexpectedly gives evidence adverse to that party.

    Procedure: With the leave of the court, the party who called the witness may cross-examine their own witness and put leading questions or impeach their credit (Todd Lucas v. Republic).

    Power of the Court to Ask Questions (Sections 176 & 177, TEA)

    Under Section 176 of the TEA, the judge or magistrate may, in order to discover or obtain proper proof of relevant facts, ask any question they please, in any form, at any time, of any witness or of the parties.

    Under Section 177 of the TEA, assessors (where applicable) are permitted to put questions to witnesses through or with the permission of the court.

    Topic 11. Refreshing Memory & Production of Writings

    1. Refreshing Memory & Production of Writings

    Refreshing Memory (Sections 168–172, TEA)

    A witness may refresh their memory while under examination by referring to any writing made by themselves at the time of the transaction or so soon afterwards that the court considers it likely that the transaction was at that time fresh in their memory (Section 168 TEA).

    Key Requirements & Conditions:

    • The writing must have been made contemporaneously or soon after the event.
    • A witness may also refer to a writing made by another person if they read/examined it when the matter was fresh in their memory and knew it to be correct.
    • Expert Witnesses (Section 170 TEA): An expert witness may refresh their memory by reference to professional treatises, guidebooks, or technical tables.
    • Bookkeepers & Business Records (Section 171 TEA): A witness may testify to facts recorded in accounting books or records even if they have no independent recollection of the specific transaction.

    Right of Adverse Party to Inspect & Cross-Examine (Section 172, TEA)

    Any writing referred to by a witness to refresh their memory must be produced and shown to the adverse party if requested. The adverse party has the right to:

    1. Inspect the document/writing.
    2. Cross-examine the witness upon the writing to test accuracy and completeness.

    2. Production & Tendering of Documents (Exhibits)

    Production of Documents (Sections 173–175, TEA)

    A witness summoned to produce a document must bring it to court notwithstanding any objection to its production or admissibility. The court decides on the validity of any objection raised.

    Step-by-Step Procedure for Tendering Documents in Court

    To admit a document into evidence as a marked exhibit, advocates must follow a standard procedural foundation:

    1. Laying the Foundation: Ask the witness questions to show their relationship to the document (how it came into their possession/knowledge).
    2. Identification: Hand the document to the witness and v ask them to identify it, state its date, and confirm its contents/signatures.
    3. Show to Opposing Counsel: Pass the document to the adverse party/counsel for inspection or potential objection.
    4. Formally Tender: Apply to the court to tender the document as an exhibit (e.g., "My Lord, I pray to tender this document as Exhibit P1").
    5. Ruling on Objections: The court hears any objections from the opposing side regarding admissibility, relevance, or authenticity and rules accordingly.
    6. Marking as Exhibit: If admitted, the court marks the document with an Exhibit number/letter.
    7. Reading Content: The witness or counsel reads relevant portions into the court record.
