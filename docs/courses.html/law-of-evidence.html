Principles & Framework of Law of Evidence in Tanzania

Topic 1: Introduction to Law of Evidence in Tanzania

1. Fundamental Concepts of Evidence

Evidence presented in a court of law must be duly proved and legally admissible. For example, a documentary instrument generally requires appropriate certification or stamping where mandated by law to be accepted as evidence.

Statutory Provision: Section 3(1) of the Evidence Act provides the statutory interpretation and legal definition of "evidence" as denotes the means by which an alleged matter of fact, the truth of which if submitted to investigation, is proved or disproved; and without prejudice to the preceding generality, includes statements and admissions by accused persons;

To establish proof, the law provides procedural mechanisms to summon witnesses and adduce testimony, enabling the court to evaluate whether the evidence is sufficient to meet the required standard of proof.

Legal Evaluation & Correction:

Note on "Have evidence without evidence?": In evidentiary jurisprudence, facts may be established without formal physical or oral evidence through Judicial Notice (facts so notorious that proof is unnecessary) or formal Admissions made by parties prior to or during proceedings.

2. Classification and Types of Evidence

The principal classifications of evidence adduced before judicial tribunals include:

  • Oral Evidence: Evidence given viva voce (by word of mouth) by a competent witness speaking on matters within their personal knowledge. Under statutory exceptions, oral evidence may be adduced through written affidavits, or via interpreters/symbols where a witness cannot speak.
  • Documentary Evidence: Statements expressed or described upon any material substance by means of letters, figures, or marks, intended to be used for recording permanent communications.
  • Direct Evidence: Evidence that directly proves a fact in issue without requiring inferences or presumptions (for example, testimony of an eyewitness who directly perceived the event).
  • Circumstantial Evidence: Evidence of relevant facts from which the existence of a fact in issue can be logically inferred. It relies on a chain of connected facts to arrive at a legal conclusion.
  • Real Evidence: Physical objects or material items produced for the inspection of the court that directly relate to the issue (for example, weapons, clothing, tools).
  • Primary Evidence: The original document or object itself produced for inspection by the court, considered the highest quality of evidence.
  • Secondary Evidence: Evidence admissible in the absence of primary evidence (for example, certified copies, mechanical duplicates, or oral accounts of the contents of a document).
  • Electronic Evidence: Data generated, stored, or transmitted via electronic or digital devices (for example, computer logs, mobile records, digital media, digital signatures).
  • Opinion Evidence: Evidence provided by an expert or layperson regarding inferences drawn from facts, where the court requires specialized expertise or judgment.
  • Exculpatory Evidence: Evidence that tends to clear an accused individual of guilt or liability in criminal or civil proceedings.

3. Historical Development of the Law of Evidence

The law of evidence evolved from primitive mechanisms such as trials by ordeal, combat, or customary rituals toward structured adversarial systems. Historical legal traditions, including medieval practice and Islamic legal systems (which historically emphasized specific witness qualifications and categories like Hadd), shaped formal rules governing witness competency and corroboration.

System Models: Modern evidence systems operate primarily under the Adversarial system (common law) or the Inquisitorial system (civil law tradition).

4. Why Legal Practitioners Study the Law of Evidence

  1. To ascertain and establish the truth of facts in dispute before a judicial tribunal.
  2. To evaluate the admissibility, relevance, and probative weight of different forms of evidence.
  3. To formulate effective legal strategies and ensure proper judicial decision-making.
  4. To meet the standard of proof required to secure a conviction or civil judgment.
  5. To understand the statutory and case law framework governing evidentiary procedures in Tanzania.
  6. To appreciate the role of evidence in shaping legal outcomes and ensuring justice.

Topic 2: Relevancy and Admissibility of Facts

1. Foundations of Relevancy and Admissibility

Under Section 3(1) of the Law of Evidence Act [Cap. 6 R.E. 2023], a "fact" is not limited merely to tangible objects that can be seen or touched. It includes any mental condition or state of mind of which any person is conscious, such as feelings, emotions, or intentional actions demonstrated in court.

General Principle: Not all facts are admissible as evidence in court. Only facts that are legally relevant and admissible under the Act may be adduced as evidence. If a fact lacks relevancy or fails the legal test of admissibility, it cannot be received as evidence.

Determination of Relevancy [Section 3(1)]

"Fact in Issue" refers to any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature, or extent of any right, liability, or disability asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding necessarily follows.

"Relevancy": One fact is said to be relevant to another when the one is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of the Evidence Act relating to the relevancy of facts.

Legal Evaluation (Adversarial System & Judicial Discretion):

In an adversarial legal system, the parties initially determine which facts to present as relevant. However, the judge or magistrate retains ultimate judicial discretion to rule on whether a piece of evidence is relevant and legally admissible. When a witness's testimony contradicts previous statements, the court assesses credibility (impeachment of witness under Sections 154–157 TEA) before deciding the weight of the evidence.

2. Admissibility and General Rules of Relevancy

Admissibility is a question of law. Some facts may be logically relevant but legally inadmissible due to exclusionary rules (for example, hearsay or privileged communications). Conversely, facts must first be relevant to be admitted.

General Rule of Relevancy (Section 7 TEA)

  1. Evidence may be given in any suit or proceeding of the existence or non-existence of every fact in issue and of such other facts as are declared to be relevant by the Act, and of no others.
  2. Parties can argue the existence or non-existence of facts in issue or relevant facts.
  3. Evidence adduced must be strictly confined to the relationship with the fact in issue or facts declared relevant.
  4. Only evidence recognized and permitted by law is admissible.

3. Facts Forming Part of the Same Transaction (Res Gestae - Section 8 TEA)

Facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the **same transaction** are relevant, whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places.

  • Contemporaneity Test: Spontaneous statements or acts must be substantially contemporaneous with the fact in issue to exclude the possibility of fabrication or afterthought.
  • Hearsay Exception: Statements forming part of the res gestae are admissible as exceptions to the rule against hearsay, provided they are uttered while the act is occurring or immediately thereafter.
Case of: Republic v. Bedingfield (1879) 14 Cox CC 341
Facts:
The accused Henry Bedingfield had a prior relationship with the deceased woman. They were having some disputes in their relationship. Bedingfield allegedly harboured resentment towards the woman due to her refusal to fulfil a proposal made by the defendant and her desire to end their relationship. This led him to issue violent threats, including threats to kill her. The victim ran a laundry business and had two assistants helping her. The night before the incident, the deceased informed local policemen about the threats and requested them to keep watch over her house. Early the next morning, Bedingfield visited her home which was unusual for him. They spent some time together and after a while, he left for a store. The woman’s two assistants came and saw her lying on the floor senseless. The defendant returned to the house and entered her room while her assistants were in the yard. A few moments later, the victim came out of her house screaming, holding her throat, which was bleeding from a deep cut. She reportedly told her assistants, “Oh, aunt, see what Bedingfield has done to me!” Within a few moments, she succumbed to her injuries on the spot.
Issues:
1. Is the statement made by the deceased before her death admissible as evidence?
2. Does the statement fall under the exception of “Res Gestae”?
Arguments:
1. Though the prosecution did not bring the dying statement directly, they initially submitted it as evidence in the court. They argued that, according to the witnesses, the victim was so frightened when she was giving the statement. Therefore, the prosecution tried to impose the doctrine of res gestae against the defendant.
2. A question was raised by the defence lawyer that, the incident could have been a suicide rather than murder. But according to the medical experts, the wound of her throat was extensive, measuring three to four inches and had severed the trachea and the jugular vein. Consequent they ruled out suicide as a possibility.
1. The defendant argued that “res gestae” requires that the statement be part of the act itself, not something said afterwards. Res gestae requires spontaneity and an immediate connection to the crime, without any opportunity for reflection or fabrication. Since the victim made the statement after leaving the crime scene, it was not part of the continuous transaction of the event.
2. The prosecution did not reintroduce the victim’s statement as a dying declaration after medical testimony was given. The defence highlighted that, for a dying declaration to be admissible, the declarant must have a full understanding of their imminent death. Since there was no proof that the victim believed she was dying when making the statement, it failed to meet the legal criteria for admissibility.
Decisions:
Lord Cockburn CJ ruled that the statement was inadmissible, as it was made after the attack had ended. The Court also noted that the statement was not admissible as a dying declaration because the deceased did not express a belief that she was about to die. Moreover, there was no evidence that she had lost all hopes of recovery from the injury when making the statement. Besides, he stated that it could not be considered part of the res gestae since it was not made during the incident but afterwards, giving room for possible fabrication. Despite this, other evidence strongly pointed to the defendant’s guilt. The nature of the victim’s injuries and the position of the razor suggested an intentional attack rather than an accident or self-inflicted harm. Cockburn CJ emphasized to the jury that it was the deceased woman, not the accused, who ran out, seemingly to raise an alarm or make a complaint. Based on these factors, the Court found Bedingfield guilty of murder, and he was sentenced to death.
Relevant Principle :
Doctrine of Res Gestae : When something is so closely connected with the main fact that the two can be considered parts of the same transaction, it is called res gestae and is admissible as evidence. Res gestae is an exception to the rule against hearsay.
Case of: Ramadhani Ismail v. The Crown (1945) 7 Z.L.R. 36
A young child, having been raped, went crying straight to her home (only 2 or 3 houses away), had a conversation with her parents, and then led her father to the house where she had been assaulted. On arrival at the accused's house, she said 'That is the Bwana'. It was held that this utterance COULD NOT be considered part of the res gestae when it comes to proving the truth of the matter.
Premji Kurji v. Republic (1940) 7 E.A.C.A. 58
The appellant appealed from a conviction of murder. The deceased had been killed with a dagger. Evidence was admitted that the deceased's brother was assaulted with a dagger and uttered threats against the deceased (words spoken by appellant: 'I have finished you and I am going to show your brother'). The court held that the evidence of the attack on the deceased's brother was admissible in the circumstances as part of the res gestae.

5. Fact showing motive,intention, preparation and subsequent conduct (Section 10 TEA)

Under Section 10 of the Evidence Act, any fact is relevant which shows or constitutes a motive or preparation for any fact in issue or relevant fact.

  • Motive vs. Intention: Intention relates to the immediate purpose or act desired, whereas motive refers to the ultimate ulterior reason or emotion inducing a person to act.
  • Preparation: Acts or measures undertaken prior to committing an offense (for example, buying a weapon, purchasing poison) are relevant if connected to the fact in issue.
  • Subsequent Conduct: Conduct of any party or accused person following an offense such as absconding, fabricating false evidence, or destroying clues is admissible as relevant conduct under Section 10(2).
Rex v. Premji Kurji, Criminal Appeal No. 55 of 1940 (Court of Appeal for Eastern Africa, on appeal from H.B.M. High Court of Zanzibar).
Facts:
​The Appellant (Premji Kurji) was convicted of murder after killing the deceased with a dagger. ​ Immediately prior to the killing, the appellant assaulted the deceased's brother (Jamnadas) with a dagger in his nearby shop and uttered threats directed at the deceased, stating: "I have finished you and am going to show your brother" (or "I am going to finish your brother"). ​ Immediately following this attack, the appellant was seen standing over the deceased in the nearby godown with a dagger in hand.
Issues:
1. Whether evidence of the prior assault on the brother was inadmissible as evidence of a separate criminal act, or admissible as part of the continuous transaction (res gestae).
2. Whether the possession and immediate prior use of the dagger constituted relevant evidence of opportunity and preparation/conduct.
Court's Holding & Application to Section 10 of Evidence Act
The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal, holding that the contested evidence was fully admissible.
Conduct and Preparation: Under the statutory equivalent of Section 10 of the Law of Evidence Act, evidence showing an accused person’s previous or subsequent conduct, as well as facts showing preparation or opportunity, is relevant and admissible.
Possession & Immediate Prior Use: The Court specifically noted that the fact that the accused had a dagger and used it immediately before the alleged killing was admissible as strong evidence of opportunity and continuous conduct.
The Entire Transaction: Where two acts committed by an accused are so interwoven as to form part of the same transaction (res gestae), the prosecution is entitled to present the full sequence of events. Evidence cannot be excluded merely because it incidentally reveals the commission of another offence.

Fact that show the conduct of any party, a conduct deemed to be relevant must have reference to the suit or proceeding pattern or to the fact in issue. This fact many involve the following:-
a. preparation and previous attempt to commit the offence.
b. Publicating false evidence.
c. Runing away of an accused immediate after commit an offence or abscondment
Subsection (3)Elaborate in the situation where the conduct of any person could be relevant in particular, this provision declares statements to be relevant provided those statements could have affected the conduct.
Subsection (4) Definition: Conduct does not include statements, except where such statements accompany and explain acts other than statements.
Types of facts admissible under conduct include:
(a) Conduct of an Accused Person: The conduct of an accused person either before or after the commission of an offense, which either influenced the crime or was influenced by a crime.
(b) Accompanying Statements: Statements made by an accused person or a company which explain or accompany such conduct.

NOTE BELOW:
When Section 10 is used to declare facts relevant, it must be shown that there is a connecting factor to the fact in issue and relevant facts.

5. Facts Necessary to Explain or Introduce Relevant Facts (Section 11 TEA)

Facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact are admissible in so far as they are necessary for that purpose. Section 11 covers:

  1. Facts necessary to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  2. Facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  3. Facts establishing the identity of any person or thing whose identity is relevant (for example, identification parades).
  4. Facts establishing the time or place at which any fact in issue or relevant fact occurred.
  5. Facts showing the relation of parties by whom any such fact was transacted.

Section 11 of the Evidence Act declares relevant facts that are intended either to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact (such as names, dates, and places, which may be admissible).

  • Introductory or Explanatory Facts: Names, descriptions, places, and times are admissible under sub-section (1).
  • Supporting or Rebutting Inferences: It admits facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  • Identity of Person or Object: Identity may be established using identification parades, DNA, or direct witness testimony.
  • Fixing Time and Place: Facts which fix the exact time and place when or where an event happened.
  • Relationship of Parties: Facts showing the relationship of parties (e.g., cohabitation, employment, agency).
Key Legal Issue: What happens when there is a wrong identification of the accused in an identification parade? (Affects weight and admissibility; requires strict adherence to identification procedures).

Procedure & Rules Requirement for Identification Parade in Tanzania

Identification parades in Tanzania are governed by police procedural guidelines under P.G.O. 232 (Police General Orders) read together with Form 3 (P.F. 186):

  1. The officer shall register the number of persons attending the parade (minimum 8 persons of similar age, height, and general appearance).
  2. He/she will register the number of witnesses under Form 3 / P.F. 186.
  3. The officer-in-charge of the case shall take no part in conducting the parade.
  4. The officer conducting the parade shall inform the suspect of his right to put on parade identification options (e.g., choice of position or presence of advocate/friend).
  5. The place of the parade must have good light.
  6. The suspect may put on the clothing he was wearing when the offense was committed during the parade.
  7. The officer conducting the parade will finally check entries in the identification parade register and sign in the space provided.
Omary Hussein @ Ludanga & Another v. Republic (Criminal Appeal No. 547 of 2017) [2021] TZCA 543
Facts:
On March 16, 2013, Sister Mary Shobana (the victim) and her driver, Erick Justine Kimanguka (PW7), were accosted by two armed assailants outside the gate of Notredame School in Njiro, Arusha. The assailants shot the victim in the leg, ordered PW7 to lay face-down on the steering wheel, and stole TZS 30,000,000/= before escaping on a motorcycle. Following an informant's tip, the appellants were arrested at a hotel. PW7 identified the 1st appellant during an identification parade. Cautioned statements were recorded where the appellants allegedly admitted involvement, though they later disowned them during trial claiming torture. The Resident Magistrates' Court convicted both appellants of armed robbery under section 287A of the Penal Code and sentenced them to 30 years imprisonment, a conviction upheld by the High Court.
Issues:
1. Whether the 1st appellant was properly identified by PW7 at the scene and during the identification parade.
2. Whether the appellants' cautioned statements and the identification parade register were properly admitted into evidence.
3. Whether the prosecution's failure to call crucial witnesses (the ballistic expert and the victim) entitled the court to draw an adverse inference.
4. Whether the offence of armed robbery was proved beyond reasonable doubt.
Court's Holding:
Identification Vitiated: The 1st appellant was not properly identified because PW7 failed to give a detailed physical description of the suspect to the police prior to participating in the identification parade. Exhibits Expunged: Although the identification parade register (Exhibit P6) and cautioned statements (Exhibits P1 & P5) were admitted, they were never read out in open court. This failure constituted a fatal, incurable procedural irregularity under section 388 of the Criminal Procedure Act (CPA), rendering them of no evidential value. Adverse Inference Drawn: The prosecution failed without sufficient reason to call the ballistic expert (to link the 2nd appellant's pistol to the crime scene) and the victim, Sister Mary Shobana. Under section 143 of the Evidence Act, the Court drew an adverse inference that their evidence would have been unfavorable to the prosecution. Verdict: With the identification evidence vitiated, key exhibits expunged, and adverse inferences drawn, no evidence remained to sustain the conviction. The Court allowed the appeal, quashed the convictions, set aside the 30-year sentences, and ordered the immediate release of both appellants. Precedents Applied
Case Citation Key Legal Principle / Holding
Rex v. Mohamed bin Allui (1942) & Bushiri Amiri v. Republic [1992] An identifying witness must provide a detailed description of the suspect's physical appearance and clothing prior to conducting an identification parade.
Emilian Aidan Fungo @ Alex & Another v. Republic (2009) Reaffirmed that for identification parade evidence to have probative value, prior description of the suspect is mandatory.
Robinson Mwanjisi & 3 Others v. Republic [2003] Once a document is cleared and admitted into evidence, it must be read out in court; failure to read it over renders it prejudicial and invalidates its evidentiary value.
Aziz Abdalla v. Republic [1991] Where the prosecution fails to call a material witness who is within reach without giving a sufficient reason, the court is entitled to draw an adverse inference against the prosecution.

6. Conspiracy & Common Intention (Section 12 TEA)

Section 12 TEA: Statements or actions referring to common intention. Anything said, done, or written by a conspirator in reference to their common design is a relevant fact against each of the persons believed to be so conspiring.

Principle of Agency: This section builds upon the agency principle—treating every individual who is involved in a conspiracy as an agent of another co-conspirator.

Two Essential Conditions Required to Apply Section 12:
  1. There must be prima facie / sufficient evidence of an agreement between those parties (conspiracy).
  2. There was a common intention by the parties to execute their plan at the time the act or statement was made.
Case Citation Key Legal Principle / Holding
Hashim Abdalla @ Simba & Omary Hussein @ Lugendo v. Republic Admissibility of co-conspirator acts and statements under common intention.
Stanislaus alias Kayambo s/o Kitambo v. R (1959) Applicability of statements of conspirators in relation to Section 11/12 TEA.
Leonard Mathias Mahani & Another v. Republic Where two or more persons conspire to commit an offense, anything said or done with reference to common desire is relevant.
R. v. Gokaldas Kanji Karia & Alibhai Mamanji (1949) 16 E.A.C.A. 116 Convicted for conspiracy to export diamond from Tanganyika. Court held agreement to conspire may be deduced from any act raising the presumption.
John Moody Lawrence Brown & Another v. R (1957) Proof of common intention and conspiracy through overt acts.

3. Defense of Alibi & Inconsistent Facts (Section 13 TEA)

Section 13 TEA: Facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact, or if by themselves or in connection with other facts they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable.

Definition of Alibi: A form of defense whereby the accused attempts to prove that he/she was elsewhere when the crime in question was committed.

Procedural Requirements for Alibi Defense in Tanzania

  • Notice of Alibi: Governed by Section 194(4) of the Criminal Procedure Act [Cap. 20 R.E. 2019]. The accused must give notice of intention to rely on an alibi during preliminary hearing or before prosecution closes its case.
  • Failure to Plead Early: Failure to give timely notice allows court to draw adverse inference or disallow late alibi evidence without leave.
  • Examples of Supporting Evidence: Eyewitness testimony, time-stamped pictures/video footage placing the accused at another location, bank/ATM receipts, GPS log data.
Relevant Authorities on Alibi & Appellate Review:
  • Tanginya Kajima v. R: Defense of alibi and standard of proof.
  • Raja v. Kotorra (1965) EA 362: Duties regarding alibi setup.
  • Mbuyi v. Dyer / Mshanga v. Tuani Diwani: Evidential burden regarding physical impossibility of presence.
  • Petter Mtingo & 4 Others v. R: Court's duty to evaluate alibi defense alongside prosecution evidence.
  • Peter v. Sunday Post Ltd (1958) EA 424: Appellate court entitlement to reassess evidence where lower court misapprehended substance or quality of evidence.

4. Damages and Customary Rights (Sections 14 & 15 TEA)

Facts Relevant in Suits for Damages (Section 14 TEA)

In suits in which damages are claimed, any fact which will enable the court to determine the amount of damages which ought to be awarded is relevant (whether to mitigate or aggravate damages).

Facts Affecting Existence of Right or Custom (Section 15 TEA)

Where the existence of any right or custom is in question, the following are relevant:

  • Any transaction by which the right or custom was created, claimed, modified, recognized, asserted, or denied.
  • Particular instances in which the right or custom was claimed, recognized, exercised, or disputed.
7 Requirements for Custom to be Valid in Law:
  1. Ancient: Must have existed from time immemorial / long standing.
  2. Continuous & Uniform: Exercised without interruption.
  3. Reasonable: Not contrary to natural justice or good conscience.
  4. Certain: Clear and definite in its scope and application.
  5. Compulsory: Obligatory and not optional.
  6. Peaceable: Enjoyed peaceably without force.
  7. Not Immoral / Not illegal: Not against statutory law or public policy.

5. Facts Showing State of Mind / Body & System (Sections 16 & 17 TEA)

State of Mind or Feeling (Section 16 TEA)

Facts showing the existence of any state of mind (such as intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, rashness, ill-will, or good-will) or state of body/bodily feeling are relevant when the existence of such state of mind or body is in issue.

John Makindi v. Republic: Evidence of similar transactions/previous conduct is admissible to show knowledge, intention, or state of mind, but cannot be used as sole proof of guilt for the specific charged offense.

System / Similar Occurrences (Section 17 TEA)

When there is a question whether an act was accidental or intentional, or done with particular knowledge, the fact that such act formed part of a series of similar occurrences is relevant.

  • Must be read together with Section 16 (state of mind).
  • Requires a specific line of defense alleging lack of intention, accident, or mistake.
  • The similar acts must be closely related in nature and time to show a systematic course of conduct.

6. Course of Business & Judicial Presumptions (Sections 18, 122 & 34B TEA)

Section 18 TEA: When there is a question whether a particular act was done, the existence of any course of business, according to which it naturally would have been done, is a relevant fact.

Section 122 TEA: The Court may presume the existence of any fact which it thinks likely to have happened, regard being had to the common course of natural events, human conduct, and public/private business in relation to the facts of the particular case.

Section 34B TEA: Written statements in criminal proceedings (Proof by written statement under Section 34B of Evidence Act / Section 289 CPA).

Topic 3: Burden of Proof & Onus of Proof

The Burden of Proof refers to the legal obligation of a party to prove an allegation or charge in court.

1. Standard of Proof (Kiwango cha Ushahidi)

  • In Criminal Cases: Proof beyond reasonable doubt (Kuthibitisha bila kuacha shaka yoyote inayojenga msingi).
  • In Civil Cases: Proof on a balance of probabilities (Kiwango cha uzito wa ushahidi unaoelekea ukweli).
