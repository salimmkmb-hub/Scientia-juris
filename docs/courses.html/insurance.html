INSURANCE LAW Sheria ya Bima - Tanzania Mainland

Principles & Regulatory Framework of Insurance Law

Topic 1: Introduction to Insurance

General Definition

Insurance is a contract in which one party (the insurer) agrees to compensate or indemnify another party (the insured) for specific losses or damages to property or lives in exchange for consideration, usually known as a premium.

Modern Insurance Definition

Modern insurance refers to a contract in which the insurer promises to indemnify the insured against loss or damage based on a promise paid for by consideration called a premium. The burden of loss is transferred to the insurance company, which pools money from many policyholders to manage financial liabilities arising from unexpected life events.

Risk Management

Insurance is fundamentally about risk management. Risk involves unforeseen or unexpected events in life, including:

  • Accidents
  • Death
  • Floods and Earthquakes
  • Fires
  • Professional negligence (Errors & Omissions for architects, financial advisors, etc.)

Purpose of Insurance

The primary purpose of insurance is to mitigate or reduce the financial burden of unexpected risks that an individual or entity cannot easily foresee or bear alone. As articulated in Prudential Insurance Co. v. Commissioners of Inland Revenue, an insurance contract deals with uncertainty and events that are not bound to happen at a known time.

Historical Background of Insurance

1. Insurance in Pre-Capitalist & Primitive Societies

In early human societies, modern legal definitions of institutional insurance did not apply because there were no formal insurance companies. Security and risk management operated through community solidarity.

A. Mutual Assistance & Kinship (Domestic Economy)

  • Nature of Economy: Based on non-industrial, domestic peasant economies where people lived together to pool labor for survival.
  • Principle of Mutual Assistance: Based on the social rule that "the loss of one is the loss of all". Members of a family, clan, or kinship group pooled their money, goods, and labor to secure against unexpected life events.
  • Role of the Head of the Family/Clan: The clan or family head oversaw labor activities and managed social risks such as funerals, bride prices (marriage), widow inheritance, and agricultural loss.

2. Early Global Foundations of Maritime & Trade Insurance

As society transitioned toward merchant capitalism, specialized division of labor and long-distance maritime trade exposed merchants to unique risks.

A. Early Mechanisms

  • Greeks and Romans: Employed bottomry and maritime loans (trajectitia pecunia) where higher interest incorporated a premium for the risk of sea loss.
  • Medieval Italy & Europe:
    • The earliest known written insurance contract (polizza) emerged in Genoa dating back to 1347.
    • Insurance courts were established in Bruges by 1310.
    • Sponsors/capitalists funded sea voyages (for example, Vasco da Gama) in exchange for returns, leading to early forms of underwriting.

B. Development of English Common Law Insurance

  • 1547: First recorded English marine policy.
  • 1688: Edward Lloyd’s Coffee House was established in London, becoming the primary venue for marine underwriters and giving rise to Lloyd's of London.
  • 18th Century (Lord Mansfield's Contributions): Chief Justice Lord Mansfield systematized English commercial and insurance law. He established core legal doctrines including:
    • The principle of Utmost Good Faith (Uberrima Fides).
    • The requirement of an Insurable Interest to prevent gambling on policies.
    • Strict compliance with warranties and non-misrepresentation.
    • The principle of Indemnity (recovering only the actual loss sustained).

3. Development of Modern Insurance in Colonial & Post-Colonial Tanzania

The evolution of insurance in Tanzania reflects the shift through colonial governance, state socialism, economic liberalization, and modern regulation.

A. Pre-Colonial and Early Colonial Era

  • Initial Colonial Legal Framework: Through the Tanganyika Order in Council (1920) and the Judicature and Application of Laws Ordinance (JALA, section. 17), British common law principles and received laws were introduced into Tanganyika.
  • Absence of Local Acts: During early colonialism, no local Insurance Act existed because commercial insurance activities were underdeveloped. Foreign British companies insured local colonial activities (like plantations) directly from their home states.

B. Late Colonial and Independence Reforms (1945–1966)

  • 1945: The Insurance Ordinance of 1945 and the Motor Vehicles Insurance Ordinance of 1945 were enacted to provide a comprehensive legal framework for insurance in Tanganyika.
  • 1961–1964: Following independence in 1961, the National Insurance Corporation (NIC) was formed in 1963. The Motor Vehicle Insurance Act was subsequently passed in 1964.
  • During this period, Tanzania operated a market-oriented economic system.

C. The Ujamaa Socialism Era (1967–1980s)

  • Arusha Declaration (1967): Tanzania adopted the Ujamaa ideology (socialism and self-reliance).
  • State Monopoly: The Insurance (Vesting of Interests and Regulation) Act of 1967 nationalized the sector. Insurance became a state-owned monopoly under NIC, resulting in an environment with no market competition.

D. Economic Liberalization & Modern Era (1990s–Present)

  • 1990s Liberalization: Pressure from international financial institutions (IMF, World Bank) and Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs) led Tanzania to transition back to a free-market economy.
  • Insurance Act of 1996: Re-opened the insurance market to private domestic and foreign companies, allowing public and private insurance firms to operate alongside one another.
  • Insurance Act of 2009: Repealed prior legislation to establish a comprehensive modern legal framework under the Tanzania Insurance Regulatory Authority (TIRA).
  • Subsequent Regulations & Special Frameworks:
    • Micro-insurance Regulations (2013).
    • Insurance Ombudsman Regulations (2013).
    • Bancassurance Regulations.
    • Introduction of alternative insurance frameworks such as Takaful Insurance (Islamic insurance complying with Sharia law).

Topic 2: Key Players

Key Players in an Insurance Transaction: Roles & Legal Functions

An insurance relationship involves four distinct legal entities. Defining their legal boundaries is essential for assigning liability during policy formation and claim disputes.

1. The Insurer (Underwriter)

Legal Definition: An individual or corporate entity carrying on insurance business, licensed under the Insurance Act, 2009 to take on risk in exchange for a premium.

  • Primary Role & Functions: Assesses proposed risks, calculates premiums using actuarial science, issues insurance policies, establishes statutory solvency reserves, and indemnifies the insured upon the occurrence of an insured risk.
  • Legal Duties: Owes a continuous duty of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) to the proposer, performs statutory disclosure obligations, and promptly settles valid claims pursuant to TIRA Claim Management Guidelines.

2. The Insured (Policyholder)

Legal Definition: The natural or legal person who enters into an insurance contract with the insurer, transfers an identifiable risk, and pays (or promises to pay) the consideration (premium).

  • Primary Role & Functions: Discloses all material facts during proposal, maintains the insured subject matter in accordance with policy conditions, mitigates loss during an event, pays premiums on time, and submits valid claim notifications upon suffering a loss.
  • Core Requirement: Must possess a valid Insurable Interest in the subject matter at the relevant legal time (at contract inception for life assurance; at the time of loss for property/indemnity insurance). Without insurable interest, the contract is void under the Gaming Act of 1845 and common law principles (Lucena v. Craufurd (1806); Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co. Ltd [1925] AC 619).

3. The Insurance Agent

Legal Definition: Defined under Section 3 of the Insurance Act, 2009 as a person who solicits applications for insurance, collects premiums, and acts in accordance with an express or implied agency agreement for a specific registered insurer, with the legal capacity to bind that insurer in the issue of cover.

  • Legal Status: Represents the Insurer. Under general principles of agency law (governed by the Law of Contract Act, Cap 345), the acts, representations, and receipts of an agent within their express, implied, or apparent/ostensible authority legally bind the principal (the Insurer).
  • Functions: Solicits insurance business, assists clients in completing proposal forms, collects premiums, and issues interim cover notes if authorized.

4. The Insurance Broker

Legal Definition: Defined under Section 3 of the Insurance Act, 2009 as an independent intermediary acting with complete freedom regarding their choice of undertaking, who brings together persons seeking insurance and insurance undertakings for commission or compensation, carries out preparatory work toward concluding insurance contracts, and assists in policy administration and claim handling.

  • Legal Status: Prima facie, a broker is an independent contractor and acts as an agent of the Insured/Proposer when procuring coverage or advising on suitable policies.
  • Personal Liability: Under Section 70(1) of the Insurance Act, 2009, a broker is personally liable for their own acts or omissions—and those of their employees—in transacting insurance business, and must maintain mandatory Professional Indemnity (PI) insurance.
  • Exception (The Dual Agency / Implied Agency Rule): Although defined by statute as an independent entity, a broker may become an agent of the insurer in specific factual contexts (e.g., when entrusted by the insurer with signed policy documents, official cover notes, and authorized premium collection).

Statutory Registration Framework under Tanzanian Law

Under the Insurance Act, No. 10 of 2009, no entity or individual may carry on insurance business, mediate contracts, or act as an intermediary in Tanzania without registration and a valid license issued by TIRA through the Commissioner of Insurance.

A. Registration of Insurers (Underwriters)

  • Mandatory Incorporation: Must be a company incorporated under the Companies Act, Cap 212 [R.E. 2019] or a body established by law.
  • Minimum Capital Requirements: Must maintain prescribed minimum solvency margins and paid-up capital deposited in approved financial institutions within Tanzania.
  • Separation of Business: Life assurance (long-term business) and General insurance business must be legally separated. Composite insurers are generally barred from operating under a single legal entity to safeguard long-term life policy funds from general business volatility.
  • Governance & Key Personnel: Principal Officers, directors, and actuaries must pass TIRA's "Fit and Proper Person" test.

B. Registration of Intermediaries (Brokers & Agents)

  • Insurance Brokers:
    • Must incorporate as a corporate entity under the Companies Act.
    • Must post and maintain a bank guarantee or security deposit with TIRA.
    • Must maintain mandatory Professional Indemnity (PI) Insurance Cover (Section 70(1)).
    • Must establish separate, audited Client Premium Accounts to segregate insurance money from operational funds.
  • Insurance Agents:
    • Must possess an agency agreement executed by a licensed insurer.
    • Must obtain an operating license from TIRA, renewable annually.
    • Must complete prescribed professional insurance training certification.
  • Bancassurance Intermediaries: Regulated under the Insurance (Bancassurance) Regulations. Banking institutions must secure TIRA licensing before offering insurance products through their retail branch networks.

Formation of Insurance Contracts: Contributions & Liabilities

An insurance contract is governed by general contract principles set out in the Law of Contract Act, Cap 345, supplemented by specific common law doctrines and statutory provisions.

1. Offer and Acceptance

  • The Proposal Form (Offer): The contract normally originates when the proposer (insured) completes and submits a standardized proposal form. Legally, the completed proposal form constitutes an offer by the prospective insured to the insurer.
  • Acceptance: Acceptance occurs when the insurer signifies unequivocal agreement to cover the risk—either by issuing an official acceptance letter, releasing a formal Insurance Policy, issuing a Cover Note, or receiving and retaining the premium without reservation.
  • Counter-Offer: If the insurer accepts the proposal subject to special conditions, higher premiums, or modified endorsement warranties, this act constitutes a counter-offer requiring the proposer's consent.

2. Consideration (The Premium)

The premium paid by the insured constitutes the legal consideration in exchange for the insurer's promise to indemnify against specified contingencies. Without valid consideration or an enforceable promise to pay, the contract lacks legal force.

3. Interim Insurance: The Legal Effect of Cover Notes

A Cover Note serves as a temporary, interim contract of insurance issued to provide immediate coverage while the insurer formally processes the proposal form and prepares the final policy documentation.

  • It binds the insurer to the risk from the minute of issuance until it expires or is superseded by a formal policy (Julian Praet et Cie v. H. Poland Ltd [1960] 1 Lloyd's Rep 416).
  • Under Tanzanian regulatory practice, a cover note must explicitly state the full premium charged and contain mandatory statutory notices.

4. Fundamental Doctrines Influencing Formation

  • Uberrima Fides (Utmost Good Faith): Insurance contracts are contracts of uberrimae fidei. Unlike ordinary commercial transactions governed by caveat emptor, both parties must disclose all material facts within their knowledge prior to contract conclusion (Carter v. Boehm (1766) 3 Burr 1905; Pan Atlantic Insurance Co. Ltd v. Pine Top Insurance Co. Ltd [1995] 1 AC 501). Non-disclosure or misrepresentation of a material fact renders the contract voidable at the option of the aggrieved party.
  • Adhesion & Contra Proferentem Rule: Insurance policies are standard form contracts ("contracts of adhesion") drafted exclusively by underwriters. Where policy language is ambiguous, Tanzanian courts strictly apply the contra proferentem rule, interpreting ambiguous provisions against the drafting insurer and in favor of the policyholder.

Non-Remittance of Premiums by Intermediaries: Legal Analysis & Case Law

A recurring issue in Tanzanian commercial litigation involves scenarios where a policyholder pays an insurance premium in full to a broker or agent, but the intermediary fails, neglects, or fraudulently refuses to remit the funds to the insurance company before a loss occurs.

1. The Cash-and-Carry Regulatory Rule in Tanzania

To curb financial instability caused by uncollected premiums, Tanzanian law enforces strict statutory limits on premium credit:

Section 137(1) of the Insurance Act, 2009: Authorizes the Commissioner of Insurance to set mandatory time limits within which insurance premiums must be remitted to Tanzanian insurers.

Regulation 35 of the Insurance Regulations, 2009:
  • Regulation 35(a): An insurance policy becomes invalid retroactively to the date of inception if full payment is not made within 7 days of inception, EXCEPT in the case of Motor Insurance, where the premium SHALL be paid at policy inception ("Cash-and-Carry Rule").
  • Regulation 35(b): All insurance policies must disclose this requirement in bold print on every cover note and policy document.

2. The Core Conflict: Statutory Invalidation vs. Agency Principles

When an intermediary collects a motor insurance premium from a policyholder but fails to remit it to the insurer, insurers historically argued under Regulation 35(a) that the policy was void ab initio for non-receipt of premium.

However, Tanzanian jurisprudence balances this regulatory rule against general agency doctrines under the Law of Contract Act, Cap 345 and common law rules regarding apparent authority.

The General Rule of Agency Law: Payment made to an agent who possesses express, implied, or apparent authority to collect premiums is legally deemed to be payment directly to the principal (the Insurer). The intermediary's failure to forward those funds is an internal administrative breach between the insurer and its agent, which cannot prejudice an innocent policyholder.

Landmark Judicial Authorities

1. Niko Insurance (T) Limited v. Hussein Athuman Mwaifyusi & Agin Insurance Brokers Limited (Civil Appeal No. 168 of 2017) [2021] TZCA 465 (6 September 2021)

Facts: The 1st Respondent (Hussein Mwaifyusi) secured a comprehensive motor insurance policy for his commercial taxi from Niko Insurance (Appellant) through Agin Insurance Brokers (2nd Respondent). He paid the full TZS 300,000 premium directly to the broker, who issued a cover note and a policy signed by Niko's CEO. Later, the vehicle was involved in an accident. Niko Insurance denied liability, claiming that Agin was an independent broker (not its agent) and had failed to remit the premium. Niko argued that under Regulation 35 of the Insurance Regulations, the policy lapsed retroactively due to non-receipt of premium.

Holding & Legal Principles Established by the Court of Appeal of Tanzania:

  1. Broker Acting as Implied/Actual Agent: Although a broker is defined under Section 3 of the Insurance Act, 2009 as an independent intermediary, where an insurer entrusts a broker with signed policies, official cover notes, and authorized premium collection, the broker acts beyond its statutory role and becomes the agent of the insurer for delivery and premium collection.
  2. Payment to Broker constitutes Payment to Insurer: Citing persuasive common law authorities (Foundation Reserve Insurance Co. Inc v. Ed S. Wesson (1969) and Bohlinger v. Zanger (1954)), the Court held that payment of a premium to a broker authorized to deliver policies and collect premiums is legally payment to the insurance company. This applies even if the broker converts the money or fails to forward it.
  3. Compliance with Cash-and-Carry Rule: The insured complied fully with Regulation 35(a) by paying the full premium at inception to the broker (who held apparent authority from the insurer). The broker's failure to remit the funds internally did not invalidate the policy. Niko Insurance was held fully liable to indemnify the insured for repair costs, storage charges, and general damages.
2. Foundation Reserve Insurance Co. Inc v. Ed S. Wesson, 447 S.W.2d 436 (Texas Civ. App. 1969) (Adopted by the Court of Appeal of Tanzania)

Principle: Agency may be established by circumstantial evidence. When an insurer entrusts a broker with policy documents for delivery to an insured without specific directions from the insured, the broker is legally regarded as the agent of the insurance company for premium collection. Payment to the broker discharges the insured's obligation.

3. Bohlinger v. Zanger, 306 N.Y. 228 (N.Y. Ct. App. 1954) (Adopted by the Court of Appeal of Tanzania)

Principle: From early common law, when an insurer entrusts a broker with an executed insurance policy for delivery, the broker acts as the insurer's agent for receiving the premium. Payment to the broker constitutes full legal payment to the insurer.

4. Stockwell v. Mutual Life Insurance Co. (1903) & Related Common Law Rules

Principle: An insurer that clothes an intermediary with ostensible authority (providing official stamps, stationery, cover notes, and policies) is estopped from denying that intermediary's authority to receive premiums on its behalf.

Legal Remedies for Insurers Against Defaulting Intermediaries

Where an insurer is held liable to pay a policyholder's claim despite non-receipt of premium due to broker default:

  • Civil Action for Breach of Trust & Conversion: The insurer may sue the broker to recover all unremitted premiums and consequential damages.
  • Enforcement of Professional Indemnity (PI) Cover: Under Section 70(1) of the Insurance Act, Cap 394, brokers must maintain mandatory PI insurance, which can be attached to satisfy liabilities arising from professional default or negligence.

Topic 3: Insurance Contracts

According to Tanzania legal authorities there is no clear definition of insurance contracts. However, insurance contracts defined under various case laws like Prudential Insurance Co. v. Commissioners of Inland Revenue, Judgment of 25 July, 2018 Insurance Contract define as "A contract for some consideration or premium where you secure yourself some benefit (money/indemnification upon it happen in some event) which usually happen unexpected or uncertainty."
In modern insurance law, an insurance contract refers to the contract which insurer promised to pay (indemnity) insured against loss or damage based on premium.

Classification of Insurance Contracts

In English insurance law, insurance contracts are classified based on the nature of the risk, the subject matter insured, and the measure of indemnity provided. Under the framework codified by the Marine Insurance Act 1906 and general common law principles, insurance contracts are broadly categorized into three major classifications:

1. Classification by Measure of Indemnity (Indemnity vs. Non-Indemnity)

The primary legal classification distinguishes contracts designed strictly to reimburse actual pecuniary loss from those designed to pay a pre-stipulated sum upon the happening of a contingent event.

a. Contracts of Indemnity

A contract of marine or property insurance is fundamentally a contract of indemnity. Under Section 1 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, the insurer undertakes to indemnify the assured against marine losses to the extent agreed. The basic principle dictates that the assured shall be fully indemnified, but shall never be more than fully indemnified (Castellain v Preston (1883)).

  • Valued Policies: Under Section 27(3) of the 1906 Act, the agreed value specified in the policy is conclusive between the parties as to the insurable value of the subject matter, in the absence of fraud. While this may lead to an indemnity that is not mathematically perfect (for example, Irving v Manning (1847)), indemnity remains the legal basis of the bargain.
  • Unvalued Policies: Under Section 28, an unvalued policy does not specify the value of the subject matter, leaving the insurable value to be subsequently ascertained, subject to the statutory limits of the policy.
  • Equitable Principles: To preserve the indemnity principle, auxiliary rules such as Subrogation (Section 79), Contribution (Section 80), and Double Insurance (Section 32) apply strictly to indemnity contracts.

b. Non-Indemnity Contracts (Contingency / Life Policies)

Unlike property or marine insurance, life assurance and personal accident policies are non-indemnity contracts. As established in Dalby v India and London Life Assurance Co (1854), life policies are agreements to pay a specified sum upon the occurrence of a specified event (such as death or injury). Consequently, principles of subrogation and contribution do not apply to contingency insurance.

2. Classification by Duration and Risk Attachment (Time, Voyage, and Mixed Policies)

In marine insurance, contracts are formally classified under Section 25(1) of the Marine Insurance Act 1906 according to how the period of risk is defined.

Policy Type Statutory Definition (MIA 1906) Key Characteristics & Application
Time Policy Where the contract is to insure the subject matter for a definite period of time. Commonly used for ship hulls (for example, Institute Time Clauses Hulls). Under Section 39(5), there is no implied warranty of seaworthiness at the outset, but unseaworthiness known to the assured may discharge liability.
Voyage Policy Where the contract is to insure the subject matter "at and from", or from one place to another. Commonly used for cargo (Institute Cargo Clauses). Subject to strict implied warranties under Section 39(1) that the ship shall be seaworthy at the commencement of the voyage.
Mixed Policy A policy where the subject matter is insured both for a specified voyage and for a definite period of time. For example, a vessel insured for a voyage from Bagamoyo to Liverpool and for 30 days after arrival.

3. Classification by Subject Matter Insured

Under Section 3(2) of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, marine adventures and insurance contracts in general are categorized according to the underlying subject matter exposed to risks. Under Section 26(1), the subject matter must be designated with reasonable certainty.

a. Physical Property Insurances

  • Ship / Hull Policies: Covers the hull, machinery, materials, outfit, and stores (Rule 15, Rules for Construction; ITCH(95)).
  • Cargo / Goods: Covers merchandise shipped. Under Rule 17, deck cargo and live animals must be specifically insured rather than under the general denomination of "goods" unless customary usage dictates otherwise (British and Foreign Marine Insurance Co v Gaunt [1921]).
  • Other Movables: Covers tangible property other than the ship, including money and valuable documents (Section 90).

b. Pecuniary Rights and Expectancies

  • Freight: Includes the profit derivable by a shipowner from carrying his own goods, as well as freight payable by a third party (Section 90). It encompasses ordinary bill of lading freight, chartered freight, and advance freight (Section 12).
  • Profits and Commissions: Expected earnings or anticipated profits arising from the safe arrival of goods or execution of a venture (for example, Wilson v Jones (1867)).
  • Disbursements: Expenditure incurred by a shipowner or agent in preparing for or executing a voyage.

c. Liability Insurances

Covers indemnification against third-party liabilities incurred by reason of maritime perils or general operations (Section 3(2)(c)), such as collision liabilities under the 3/4ths Collision Liability Clause or Protection and Indemnity (P&I) Club entries.

4. Classification by Enforceability (Valid Contracts vs. Wagers)

Insurance law strictly segregates legally enforceable contracts of risk-transfer from void gambling or wagering agreements.

  • Valid Contracts: Contracts where the assured possesses a recognized legal or equitable relation to the property or adventure at risk (Insurable Interest, Section 5).
  • Void / Wagering Contracts: Under Section 4(1), any insurance contract entered into by way of gaming or wagering is void.
    • Contracts where the assured has no insurable interest and no bona fide expectation of acquiring one (Section 4(2)(a)).
    • "PPI" (Policy Proof of Interest) / Honour Policies: Policies containing terms such as "interest or no interest" or "without further proof of interest than the policy itself" (Section 4(2)(b)). These contracts are legally void regardless of whether an actual insurable interest exists in fact (Thomas Cheshire & Co v Vaughan Bros & Co [1920]). Furthermore, under the Marine Insurance (Gambling Policies) Act 1909, effecting a policy without interest constitutes a criminal offense.
    • Lucena v Craufurd (1806) 2 Bos&P (NR) 269 (Definition of Insurable Interest).
      England and France were at war and Holland was neutral, but under threat from France. Legislation provided that Crown commissioners could take possession of, and manage the affairs of, ships owned by Dutch nationals, but only when such ships were brought into a British port. Ships were taken into possession by British naval vessels but losses were suffered before they reached a British port. The commissioners had insured the vessels when they were at sea. It was held that there was no insurable interest vested in the commissioners at that time. The fact that there was an expectation that ultimately they would reach the safety of a British port was not sufficient to create an insurable interest while they were still at sea.
    • Castellain v Preston (1883) 11 QBD 380 (The Indemnity Principle).
    • Irving v Manning (1847) 1 HLC 287 (Valued Policies and Indemnity).
    • British & Foreign Marine Insurance Co v Samuel Sanday & Co [1915] 1 AC 650 (Loss of Adventure).
    • Thomas Cheshire & Co v Vaughan Bros & Co [1920] 3 KB 240 (Void PPI Policies).

1. Making/Formation of the Insurance Contract

A. Offer and Acceptance

Insurance contracts follow standard contractual principles of offer, acceptance, and consideration, subject to specialized features. Under Section 22 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, marine insurance contracts are inadmissible in evidence unless embodied in a written policy; non-marine insurance contracts do not legally require writing, though written documentation is standard practice.

An insurance application or completed proposal form typically constitutes an offer by the applicant. Unconditional acceptance by the insurer completes the agreement. However, if acceptance introduces modified terms or premium calculations not previously agreed upon, it operates as a counter-offer requiring the applicant's assent.

  • Canning v Farquhar (1886) 16 QBD 727: A proposal for life insurance was accepted by the insurer on the condition that the first premium be paid. Before payment, the applicant suffered severe injuries. The insurer refused tender of the premium. The Court of Appeal held that the insurer was not bound. The acceptance was conditional, and the alter-condition of the risk prior to payment altered the basis of the proposal, allowing the insurer to decline the risk.
  • Looker v Law Union and Rock Insurance Co [1928] 1 KB 554: The applicant stated he was in good health in a proposal that formed the basis of the contract. Between acceptance and payment of the first premium, the applicant fell critically ill and died shortly after payment was sent. The court held the insurer was not liable: material changes in the risk occurring prior to contract completion must be disclosed.

B. Consideration (Premium) and Renewals

The insured’s consideration is the premium (or the legal obligation to pay it), while the insurer’s consideration is the promise to indemnify or pay on the happening of an insured event.

Under Section 31 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, where insurance is effected at a premium "to be arranged" and no arrangement is made, a reasonable premium is payable. Also, under section 72(1) of Insurance Act, Cap 394, it cover the duty of the insured to pay the premium, and the insurer's duty to provide cover, as consideration for the contract. However, section 139(1) of Insurance act, Cap 394, it provides limitation of periods for the payment of premium, and the insurer's right to cancel the contract if the premium is not paid within the specified period (14 days).

Non-life insurance policies are typically annual contracts requiring renewal. At common law, insurers are not legally obligated to issue renewal notices. However, self-regulatory codes such as the General Insurance Standards Council (GISC) Private Customer and Commercial Codes—mandate advance renewal notices to ensure continuity of cover.

C. Cover Notes (Interim Insurance)

Cover notes provide immediate, temporary insurance protection while the formal proposal is processed. A cover note constitutes an independent contract of interim insurance. Section 72(2) of Insurance Act, Cap 394, it provides that the cover note is a temporary contract of insurance, while it take effect until the insured deposit premium.

The duty of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) applies fully to interim cover notes, requiring disclosure of all material facts known at the time the cover note is issued.

  • Julien Praet et Cie v Poland [1960] 1 Lloyd's Rep 416: Highlighted the commercial necessity of cover notes in providing immediate cover for motorists before formal underwriting is completed.
  • Mayne Nickless Ltd v Pegler [1974] 1 NSWLR 228: Interim cover issued subject to a "satisfactory proposal" was rendered void when the applicant failed to disclose a prior motor accident on obtaining the cover note, despite completing the proposal form later.
  • Australian Insurance Contracts Act 1984 (Cth), Section 38: Replaced strict common law rules by rendering void any provision in an interim insurance contract making the insurer's liability dependent on the formal acceptance of a subsequent proposal form.

D. Insurers' Usual Terms & Consumer Protection

Where an insurer accepts a proposal based on its "usual terms," those terms bind the insured if the insured had actual or constructive knowledge of them through prior course of dealings or available specimen policies.

Under the Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Regulations 1999 (SI 1999/2083), terms that bind a consumer to obligations they had no real opportunity to become acquainted with prior to the conclusion of the contract may be scrutinised for unfairness.

  • Re Coleman's Depositaries Ltd and Life and Health Assurance Association [1907] 2 KB 798: An employer obtained interim cover for workplace liabilities. An injury occurred before the formal policy—containing an immediate notice condition—was delivered. The Court of Appeal held that the insured was not bound by the uncommunicated condition prior to receiving the formal policy document.

E. Contract Formation at Lloyd's

Insurance at Lloyd's is placed through brokers acting on behalf of the proposer. The broker prepares a "slip" outlining the risk details using standardized market abbreviations. The slip is presented to active underwriters representing syndicates.

When a syndicate underwriter initials the slip ("subscribes"), a binding contract of insurance is formed for that underwriter's specified percentage of the risk. Each participating syndicate enters into a separate, independent contract for its respective proportion of the risk.

2. Tanzanian Insurance Law Context

A. Nature of Insurance Contracts in Tanzania

Insurance contracts possess unique legal characteristics:

  • Aleatory Contract: A contract dependent on uncertain future events involving unequal values exchanged.
  • Contract of Adhesion: A standardized form contract drafted by one party on a "take it or leave it" basis.
  • Uberrimae Fidei (Utmost Good Faith): Both parties are required to disclose all material facts.
  • Contract of Indemnity: Intended solely to compensate actual loss without yielding a profit (excluding life insurance).

3. Breaking the Contract: Vitiating Factors

A. Mistake

Contracts of insurance are subject to general contractual rules on mistake:

  • Common Mistake: Renders a contract void ab initio at common law where both parties enter an agreement under a fundamental shared misconception going to the root of the contract (for example, Strickland v Turner (1852) 7 Exch 208, where an annuity was purchased on a life that had already expired).
  • Mutual Mistake: Occurs where parties operate at genuine cross-purposes, preventing consensus ad idem.
  • Unilateral Mistake & Rectification: Occurs where one party is mistaken and the other knows of the error. Rectification requires convincing proof that the written document failed to record the actual common intention of the parties (such as, Mint Security Ltd v Blair [1982] 2 Lloyd's Rep 188).

B. Illegality

An insurance contract may be void or unenforceable for illegality under statutory provisions or public policy principles.

1. Unauthorised Insurers

Under Section 2 of the Insurance Companies Act 1982 (and subsequent framework under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA)), insurers must be statutory authorised to carry on insurance business in the UK.

Under Section 132 of the Financial Services Act 1986 (retained in Section 26–28 of FSMA 2000):

  • An insurance contract issued by an unauthorized insurer is unenforceable against the insured.
  • The insured is entitled to recover any money or property paid under the contract, along with compensation for loss sustained.
  • The court retains judicial discretion to enforce the contract at the instance of the unauthorized insurer if the insurer reasonably believed it was not acting in contravention of authorisation requirements and enforcement is just and equitable.

2. Public Policy and Unlawful Acts

Contracts that insure against the legal consequences of intentional criminal acts or deliberate wrongdoing committed by the insured are void as contrary to public policy.

Legal Principles and Case Law:
  • Beresford v Royal Insurance Co Ltd [1938] 2 All ER 602 (HL): The House of Lords held that an insured who deliberately took his own life (which was then a crime) could not derive a benefit, nor could his personal representatives recover insurance proceeds, due to the principle that a person cannot benefit from their own criminal act.
  • Forfeiture Act 1982: Grants courts discretionary power to modify the strict forfeiture rule excluding a wrongdoer from obtaining property/insurance benefits resulting from an unlawful killing (excluding murder).
  • Hardy v Motor Insurers' Bureau [1964] 2 QB 745: Third-party victims of intentional criminal acts involving motor vehicles remain protected under compulsory motor liability insurance schemes, even though the criminal driver is precluded from claiming personal indemnity.
  • Geismar v Sun Alliance and London Insurance Ltd [1978] QB 383: An insured who brought uncustomed goods into the UK without paying import duty could not claim indemnity under a contents policy for their subsequent theft, as enforced indemnity would allow the insured to profit from intentional illegal conduct.

C. Misrepresentation and Non-Disclosure

Insurance contracts are contracts of the utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei). Section 17 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906 provides that if the utmost good faith be not observed by either party, the contract may be avoided by the other party.

Under Sections 18–20 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, the prospective insured must disclose every material circumstance known to them prior to contract conclusion. A circumstance is material if it would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium or determining whether to take the risk (Pan Atlantic Insurance Co Ltd v Pine Top Insurance Co Ltd [1995] 1 AC 501).

Breach of the duty of disclosure or misrepresentation of a material fact entitles the innocent party to rescind the contract ab initio.

Topic 4: Terms, Warranties & Conditions

1. Terms and Warranties in Insurance Contracts

In insurance law, a significant distinction exists between basic conditions and warranties. According to Marine Insurance Law principles, warranties carry a meaning distinct from general contract law.

A. Conditions

Conditions are essential terms forming the core of a contract. Non-compliance or breach of a condition entitles the aggrieved party to repudiate the contract or refuse indemnity.

  • Condition Precedent: An example is giving timely notice of a claim. In Legal and General Assurance Society Ltd v Drake Insurance Co Ltd (1992), it was held that a clause requiring immediate written notice serves as a condition precedent to liability.
  • Effect of Breach: Breaching a condition precedent following an incident entitles the insurer to reject the claim.

B. Insurance Warranties

Under Section 33(1) of the Marine Insurance Act 1906, a Promissory Warranty is a warranty by which the insured undertakes that a particular thing shall or shall not be done, or that some condition shall be fulfilled, or affirms/negates the existence of a particular state of facts.

"A warranty must be exactly complied with, whether it be material to the risk or not."

Types of Warranties:

  1. Express Warranties: Explicitly set out in the policy or incorporated documents.
  2. Implied Warranties: Not explicitly stated but implied by law, such as:
    • Warranty of Seaworthiness: An implied undertaking that the vessel is seaworthy for the intended voyage (s. 39).
    • Warranty of Legality: An implied undertaking that the insured adventure is lawful and will be carried out in a lawful manner (s. 41).

Effects of Breach of Warranty:

Under Section 33(3) of the MIA 1906, a breach of warranty automatically discharges the insurer from liability as of the date of the breach. It provides no defense that the breach was remedied before the loss occurred (s. 34(2)).

C. Exclusion Clauses (Exceptions and Excluded Losses)

Exclusion clauses limit or exempt the insurer’s liability for specified perils or losses.

Statutory Exclusions under Section 55 of the MIA 1906 include:

  • Wilful Misconduct: Losses attributable to the intentional misconduct of the insured.
  • Delay: Losses proximately caused by delay, even if the delay was caused by an insured peril.
  • Ordinary Wear and Tear: Normal wear, tear, or gradual deterioration of the subject matter.
  • Inherent Vice: Losses caused by the natural defect or inherent nature of the goods (e.g., improper packing or decay).
  • War & Strikes Exclusions: Losses arising from war risks, strikes, or terrorism (unless covered under specific extensions).

Topic 5: Insurance Principles

Fundamental Principles of Insurance Law and Their Application

The valid formation of an insurance contract and the legal right to enforce a claim are built upon six fundamental principles established by common law, statute, and judicial precedent.

1. Utmost Good Faith (Uberrima Fides)

  • Principle: Unlike ordinary commercial transactions governed by caveat emptor (let the buyer beware), insurance contracts require the highest standard of honesty. Both the proposer and the insurer must voluntarily disclose all material facts that would influence a prudent underwriter in assessing the risk or setting the premium.
  • Application during Formation: Operates during pre-contractual negotiations. Misrepresentation or fraudulent non-disclosure gives the aggrieved party the legal right to rescind the contract ab initio (from inception).
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • Carter v. Boehm (1766) 3 Burr 1905: Lord Mansfield established that insurance is a contract upon speculation where material facts generally lie in the knowledge of the insured alone.
    • Pan Atlantic Insurance Co. Ltd v. Pine Top Insurance Co. Ltd [1995] 1 AC 501: Clarified that a material fact is one that would influence the judgment of a prudent underwriter in determining whether to take the risk.
    • General provisions on voidable agreements under the Law of Contract Act, Cap 345 [R.E. 2019].

2. Insurable Interest

  • Principle: The insured must have a recognized legal or equitable financial relationship to the subject matter of insurance, such that they benefit from its safety and suffer direct pecuniary loss from its damage or destruction.
  • Application during Formation: Without insurable interest, the agreement is a wagering or gaming contract and is void by law.
    • General/Property Insurance: Insurable interest must exist both at the time of contract formation and at the time of loss.
    • Life Assurance: Insurable interest must exist at the time the policy is issued.
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • Lucena v. Craufurd (1806) 2 Bos & Pul NR 269: Established that interest implies a right in property or a right derivable out of a contract regarding property.
    • Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co. Ltd [1925] AC 619: Held that even a sole shareholder and creditor of a company lacks an insurable interest in the physical assets owned by the company entity.
    • Gaming Act 1845 (Statute of General Application in Tanzania).

3. Principle of Indemnity

  • Principle: Applies to general insurance contracts (e.g., fire, marine, motor). The insured is entitled to be restored to the exact financial position they occupied immediately prior to the loss—no more, no less. Insurance cannot be used as a vehicle for profit.
  • Application to Claims: Excludes life assurance and personal accident policies (which are fixed-benefit contracts). The insurer calculates loss based on market value, repair cost, or replacement cost, capped by the sum insured.
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • Castellain v. Preston (1883) 11 QBD 380: Reaffirmed that indemnity is the controlling principle of general insurance law, ensuring the policyholder never recovers more than the actual loss suffered.

4. Subrogation

  • Principle: A direct corollary of indemnity. Once an insurer fully indemnifies the insured for a loss, the insurer steps into the legal shoes of the insured to exercise all rights, remedies, and claims the insured possesses against third-party tortfeasors responsible for the loss.
  • Application to Claim Rights: Prevents double recovery by the insured (recovering from both the insurer and the tortfeasor). The insurer sues the wrongdoer in the insured's name.
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • Burnand v. Rodocanachi (1882) 7 App Cas 333: Confirmed that the insurer is entitled to every gift or advantage that reduces the loss paid.
    • Yorkshire Insurance Co. Ltd v. Nisbet Shipping Co. Ltd [1962] 2 QB 330: Reaffirmed that subrogation rights arise only upon full payment of indemnity.

5. Contribution

  • Principle: Applies when an insured maintains multiple insurance policies with different underwriters covering the exact same subject matter, risk, and interest. Under total loss, the insured cannot collect full indemnity from every insurer; instead, the loss is prorated among the insurers.
  • Application to Claim Rights: If one insurer pays the full claim to the policyholder, it holds an equitable right to claim proportional contribution from co-insurers.
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • North British & Mercantile Insurance Co. v. London, Liverpool & Globe Insurance Co. (1877) 5 Ch D 569: Established requirements for contribution: identical subject matter, same risk, same interest, and multiple enforceable contracts.

6. Proximate Cause (Causa Proxima)

  • Principle: The active, efficient, and dominant cause that sets in motion a continuous chain of events bringing about the loss, without the intervention of any new, independent force (causa proxima non remota spectatur).
  • Application to Claims: To establish a valid claim, the proximate cause of the loss must be an insured peril covered under the policy, rather than an excluded or uninsured peril.
  • Statutory & Case Law Authorities:
    • Leyland Shipping Co. v. Norwich Union Fire Insurance Society [1918] AC 350: The House of Lords held that proximate cause is not simply the last event in time, but the dominant and effective cause.

2.2 Non-Remittance of Premiums by Intermediaries

When a policyholder pays the full premium to a broker or agent who subsequently defaults on remitting the money to the insurer, the legal position is settled by Tanzanian jurisprudence:

Niko Insurance (T) Limited v. Hussein Athuman Mwaifyusi & Agin Insurance Brokers Limited (Civil Appeal No. 168 of 2017) [2021] TZCA 465

Facts: The 1st Respondent paid the full motor insurance premium to an insurance broker (2nd Respondent), who issued a cover note and a policy signed by the Appellant's management. The broker failed to remit the premium to Niko Insurance. Following an accident, Niko Insurance denied liability, citing non-receipt of premium and Regulation 35 of the Insurance Regulations, 2009.

Legal Principles Established by the Court of Appeal of Tanzania:

  1. Broker Acting as Implied/Actual Agent: Where an insurer entrusts a broker with signed policies, official cover notes, and authorized premium collection, the broker acts beyond its statutory independent role and becomes the agent of the insurer for delivery and premium collection.
  2. Payment to Broker is Payment to Insurer: Payment of a premium to an authorized broker is legally payment to the insurance company. The broker's failure to remit the funds internally does not invalidate the contract, nor does it prejudice an innocent policyholder.
  3. Insured's Compliance: The insured complied fully with Regulation 35 by paying the premium at inception to the intermediary holding ostensible authority. Niko Insurance was held fully liable to indemnify the insured.

3.3 Legal Remedies for Insurers Against Defaulting Intermediaries

Where an insurer is held liable to pay a policyholder's claim despite non-receipt of premium due to broker default:

  • Civil Action for Breach of Trust & Conversion: The insurer may sue the broker to recover all unremitted premiums and consequential damages.
  • Enforcement of Professional Indemnity (PI) Cover: Under Section 70(1) of the Insurance Act, Cap 394, brokers must maintain mandatory PI insurance, which can be attached to satisfy liabilities arising from professional default or negligence.
