Topic 1: Introduction to Business Association Law

1.1 Meaning of Business

A business refers to the exchange of goods or services in consideration for money to generate a profit. It can also be defined as an enterprise or entity engaged in commercial, industrial, or professional activities, or the organized efforts and activities of individuals to produce and sell goods and services for profit.

1.2 Definition of Association & Business Association

  • Association: A group of individuals coming together to form a single entity or organization for a specific shared purpose.
  • Business Association: An organizational framework bringing together business owners or investors within a specific domain or legal structure to conduct trade, commercial, or industrial activities for mutual profit.

1.3 Sources of Business Association Law in Tanzania

  1. The Constitution: The Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (CURT), 1977 (e.g., Articles 64(5) and 30(5)).
  2. Statutes / Acts of Parliament: Principal legislation governing business entities, including the Companies Act (Cap 212), the Law of Contract Act (LCA, Cap 345), and the Electronic Transactions Act.
  3. Subsidiary / Delegated Legislation: Statutory instruments, rules, and regulations issued by authorized administrative bodies or Ministers.
  4. Case Law / Precedents: Decisions from superior courts setting binding principles (e.g., Makaula v. Regem).
  5. Received Law: Common Law, Doctrines of Equity, and Statutes of General Application as incorporated under Section 2(3) of the Judicature and Application of Laws Act (JALA, Cap 358).
  6. Customary Law: Recognized under Section 9(1) of JALA (Cap 358) where applicable.
  7. Islamic Law: Applied in personal and civil matters under relevant statutory rules.
  8. International Law: Treaties, conventions, and Private International Law principles adopted by Tanzania.

Topic 2: Forms of Business Enterprises in Tanzania

2.1 Sole Proprietorship (Sole Trader)

A business owned and run by one individual in pursuit of profit. The business may operate under the owner's personal name or a registered trade/assumed name.

Key Elements & Characteristics:

  • Simplicity: It is the easiest form of business to establish, operate, and maintain.
  • Autonomy: The owner exercises full control over management and operational decisions.
  • Unlimited Liability: The sole proprietor is personally liable for all obligations and debts incurred by the business.
  • Capacity: The sole trader may employ workers to assist in running the enterprise.
Procedure for Registering a Sole Proprietorship (Business Name) in Tanzania:
  1. Account Creation & Name Search: Create an account on the Business Registrations and Licensing Agency (BRELA) Online Registration System (ORS). Conduct a search to ensure the proposed business name is available and unique.
  2. Name Reservation: Reserve the chosen business name through BRELA.
  3. Documentation & Application: Prepare required credentials—National ID (NIDA) or Passport, Tax Identification Number (TIN) from TRA, physical business address details (e.g., lease agreement or utility bills), and completed statutory registration forms.
  4. Fee Payment & Certification: Pay prescribed registration fees to BRELA to obtain the Certificate of Registration.
  5. Tax & Local Licensing: Register with the Tanzania Revenue Authority (TRA) for a Tax Clearance Certificate, obtain a business license from the local municipal council (e.g., via the TAUSI portal), and comply with sector-specific regulations.

2.2 Partnership

Under Section 190(1) of the Law of Contract Act (LCA, Cap 345), a partnership is defined as the relation that subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view to profit.

Key Features & Legal Rules (Sections 190–195, LCA):

  • Plurality: Formed by two or more persons (subject to statutory maximum limits).
  • Mutual Agency: Every partner is an agent of the firm and of the other partners for the purpose of the partnership business.
  • Profit & Loss Sharing: Partners share profits and liabilities (such as business debts) as agreed upon.
  • Lack of Separate Legal Personality: A traditional partnership is not a distinct legal entity separate from its partners.
  • Determination Rules (Section 191, LCA): Joint tenancy, tenancy in common, or sharing gross returns does not of itself create a partnership without a legal contract or business intent.

Formation of Partnership (Section 193, LCA):

  • Express Contract: Formed explicitly via oral agreement or a written document known as a Partnership Deed.
  • Implied Contract: Inferred from the conduct of the parties and business dealings demonstrating partnership elements.

Types of Partnerships & Partners:

Type Description
General Partnership (GP) Partners have equal or defined rights to manage the firm and share joint and several liability for all debts.
Limited Partnership (LP) / LLP Consists of general partners and limited partners whose liabilities are restricted to their agreed capital contributions.
Dormant / Sleeping Partner Contributes capital and shares in profits but does not actively participate in management.
Salaried Partner Receives a fixed salary rather than a profit share; retains employee status relative to management.
Partner by Holding Out (Estoppel) A non-partner who allows themselves to be represented as a partner; under Section 206 of LCA, they are estopped from denying liability to third parties.

Duties and Liabilities of Partners (LCA Cap 345):

  • Duty of Utmost Good Faith (Section 192): Partners must carry on business for the greatest common benefit and remain faithful to one another.
  • Duty to Render Accounts: Must render true accounts and full information regarding all affairs affecting the firm.
  • Liabilities (Sections 194, 203, 204, 208): Partners are jointly and severally liable for torts, wrongful acts, or debts committed in the ordinary course of business (e.g., Hamlyn v. Houston & Co. [1903]).

Dissolution of Partnership (Sections 212–215, LCA):

  • By expiration of agreed term or completion of undertaking (Section 212).
  • By bankruptcy or death of a partner (Section 213).
  • By supervening illegality of the partnership business (Section 214).
  • By court decree on ground of insanity, permanent incapacity, or misconduct (Section 215).

Topic 3: Company Law & Incorporation

3.1 Nature and Characteristics of a Company

A company is an artificial legal person created under the provisions of the Companies Act (Cap 212). Chief Justice Marshall defined a corporation as "an artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law."

Core Legal Characteristics:

  1. Separate Legal Entity: A company is distinct from its owners (Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd. [1897]). It can own property, enter contracts, incur debt, and sue or be sued in its own name.
  2. Limited Liability: Members' liability is limited to the unpaid amount on their shares or the amount guaranteed in the Memorandum.
  3. Perpetual Succession: A company’s existence is uninterrupted by the death, insanity, or bankruptcy of individual shareholders.
  4. Transferability of Shares: Shares represent transferable personal property (regulated under Section 71 for public companies and restricted under Section 27 for private companies).
  5. Separate Management: Managed by an appointed Board of Directors rather than directly by all shareholders.

3.2 Key Differences: Partnership vs. Company

Feature Partnership Company
Legal Status No separate legal personality. Distinct legal entity.
Liability Generally unlimited joint and several liability. Limited by shares or guarantee.
Management Managed directly by partners (unless agreed otherwise). Managed by a Board of Directors.
Transfer of Interest Requires consent of all partners. Shares are freely transferable (subject to Articles).
Dissolution Dissolved easily by death, agreement, or court order. Wound up via formal statutory procedures.

3.3 Promoters & Pre-Incorporation Contracts

A promoter is any person who undertakes to form a company, sets it going, and takes necessary legal steps to accomplish incorporation (Twycross v. Grant).

  • Legal Status: A promoter is not an agent or trustee of the unformed company (as the company does not yet exist), but occupies a fiduciary position.
  • Fiduciary Duties:
    • Duty to disclose all secret profits made during promotion (Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co.).
    • Duty to avoid conflicts of interest and undue influence.
  • Pre-Incorporation Contracts: Contracts entered into by promoters before company incorporation are not binding on the company because an unformed entity lacks contractual capacity. A company cannot ratify pre-incorporation contracts; it must enter into a new contract (novation) after incorporation.

3.4 Corporate Veil & Exceptions (Lifting the Veil)

The corporate veil is a legal fiction separating the legal personality of the company from its directors, shareholders, and employees.

Courts or statutes may "lift" or "pierce" the corporate veil to hold individual officers personally liable in special circumstances:

  • Prevention of Fraud or Improper Conduct: Where a company is formed as a sham or device to avoid existing legal obligations (Gilford Motor Co. v. Horne [1933]; Jones v. Lipman).
  • Determination of Enemy Character: During wartime to identify controlling personnel (Daimler Co. Ltd. v. Continental Tyre & Rubber Co.).
  • Statutory Provisions: Under the Companies Act for fraudulent trading, reckless trading, or failure to maintain minimum membership requirements.

3.5 Directors and Corporate Governance

Directors serve as agents and fiduciary representatives through whom the artificial personality of the company acts (Ferguson v. Wilson [1866]). Under Sections 186–187 of the Companies Act, every company must have at least two directors.

Categories of Directors:

  • Executive Director: Full-time working director involved in daily management and operations (e.g., Managing Director/CEO).
  • Non-Executive Director: Part-time director appointed for independent governance, oversight, and policy consultation.
  • Shadow Director: A person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the directors of the company are accustomed to act.
  • De Facto Director: A person acting as a director without being formally or validly appointed.
  • Alternate Director: A substitute appointed to act in the absence or incapacity of an executive director.

Duties of Directors (Sections 181–185, Companies Act):

  • Duty to act bona fide in the best interests of the company.
  • Duty to exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence.
  • Duty to avoid conflicts of interest and disclose personal interest in contracts.
  • Duty to exercise powers for proper statutory and corporate purposes.

Topic 4: Joint Ventures, Mergers & Acquisitions

4.1 Joint Ventures (JV)

A Joint Venture is a strategic commercial arrangement in which two or more independent business entities pool their resources, capital, technology, and expertise to accomplish a specific project or business objective while maintaining their separate legal identities.

  • Types: Equity Joint Ventures (incorporating a new joint enterprise) or Contractual Joint Ventures (governed purely by a JV agreement).
  • Reasons for Formation: Sharing financial and operational risks, expanding market access, combining technology/IP, and improving capital capacity.

4.2 Mergers and Acquisitions (Fair Competition Act Framework)

Regulated principally under Sections 11 and 12 of the Fair Competition Act (FCA), 2003 in Tanzania:

  • Merger: The consolidation of two or more commercial entities into an existing enterprise or a new corporate entity.
  • Acquisition: The direct or indirect acquisition of shares, controlling interest, or assets of another enterprise.
  • Regulatory Review: Mergers and acquisitions meeting statutory financial thresholds require notification to and approval from the Fair Competition Commission (FCC) to prevent creation of dominant market position or abuse of trade.

Topic 5: Cooperative Societies in Tanzania

5.1 Meaning and Statutory Definition

Under Section 2 of the Co-operative Societies Act (Cap. 211 R.E. 2015), a Cooperative Society is an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social, and cultural needs and aspirations through a jointly-owned and democratically-controlled enterprise.

5.2 Core Principles of Cooperative Societies

Cooperative societies operate based on internationally recognized cooperative principles set out under Section 4 of Cap. 211:

  • Voluntary and Open Membership: Membership is open to all persons able to use their services and willing to accept responsibilities without gender, social, racial, political, or religious discrimination.
  • Democratic Member Control: Members have equal voting rights (one member, one vote) and actively participate in decision-making and setting policies.
  • Member Economic Participation: Members contribute equitably to, and democratically control, the capital of their cooperative.
  • Autonomy and Independence: Cooperatives are self-help, autonomous organizations controlled strictly by their members.
  • Education, Training, and Information: Cooperatives provide education and training for their members, elected representatives, managers, and employees.
  • Cooperation among Cooperatives: Strengthening the movement by working together through local, national, regional, and international structures.
  • Concern for Community: Working for the sustainable development of their communities through policies approved by their members.

5.3 Types and Hierarchy of Cooperative Societies

In Tanzania, cooperatives are structured in a hierarchical order (Sections 15–20, Cap. 211):

  1. Primary Cooperative Societies: Formed by at least 20 individual persons (or 10 in special agricultural/specialized sectors) who share a common interest at the local level (e.g., Agricultural Marketing Co-operative Societies - AMCOS, SACCOS).
  2. Secondary Cooperative Societies (Joint/Secondary Unions): Formed by two or more registered primary societies to provide specialized services to member primary units.
  3. Apex / National Federation: An overarching national organization representing all registered cooperative entities in the country (e.g., Tanzania Federation of Cooperatives - TFC).

5.4 Registration and Legal Personality

  • Registration Authority: Registration is managed by the Registrar of Cooperative Societies through the Tanzania Cooperative Development Commission (TCDC).
  • Effect of Registration (Section 35): Upon receiving a Certificate of Registration, a cooperative society becomes a body corporate with:
    • Perpetual succession and a common seal.
    • Power to hold real and personal property.
    • Power to enter into binding legal contracts.
    • Capacity to sue and be sued in its registered corporate name.

5.5 Rights, Liabilities, and Dissolution

  • Limited Liability: Liability of members for debts of the society is limited to unpaid amounts on shares held or specified guarantees in the society’s bylaws.
  • Management Structure: Managed by an elected Board/Board of Directors accountable to the General Meeting (Supreme organ of the society).
  • Dissolution / Winding Up: May be wound up by an order of the Registrar or through liquidation proceedings under Sections 96–105 of Cap. 211 due to insolvency, cancellation of registration, or membership falling below statutory minimum thresholds.
