LAND LAW

Topic 1: Introduction to the Land law

Meaning of Land Law

Land law is a body of doctrines governing the ownership, commercial transfer, and use of land. It focuses on the relationship between land, the legal rights that can exist in or over land, and the competing interests of individuals seeking to own or defeat those claims.

Core Components of Land Law

  • Ownership: Refers to the physical scope or extent of the land owned or possessed.
  • Interests: Rights enjoyed by a person who is not the absolute owner of the land, which remain binding on subsequent holders or owners (for example, lessees, mortgagees).
  • Restrictions: Limitations or statutory regulations governing specific forms of land disposition or interests in land.
  • Machinery of the Law: The procedural rules and mechanisms used to resolve land disputes.

Objectives of Studying Land Law

The general objective of studying land law is to understand how land is managed and regulated[cite: 211]. Specific objectives include:

  • Rights and Liabilities: Acquiring knowledge regarding rights and liabilities attached to interests in land and learning how to address them.
  • Property Distinction: Understanding the legal definition of land and its distinctions from other types of property.
  • Conveyancing Rules and Procedures: Mastering rules and procedures that regulate conveyancing. A conveyancer must account for various parties—such as vendors, vendees, mortgagors, mortgagees, lessors, and lessees—while ensuring clients acquire clear, unencumbered interests without a reduction in value.

Successful navigation of land law requires a disciplined approach, logical analysis, and the ability to think and argue conceptually within a framework of complex legal categories and subcategories.

The Meaning of Land

The Common Law Context in History: Land and Fixtures

At common law, articles annexed to land became part of the land, though exceptions were established for tenants to remove trade fixtures (e.g., bar fittings, petrol pumps) or ornamental/convenience fixtures (e.g., stoves, grates). However, fixtures that could not be removed without substantial damage, or items fixed purely for agricultural operations (as held in Elwes v. Maw), remained exceptions mitigated later by English Agricultural Holdings Acts[cite: 213, 214]. Statutory definitions in the UK (such as section 2(ii) of the Conveyancing and Law of Property Act 1881 and the Interpretation Act 1889) broadly defined land to include land of any tenure, tenements, hereditaments (corporeal and incorporeal), and buildings[cite: 214].

Land in the Tanzanian Context

Under section 2 of the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002), land includes:

  • The surface of the earth and the earth below the surface.
  • All substances other than minerals and petroleum forming part of or below the surface.
  • Things naturally growing on the land.
  • Buildings and other structures permanently affixed to land.

Historically, common law vested mines and minerals with the soil owner, except gold and silver (reserved via royal prerogative). In Tanganyika and post-independence Tanzania, all minerals (such as gold, diamond, tanzanite, copper) and petroleum are vested exclusively in the State[cite: 215].

Degree and Purpose of Annexation: Whether an object forms part of the land depends on its degree and purpose of annexation. Mere resting on soil is insufficient (Virji v. Abdulrehman)[cite: 215, 216]. Unexhausted improvement is defined under section 2 of the Land Act as anything permanently attached to land directly resulting from capital or labor expenditure, increasing productive capacity, utility, or environmental quality (including trees, standing crops, and growing produce)[cite: 216].

Real vs. Personal Property: Land is immovable property (Law of Limitation Act Cap. 89) categorized under common law as real property, while chattels are personal property. Real property includes corporeal hereditaments (tangible realty like houses and trees) and incorporeal hereditaments (intangible rights flowing from land, such as easements or rights of way).

Land is distinguished from other properties because:

  • It is permanent, allowing long-term rights to be created.
  • It supports a greater variety of rights than other property (e.g., leases, mortgages, easements, usufructuary rights).
  • It is an unusually valuable asset, requiring strict formalities for disposition (such as written transactions, prescribed forms, and approvals).
  • It is immovable, unlike most chattel.

In England, property is categorized into real property (primarily freehold land) and personal property (all other properties, including fixed-term leases). This classification is irrelevant in Tanzania due to the total abolition of historical real property concepts. Societal perceptions of land also vary globally; for example, Australian aborigines felt the land owned them rather than the other way around, while indigenous North Americans believed the earth was created without lines of demarcation and could not be privately owned.

B. Land Law

Land law regulates and governs land ownership, distribution, and use—essentially governing "land affairs". It deals with abstract interests such as leases, mortgages, freeholds, usufructuary rights, and easements rather than just physical soil. In Tanzania, primary sources of land law include the Constitution of the URT (1977), the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002), the Village Land Act (Cap. 114 R.E. 2002), GN No. 77 of 2001, and the Courts (Land Disputes Settlement) Act No. 2 of 2002.

Sources of Land Law

Section 180 of the Land Act outlines the laws applicable in adjudicating land issues in Tanzania Mainland, discouraging the application of common law except where matters cannot otherwise be catered for[cite: 217].

  • The Land Acts: The Land Act (Cap. 113) governs land other than village land, while the Village Land Act (Cap. 114) governs village land. Where conflicts arise, the Land Act prevails[cite: 217, 218].
  • Other Written Laws: Acts and Ordinances enacted by Parliament, including the Land Registration Act (Cap. 334), Registration of Documents Act (Cap. 117), Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 118), and Land Disputes Courts Act (Cap. 216).
  • The URT Constitution: The Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (1977, as amended) protects private property and mandates compensation for acquisitions under Article 24.
  • Customary Law: Recognized under section 180 of the Land Act and section 20 of the Village Land Act[cite: 218, 219]. Customary rules or decisions denying women, children, or persons with disabilities lawful access to land are void and inoperative. Section 11 of the Judicature and Application of Laws Act (JALA, Cap. 358) governs the civil application of customary law based on community membership, certainty, and justice[cite: 219, 220]. English parliamentary acts do not apply to customary land[cite: 221].
  • English Law: Includes the substance of the common law and doctrines of equity from Commonwealth countries, and Statutes of General Application in force in England on July 22, 1920, that have been expressly declared by Tanzanian courts to be part of local law[cite: 221, 222]. Courts are charged with creating a unified common law of Tanzania guided by the National Land Policy.
  • Indian Laws: Applied via section 14 of JALA (e.g., the Indian Succession Act 1865 for certain intestate successions).
  • Islamic Law: Applicable in specific civil matters (marriage, divorce, guardianship, inheritance, wakf) for members of the Islamic community pursuant to JALA[cite: 222].

Land and Property

Received English law introduced property concepts dividing chattels into real property (chattels real, historically protected by real actions and connected to land, such as leaseholds) and personal property (chattels personal, protected by personal actions for damages)[cite: 223].

Tenure and Estates

  • Tenure: Derived from tenere (to hold), tenure describes the system and social relationship under which land is held and reflects the quality of a tenant's holding[cite: 223, 224].
  • Estates: Estates measure the duration or length of time for which a tenant is entitled to hold land. Under section 2(1) of the Land Registration Act (Cap. 334), an "estate" means a right of occupancy for which a certificate of occupancy has been issued (excluding separately owned buildings or trees)[cite: 224].
  • Types of English Freehold and Leasehold Estates (Historical Context):
    • Estate in Fee Simple: The largest estate in duration, enduring infinitely and capable of inheritance without restriction.
    • Estate in Fee Tail: An estate restricted to inheritance by specified descendants of the original grantee.
    • Life Estate: An estate lasting strictly for the lifetime of the grantee (pur autre vie if measured by the life of another person).
    • Leaseholds & Reversionary Interests: Estates of fixed, certain duration created by agreement, with reversionary interests remaining in the grantor after a particular estate determines[cite: 225, 226].

Land Use and Occupation Before Colonialism

A. Primitive Communities

In early stone ages, human societies depended entirely on nature through hunting and gathering, utilizing caves for shelter with low levels of science and technology. By the late Stone Age, stone tool creation allowed for basic crop cultivation, animal keeping, and permanent settlements. The invention of iron technology marked a major turning point, enabling people to clear large tracts of land, advance agriculture, and form advanced communities. Closely related families formed clans holding collective clan land, where trespassing by outsiders was strictly barred and served as a primary source of tribal conflicts.

B. Feudal Communities

During feudalism, land was the major factor of production and a determinant of wealth. Land was largely communally owned, and individual entitlement depended on membership in a family, clan, or tribe. Chiefs and kings acted as custodians or trustees of the radical title rather than absolute owners. Unoccupied vacant land was open for acquisition through clearing virgin bushes.

Ownership of Land

Ownership implies possessing exclusive rights or bundles of rights and duties over land. Historically, land ownership has never been unlimited, being controlled by customs and legislation concerning its use, scope, and disposition. During feudalism, dominant classes (kings and feudal lords) rented land to subordinate tenants and transferred interests commercially.

Two conflicting views exist regarding customary land ownership:

  • First view: Customary law does not recognize absolute individual ownership; individuals hold merely usufructuary rights subject to community control, with land belonging ultimately to the community or state.
  • Second view: Clearing and developing vacant land vests sole ownership in the individual, granting them overriding interests and freedom of disposition and succession without community authorization.

Additionally, unallocated natural resources like forests, arid lands, and large water bodies were considered open public domains not subject to customary tenure. Because customs varied significantly among ethnic groups and pastoralists, generalizations about African land ownership can be misleading.

4. Land Law Under Colonial Administration

Colonial powers disrupted traditional African land systems due to ignorance of native tenure, translation errors regarding chief authorities, and economic demands for raw materials and minerals.

A. During German Rule

Following the Berlin Conference (1884–1885) and Anglo-German treaties, Germany colonized Tanganyika. The Imperial Decree of November 26, 1895, declared all land in Tanganyika to be crown land vested in the Empire, excepting proven private or native claims. Germans promoted plantation agriculture (such as sisal in Tanga) while recognizing customary tenure and utilizing local chiefs for indirect control. Recognized non-customary tenures included conditional freehold, leasehold, and prescription.

B. During British Rule

Following the First World War, Tanganyika became a British protectorate under a League of Nations Trusteeship Agreement. Under the Tanganyika Order in Council of 1920, the Governor enacted the Land Ordinance (Cap. 113 of 1923).

  • The Ordinance declared all land in Tanganyika, occupied or not, to be "public land" vested in the Governor to be held for the common benefit of natives.
  • The primary form of tenure was the **Right of Occupancy**, granted for terms not exceeding 99 years, requiring gubernatorial consent for disposition.
  • German-granted freeholds were converted into freehold titles upon registration.
  • Because "public land" was controlled entirely by the Governor, native customary tenure was treated as a permissive right or license, lacking true statutory protection until the Land (Amendment) Ordinance No. 7 of 1928 officially expanded the definition of a Right of Occupancy to include native customary occupation.
  • Native Authorities and Native Courts were utilized to regulate peasant production and enforce conservation by-laws.

5. Land Laws After Independence

Upon independence in 1961, the independent government retained state control over land, substituting the President for the Governor under the Land Ordinance (Cap. 113).

A. Effects of Land Laws and Policies at Independence to Non-Natives

  • Positive Effects: The government recognized the vital export contributions of the plantation sector (comprising less than 1% of land but accounting for ~40% of exports) and supported private agricultural investments.
  • Negative Effects: Guided by socialist aspirations and anti-capitalist policies spearheaded by President Julius Nyerere, the government acted against individual freehold ownership. Freehold titles were abolished and converted into 99-year government leases via the Freehold Titles (Conversion to Government Leases) Act, Cap. 523 of 1963. In 1969, Government leases were further converted into Rights of Occupancy under Act No. 44 of 1969.

B. Effects to Natives

The post-independence government sought to eliminate exploitative feudal tenures, such as the Nyarubanja system in Kagera, via the Nyarubanja Tenure (Enfranchisement) Act No. 1 of 1965 and the Customary Leaseholds (Enfranchisement) Act. Under the First Five-Year Plan (1964–1969) and the Land Tenure (Village Settlement) Act, the state pursued transformation and villagization approaches, abolishing traditional chieftainships (African Chiefs Ordinance (Repeal) Act No. 13 of 1963) and forcefully grouping populations into villages (Villages and Ujamaa Villages Act No. 21 of 1975). These mass villagization programs (Operation Vijiji) severely disrupted customary tenure and traditional land administration.

6. Protection of Customary Tenure: Judicial Articulation

The tension between granted rights of occupancy and customary rights of occupancy persisted post-independence. While administrative policies and certain court rulings (such as *Mwalimu Omari & Another v. Omari Bilali*, holding that customary law cannot apply in urban areas due to compulsory registration requirements) subordinated customary tenure, judicial activism in other landmark cases sought to protect customary rights.

  • In National Agricultural and Food Corporation v. Mulbadaw Village Council, the Court of Appeal indicated that holders of deemed rights of occupancy enjoy equal status and protection and can only be evicted under the Land Acquisition Act.
  • In Methuselah Paul Nyagwaswa v. Christopher Mbote Nyirabu, the court noted via obiter dicta that customary tenure is not automatically extinguished when an area is declared a planning area.
  • In Yoke Gwaku v. NAFCO, evictions of Barbaig pastoralists without compliance with the Land Acquisition Act were declared void.
  • When the government enacted the draconian Regulation of Land Tenure (Established Villages) Act No. 22 of 1992 to extinguish customary claims arising from villagization without compensation, the Court of Appeal struck down its derogative sections as unconstitutional in *Attorney General v. Lohay Akonaay and Joseph Lohay*.

Topic 2: Classification and Administration of Land in Tanzania

Following the historical evolution of land tenure from pre-colonial systems through colonial administration to post-independence reforms, modern Tanzanian land law is structured around a distinct categorization and administrative framework. This chapter examines how land is classified, managed, and allocated under the current statutory regime, primarily governed by the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002) and the Village Land Act (Cap. 114 R.E. 2002).

1. Categorization of Land Under Tanzanian Law

Under Section 4 of the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002), the entirety of land in mainland Tanzania is public land and is vested in the President as trustee for and on behalf of all citizens. To ensure efficient administration, management, and dispute settlement, Section 4 classifies all land into three distinct categories:

  • General Land: This comprises all public land that is neither village land nor reserved land. It includes urban areas, trading centers, and unallocated public lands. General land is governed directly by the Commissioner for Lands under the provisions of the Land Act.
  • Village Land: Defined under the Village Land Act (Cap. 114 R.E. 2002), village land comprises all land inside the boundaries of registered villages. Village councils and village assemblies manage and administer this land, empowering local communities to allocate customary rights of occupancy.
  • Reserved Land: This category includes land set aside for special national, environmental, or public purposes under specific statutory regimes. It encompasses forest reserves, national parks, game reserves, wildlife management areas, land reserved for public utilities, infrastructure, and historical or archaeological sites.

2. Transfer and Conversion of Land Categories

The statutory framework permits the conversion of land from one category to another to accommodate urban expansion, developmental projects, or community needs, subject to strict legal procedures and safeguards:

  • Transfer from Village Land to General or Reserved Land: Under Section 4 of the Village Land Act, the President may transfer village land to general or reserved land for public interest purposes. This process requires consulting the village council, publishing a notice in the Gazette, and paying prompt, fair, and adequate compensation to affected villagers.
  • Transfer from General Land to Village Land: The Minister responsible for lands may declare general land to be village land, particularly when expanding village boundaries or formalizing settlements.
  • Transfer to Reserved Land: Both general and village land can be gazetted as reserved land for conservation or infrastructural projects, provided statutory prerequisites are meticulously observed.

3. Tenurial Systems in Tanzania

Land tenure refers to the terms and conditions under which land is held, used, and transferred. Tanzanian land law recognizes two primary forms of tenure:

  • The Right of Occupancy: The fundamental title to land in Tanzania is the Right of Occupancy, which may be granted or customary. It confers secure occupation and use rights while retaining the radical title in the state.
    • Granted Right of Occupancy: Granted by the President (through the Commissioner for Lands) over general or reserved land for a specified term (typically up to 99 years), subject to development conditions, rent payments, and covenants.
    • Customary Right of Occupancy: Held by communities or individuals under customary law, largely within village land. It is governed by the Village Land Act and enjoys equal status and protection in law compared to granted rights.
  • Derivative Rights: Rights created out of a granted or customary right of occupancy, such as sub-leases, residential licenses, and mortgages, enabling flexible utilization of land assets.

4. Institutional Framework for Land Administration

The administration of land involves several key statutory authorities operating at national, regional, and local levels:

  • The Commissioner for Lands: Responsible for the day-to-day administration of general land, granting rights of occupancy, managing land records, and collecting land rent.
  • The National Land Advisory Council: Advises the Minister on policy matters, land use planning, and strategic disputes.
  • Village Councils and Assemblies: Act as managers of village land, issuing Certificates of Customary Occupancy (CCOs) and resolving minor local disputes.
  • Land Courts Structure: Established under the Courts (Land Disputes Settlement) Act No. 2 of 2002, comprising Village Land Councils, Ward Tribunals, District Land and Housing Tribunals, the High Court (Land Division), and the Court of Appeal as the final appellate tier.

Topic 3: Land Disposition & Mortgages

Building upon the classification and administration of land outlined in Chapter 2, Chapter 3 examines the primary landholding title under Tanzanian statutory law: the Right of Occupancy. This chapter explores the nature, creation, terms, and legal incidents of both Granted Rights of Occupancy and Customary Rights of Occupancy, as well as the rules governing disposition, derivative rights, and encumbrances.

1. Nature and Types of Rights of Occupancy

Under Section 2 of the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002) and the Village Land Act (Cap. 114 R.E. 2002), the Right of Occupancy is the sole statutory form of title recognizing exclusive use and occupation of public land. Tanzanian law recognizes two fundamental forms of this right:

A. Granted Right of Occupancy

A Granted Right of Occupancy is an express title over General or Reserved Land granted directly by the President through the Commissioner for Lands under Part VI of the Land Act. Key characteristics include:

  • Term Limit: Granted for a specific duration not exceeding 99 years.
  • Statutory Instrument: Evidence of title is issued in the form of a Certificate of Title registered under the Land Registration Act (Cap. 334).
  • Development Conditions: Subject to mandatory statutory implied and express covenants, including the duty to pay land rent, comply with land-use planning rules, and build or improve the land within prescribed timelines.
  • Eligibility: Available to individual Tanzanian citizens, corporate bodies, non-citizens (solely for investment purposes under the Tanzania Investment Act), and joint ventures.

B. Customary Right of Occupancy

A Customary Right of Occupancy is held under customary law or deemed customary tenure within Village Land under the Village Land Act (Cap. 114). Key features include:

  • Status and Equality: Section 18 of the Village Land Act explicitly declares that a Customary Right of Occupancy is equal in status and effect to a Granted Right of Occupancy.
  • Duration: May be granted for an indefinite period or a specified term.
  • Documentation: Evidenced by a Certificate of Customary Right of Occupancy (CCRO) issued by the relevant Village Council and registered at the District Land Registry.
  • Regulation: Managed according to local customary rules, provided those rules do not violate statutory provisions or constitutional protections against gender discrimination.

2. Disposition of Land Interests

Disposition encompasses any transaction intended to transfer, create, alter, or terminate an interest in land, including sales, assignments, leases, mortgages, and gifts. Tanzanian land law imposes strict rules and procedures to prevent land speculation and ensure regulatory compliance.

A. Legal Requirements and Regulatory Consent

  • Notification and Approval: Dispositions of Granted Rights of Occupancy must be notified to or approved by the Commissioner for Lands under Section 36 of the Land Act. Dispositions involving village land require the consent of the Village Council or Village Assembly depending on the nature and size of the allocation.
  • Pre-conditions: Unimproved land held under a Granted Right of Occupancy generally cannot be sold or transferred without demonstrating compliance with initial development requirements and obtaining clearance from the Commissioner.
  • Spousal Protection: Section 161 of the Land Act and corresponding provisions mandate spousal consent for any disposition involving matrimonial land or home. Dispositions executed without such consent are voidable at the instance of the non-consenting spouse.

3. Derivative Rights and Encumbrances

Apart from direct ownership under a Right of Occupancy, land rights may be carved out as secondary or derivative interests:

  • Leases and Sub-leases: An occupier may grant a lease over the whole or part of their land for a duration shorter than their own term. The Land Act sets out implied covenants for lessors and lessees regarding maintenance, quiet enjoyment, and rent payment.
  • Mortgages: Section 112 of the Land Act governs mortgages, enabling occupiers to encumber their title as security for financial loans. The statutory regime outlines remedies for mortgagees (e.g., appointment of a receiver, taking possession, power of sale) while protecting mortgagors against equity-of-redemption forfeitures.
  • Easements and Covenants: Rights enjoyed by one landowner over the neighboring land of another (e.g., rights of way, drainage, or light) are regulated as statutory easements under Part X of the Land Act.

4. Revocation and Termination of Rights of Occupancy

A Right of Occupancy may be terminated through expiration of term, surrender, or compulsory revocation by the President:

  • Revocation for Breach of Conditions: The President may revoke a Granted Right of Occupancy under Section 45 of the Land Act if the occupier breaches development covenants, fails to pay land rent, or abandons the land. Prior notice to remedy the breach must be served on the occupier.
  • Compulsory Acquisition for Public Interest: Land may be acquired by the state for public interest under the Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 118). Such acquisition mandates prompt, fair, and full compensation covering market value, disturbance, transport, loss of profit, and unexhausted improvements.

Topic 4: co-occupancy

Introduction

A person may hold a right of occupancy individually (in severalty) or concurrently with one or more persons.

Holding Severalty vs. Concurrent Holding

  • Holding in Severalty: Refers to individual ownership where a single person holds the entire right of occupancy.
  • Holding Concurrently: Refers to a situation where two or more persons have simultaneous interests in land. For example, a grant to A and B creates concurrent interests, whereas a grant to A for life with a remainder to B creates consecutive, not concurrent, interests.

Under Section 159(1) of the Land Act, Cap. 113, co-occupancy is defined as the occupation of land held under a right of occupancy or a lease by two or more persons in undivided shares, taking the form of either a joint occupancy or an occupancy in common.

The nature of the co-occupation must be expressly indicated in the instrument or document creating the interest (Section 159(3)(a)). If it is an occupancy in common, the instrument must specify the fractional share of each occupier (e.g., 1/2, 1/3) (Section 159(3)(b)). Conversely, under a joint occupancy, no separate individual shares exist, and the instrument cannot state fractional shares.

Incidents and Features of Joint Occupancy

Under a joint occupancy, two or more persons are treated in law as a single owner. The entire estate or interest in land is vested simultaneously in all co-owners, with each possessing the whole property concurrently. As between themselves, joint occupiers possess distinct equitable rights, but as against third parties, they constitute a single composite owner.

Principal Features of Joint Occupancy

  • The Right of Survivorship (Jus Accrescendi): The defining feature of joint occupancy. Upon the death of one joint occupier, their interest automatically extinguishes and passes to the surviving joint occupiers. This process continues until a single survivor holds the land as sole owner. Jus accrescendi takes precedence over any testamentary disposition (will) or rules of intestacy. Under Section 69(1) of the Land Registration Act, Cap. 334, upon proof of death, the Registrar will delete the deceased joint owner's name from the land register.
  • Consent and Transfer Restrictions: All joint occupiers must consent to any disposition or transfer of the land (Section 157(4)(a) of the Land Act). A joint occupier can only transfer their interest inter vivos to fellow joint occupiers; they cannot transfer to a third party unless the joint occupancy has first been legally severed.
  • Severance: Joint occupiers may agree to sever the joint occupancy into an occupancy in common by executing a formal instrument of severance (e.g., Form 61), which must be registered to take legal effect (Section 159(7)).
  • Statutory Presumption: Under Section 159(8) of the Land Act, any joint occupancy created between persons who are not spouses requires the leave of court; otherwise, it operates by law as an occupancy in common.

The Four Unities of Joint Occupancy

For a valid joint occupancy to exist, the "four unities" must be present simultaneously:

  1. Unity of Possession: Each joint occupier is equally entitled to physical possession of the entire property. No co-owner can claim exclusive possession of any specific part of the land (citing Bull v. Bull [1955] 1 QB 234).
  2. Unity of Interest: The interest of each co-owner must be identical in nature, extent, and duration (e.g., sharing rent and profits equally).
  3. Unity of Title: All joint occupiers must derive their title from the same document, instrument, or certificate of title (Section 159(3)(a) and Section 160).
  4. Unity of Time: The interest of each joint occupier must vest at the exact same time.

Additionally, joint occupancy is characterized by a total lack of words of severance (i.e., no expressions indicating separate shares such as "in equal shares", "to be divided between", or fractional allocations).

Incidents and Features of Occupancy in Common

Occupancy in common differs significantly from joint occupancy. Each occupier holds a distinct, undivided fractional share of the whole property (Section 159(5) of the Land Act). Key incidents include:

  • Transmission on Death: Upon the death of an occupier in common, their undivided share forms part of their estate and passes under a will or the rules of intestacy (Section 159(5)). There is no right of survivorship.
  • Dealings with Shares: An occupier in common may deal with their undivided share (transfer, mortgage, or lease) independently, though restrictions apply regarding dealings with non-co-occupiers unless proper consent is obtained in writing or freely given orally without undue influence (Section 159(6)).
  • Unity Required: Unlike joint occupancy, the only unity essential for an occupancy in common is the unity of possession. Shares may be unequal, and if the instrument does not specify proportions, a rebuttable legal presumption of equal shares applies.
  • Certificate of Occupancy Copies: Under Section 160 of the Land Act, each co-occupier is entitled to apply for and receive a separate copy of the certificate of occupancy (using Form 64), which the Registrar records in the register. Such a copy can be used to execute a transfer, mortgage, or lien over the holder's undivided share, after which the Registrar retains and cancels or destroys the copy upon completion.

Co-Occupancy and Spousal Relationships

To protect family homes and vulnerable dependents, Section 161 of the Land Act, Cap. 113 establishes robust protections:

  • Statutory Presumption of Tenancy in Common: Where a right of occupancy is obtained for occupation by spouses, or where a grantee has more than one wife, there is a rebuttable presumption that the land is held as an occupancy in common. If sole ownership is intended, it must be expressly stated at grant and endorsed on the certificate of occupancy (Section 161(1)).
  • Spousal Contribution: Even if a certificate of occupancy is registered in the name of one spouse only, if another spouse has contributed labor or resources to the upkeep or improvement of the land, they are deemed to have acquired an equitable interest equivalent to an occupancy in common (Section 161(2)).
  • Consent Requirements: Mortgagees, lenders, and purchasers acquiring an interest in a matrimonial home or family land must ensure that all spouses have given free and informed consent. Any disposition executed through intentional misrepresentation or without required spousal consent is voidable at the option of the non-consenting spouse (Section 161(3); citing Omary Mohamed v. Awadh Abdallah [1992] TLR 35).

Determination and Termination of Co-Occupancy

Co-occupancy (whether joint or in common) may be determined or terminated through several legal mechanisms:

Methods of Termination

  • Conversion and Severance: Converting a joint occupancy into an occupancy in common via mutual agreement and registered instrument (Section 159(7)).
  • Alienation or Acquisition: Transferring one co-owner's interest to a third party, or the acquisition of a larger or disparate interest that destroys the unities.
  • Partition: Splitting the physical land among co-occupiers. Under Section 162 of the Land Act, an occupier in common may apply to the Registrar (using Forms 62, 63, or 64) for a partition order. The Registrar evaluates specific statutory factors under Section 162(3), including:
    • Compliance with subdivision laws and planning covenants;
    • Nature and location of the land;
    • Number of co-occupiers and size of shares;
    • Value of improvements and financial contributions made;
    • Protection of spouses and dependents from homelessness;
    • Protection of traditional grazing rights for pastoralist communities; and
    • Avoidance of undue hardship.
  • Sale in Lieu of Partition: Under Section 164 of the Land Act, if physical partition is impracticable, would adversely affect land use, or if co-owners request it, the court or Registrar may order the sale of the land (by public auction or private treaty) and distribute the proceeds proportionally. Any co-owner may bid and purchase at such sale (citing G.V. Patel v. D.M. Patel [1939] 6 EACA 48).
  • Reorganization or Winding Up: Section 166 of the Land Act provides mechanisms for the reorganization or winding up of land-sharing schemes operating under co-occupancy frameworks.

Topic 5: land registration

Introduction

Land registration in Tanzania operates primarily under two distinct systems:

  1. Registration of Documents (Deeds Registration): Governed by the Registration of Documents Act, Cap. 117.
  2. Registration of Title: Governed by the Land Registration Act, Cap. 334, and the Land Act, Cap. 113.

Registration of Documents (Deeds Registration)

Under a deeds registration system, it is the transactional document (deed) that is registered rather than the underlying title itself. To establish a valid dealing, an intending purchaser must trace ownership back to a good root of title through a sequence of historical recorded events and transactions, each of which must be independently proved.

Key Characteristics of Deeds Registration

  • Isolated Transactions: Each document serves merely as evidence that a specific transaction took place between parties.
  • Lack of State Guarantee: Documents presented for registration are recorded at face value; the Registrar does not examine them for legal correctness or verify the validity of the underlying title (Section 8, Cap. 117).
  • No Folio System: Unlike title registration, deeds registration does not allocate a separate folio in the register to each discrete parcel of land.
  • Evidentiary Role: The document proves the title, not the register itself.

Under Section 4 of the Registration of Documents Act, Cap. 117, the registry maintains three primary records: the Register of Compulsory Documents, the Register of Optional Documents, and the Presentation Book.

Manner and Procedure in Registration of Documents

Pursuant to Sections 14 and 15 of Cap. 117, documents may be presented for registration by:

  • Any party executing or claiming an interest under the document, or their duly appointed attorney;
  • An advocate of the High Court;
  • A guardian, next friend, or court-appointed representative acting for a minor or a person of unsound mind; or
  • Postal submission or under cover, provided the appropriate statutory fee and a formal request letter signed by an authorized presenter are included.

Registration of Titles

Regulated by the Land Registration Act, Cap. 334, title registration treats registration not as a one-off event, but as an ongoing legal process. Under Section 2 of Cap. 334, a "register" means the inscription in the land register recording title to any estate or disposition thereof, and an "owner" is defined as the person in whose name the estate is registered (citing Salum Mateyo v. Mohamed Mateyo [1987] TLR 111).

Registrable Estates and Exclusions (Section 8, Cap. 334)

Registrable estates include leases and statutory rights of occupancy, but explicitly exclude:

  • Leases for an unexpired term of five (5) years or less (unless containing an option to renew extending the total beyond 5 years);
  • Periodic tenancies (year-to-year or less), unless the initial fixed term exceeds four (4) years; and
  • Rights of occupancy prior to formal first registration (though rights of occupancy form the substantive basis of granted land titles under the Land Act).

First Registration Procedure

  • Application: Any person claiming a registrable estate in unregistered land applies to the Registrar, submitting all underlying title documents (Section 10).
  • Public Advertisement: The Registrar advertises the application in the Gazette and local newspapers at the applicant's expense, and issues notices to adjoining landowners (Section 12).
  • Objections: Interested parties may file written objections within the statutory notice period (Section 13). The Registrar investigates the root of title, has absolute discretion to admit evidence, and must not finalize registration until at least two (2) months have elapsed from the Gazette advertisement.
  • Completion: Upon being satisfied, the Registrar opens a folio divided into three parts (property description, ownership particulars, and encumbrances) pursuant to Section 30.

Village Land Registration

Under the Village Land Act, Cap. 114 and Regulation 36 of the Village Land Regulations, 2001 (G.N. No. 86 of 2001), village authorities maintain a Village Land Register divided into three parts:

  • Part A: Certificate of Village Land
  • Part B: Title Register
  • Part C: Public Charges Register

Goals and Objectives of Land Registration

Land registration serves several vital socioeconomic and administrative functions:

  • Security of Tenure: Eliminates uncertainty by issuing certificates of title, granting indefeasible ownership rights, protecting purchasers and lenders, and providing state-backed indemnity against title defects (Section 33, Cap. 334).
  • Minimizing Litigation: Precise boundary surveys and conclusive registers reduce boundary disputes and eliminate the need for historical title investigations.
  • Preventing Fragmentation: State oversight via planning laws and statutory consent requirements prevents excessive subdivision and uneconomic land holdings.
  • Facilitating Tax Administration: Enables the government to identify property owners for accurate assessment and collection of land rent, capital gains tax, and property rates.
  • Supporting Credit and Loan Systems: Secure, registered titles provide financial institutions with reliable collateral, facilitating mortgage lending and capital mobilization.
  • Determining Squatter Rights: Provides a legal mechanism to regularize informal settlements and integrate squatters into formal development schemes through long-term occupancy rights.
  • Public Planning and Statistics: Furnishes reliable baseline data for physical planning, infrastructure development, and demographic management.

Effects of Registration and Non-Registration

Priority and Mandatory Interests

Registered interests and documents take priority according to the chronological order in which they were presented to the registry, rather than the date of execution (Section 60(1), Cap. 334; Section 10, Cap. 117). Mandatory registrable interests include granted rights of occupancy, transfers, mortgages, charges, and long-term leases (Land Act sections 61–63; Cap. 334 sections 29 and 57).

Non-Registrable Interests

Certain interests—such as equitable mortgages created by the deposit of title documents, licenses, agreements to lease, and short-term leases—cannot be directly registered as legal estates (Section 55, Cap. 334). However, they can be protected through equitable memoranda or by lodging a caveat (Section 78, Cap. 334).

Consequences of Non-Registration

Under Section 9 of Cap. 117 and Section 41 of Cap. 334, failure to register a compulsorily registrable instrument has severe legal consequences:

  • The instrument is ineffectual to pass any legal estate or create an interest valid against third parties;
  • The document cannot be received as evidence of any dealing affecting the land in any court of law; and
  • As between the immediate parties, the unregistered instrument operates only as an executory contract creating personal rights and liabilities that may support a claim for specific performance, but it does not encumber the land *in rem*.

Topic 6: Land Dispute Settlement Framework in Tanzania

This chapter will focuses on the legal mechanism for resolving land disputes in mainland Tanzania. Governed primarily by the Courts (Land Disputes Settlement) Act, Cap. 216 R.E. 2002, this statutory framework establishes a specialized, tiered machinery designed to provide accessible, expeditious, and fair adjudication of land-related conflicts.

1. Fundamental Principles and Statutory Scope

Prior to the enactment of Act No. 2 of 2002 (Cap. 216), land disputes were handled across various ordinary courts, local tribunals, and administrative bodies, resulting in jurisdictional overlaps and systemic delays. Cap. 216 streamlined land dispute resolution by establishing an exclusive hierarchy of specialized tribunals and courts dedicated solely to land matters.

  • Exclusive Jurisdiction: Section 3 of Cap. 216 grants exclusive jurisdiction over all land disputes to the statutory bodies established under the Act. No ordinary court has jurisdiction to entertain land disputes unless expressly authorized by statute.
  • Hierarchy of Dispute Bodies: The statutory machinery operates on a five-tier hierarchy ascending from the local community level to the highest appellate court:
    1. The Village Land Council (Baraza la Ardhi la Kijiji)
    2. The Ward Tribunal (Baraza la Kata)
    3. The District Land and Housing Tribunal (DLHT)
    4. The High Court of Tanzania (Land Division)
    5. The Court of Appeal of Tanzania

2. Powers and Operation of Land Dispute Machinery

A. The Village Land Council

Established under Section 60 of the Village Land Act (Cap. 114) and recognized under Cap. 216, the Village Land Council operates at the grass-root level. Its primary function is mediation and reconciliation rather than strict judicial determination. It assists parties in reaching an amicable settlement regarding customary village land disputes without imposing formal court orders or legal penalties.

B. The Ward Tribunal

Operating at the ward level, the Ward Tribunal functions as both a conciliatory body and a primary adjudicative organ for minor land claims. It has jurisdiction over land matters within its ward up to a prescribed pecuniary limit. Appeals from the Village Land Council or unresolved mediations are referred to the Ward Tribunal, which can issue binding orders, subject to appeal.

C. The District Land and Housing Tribunal (DLHT)

The DLHT is a statutory tribunal of record established for each district or specified geographical zone. Governed by a Chairman who is a legally qualified advocate or magistrate, the DLHT exercises original jurisdiction over real property disputes, eviction proceedings, boundary disputes, and housing/tenancy matters exceeding the pecuniary limits of Ward Tribunals. Proceedings are conducted with assessors present to advise on local customs and facts.

D. The High Court (Land Division)

Established as a specialized division of the High Court of Tanzania, the Land Division exercises both original and appellate jurisdiction:

  • Original Jurisdiction: Adjudicates high-value land disputes, complex commercial property matters, land acquisition claims, public interest litigation, and disputes involving Granted Rights of Occupancy exceeding the pecuniary jurisdiction of the DLHT.
  • Appellate and Supervisory Jurisdiction: Hears appeals arising directly from decisions of the District Land and Housing Tribunals and exercises supervisory control via judicial review over lower land bodies.

E. The Court of Appeal of Tanzania

As the final judicial authority in the country, the Court of Appeal hears appeals from the High Court (Land Division) on points of law. Decisions rendered by the Court of Appeal establish binding judicial precedents for the interpretation of land statutes, constitutional property rights, and customary tenure principles.

3. Key Procedural and Remedial Aspects

  • Limitation of Actions: Land actions are subject to strict statutory time limits under the Law of Limitation Act (Cap. 89), requiring actions for the recovery of land to be instituted within 12 years from the date the cause of action accrued.
  • Execution of Orders: Decrees and orders issued by the DLHT and the High Court are enforceable through statutory execution procedures, including eviction orders, vacant possession, injunctions, and registration of decrees against land titles.
  • Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR): Emphasized throughout all tiers, ADR mechanisms (such as mediation and negotiation) must be attempted before formal trial proceedings commence in both the DLHT and the High Court.

Topic 7: land acquisition and land compensation in Tanzania

Introduction

This chapter examines the legal framework and principles governing compulsory land acquisition and land compensation in Tanzania. Since the colonial era, land has been subject to acquisition under various legislative instruments, frequently justified under the guise of public purpose or public interest.

The introduction of 19th-century imperial rule in Africa established legal doctrines regarding land ownership, utilizing terms such as crown lands, native lands, and public lands. While German administrators designated lands as crown lands, the British administration favored the term public lands. Scholars such as Van Rees noted that the designation of "public land" did not signify state ownership; rather, it established an administrative relationship whereby land was placed under the control of the Governor (and subsequently the President) in trust on behalf of all citizens.

Placing all public lands under the disposition of the executive authority and requiring official consent for land validity meant that indigenous occupiers lacked absolute security of tenure against state intervention. Retaining similar concepts in post-independence legal frameworks has enabled governments to acquire land expediently for national development projects.

The National Land Policy (1995) explicitly acknowledges that statutory powers enabling the President to acquire land for public purposes or urban redevelopment are essential for national development[cite: 486]. Policy Statement 16 outlines key principles: the President's acquisition powers must be retained with clearly defined statutory grounds for "public interest"; acquisition processes must be subject to judicial review; and compensation must be determined based on the principle of opportunity cost.

The Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 118 R.E. 2002)

The Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 118 R.E. 2002) provides the primary statutory mechanism and procedure for compulsorily acquiring land in Tanzania Mainland. Under Section 3 of the Act, the President is empowered to acquire any land for any term when required for a public purpose.

"Public purpose" is broadly defined under the statute to encompass:

  • Exclusive government use, general administrative use, or government housing and social services.
  • Sanitary improvements, land reclamation, and urban planning schemes.
  • Development of airfields, ports, harbors, and public utilities.
  • Mining operations for minerals or oil.
  • Agricultural development projects approved by the President[cite: 487].

Where land is required for a corporation, Section 4(2) requires a presidential declaration supported by a resolution of the National Assembly and published in the Official Gazette. Section 5 empowers authorized officers to enter land upon a three-day notice to conduct preliminary examinations and valuations.

Procedure Prior to Acquisition

Compulsory acquisition requires strict adherence to statutory notice procedures under Sections 6, 7, and 8 of the Land Acquisition Act. The Minister must serve formal notices of intention to acquire land to all interested parties, specifying a minimum notice period of six weeks, which may be shortened in cases of certified urgency.

Service of notice may be effected personally, left at the usual place of business or abode, or affixed to a conspicuous part of the land if no occupier can be located[cite: 488, 489]. Crucially, publication of cuspis acquisition notice in the Official Gazette serves as a curative mechanism for procedural irregularities in personal service, whereas failure to publish in the Gazette invalidates the acquisition process.

In National Bank of Commerce v. Suleiman Nassor Ally [1989] TLR 67, the Court of Appeal confirmed that compulsory acquisition under the Act is complete upon the expiration of six weeks following the publication of the acquisition notice in the Gazette, independent of subsequent land registry documentation formalities.

Compensation of Land Rights

Article 24 of the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (1977) guarantees every person the right to own property and receive protection under the law, explicitly prohibiting the deprivation of property without fair and prompt compensation. Case law consistently reinforces that unlawful or arbitrary dispossession without adequate compensation violates fundamental legal principles.

Judicial precedent establishes distinct rules regarding trespassers and lawful occupants. In Ramadhani Kambi Mkinga v. Ramadhani Saidi [1985] TLR 140, the court held that a party who knowingly enters land as a trespasser despite prompt protests is not entitled to compensation for improvements. Conversely, where occupants act under apparent color of right, they are protected against arbitrary state seizure without adequate recompense, as affirmed in Ntiyahela Boneka v. Kijiji Cha Ujamaa Mutala [1988] TLR 156.

A landmark shift occurred in Attorney General v. Lohay Akonaay and Joseph Lohay [1995] TLR 80, where the Court of Appeal ruled that customary or deemed rights of occupancy constitute real property protected under Article 24 of the Constitution. The court established that fair compensation is not restricted solely to unexhausted improvements but extends to labor and effort expended by occupiers in clearing and developing land.

The Current Position on Compensation

The National Land Policy (1995) and the Land Act (Cap. 113 R.E. 2002) recognize that land possesses intrinsic economic value driven by scarcity and market demand. Section 3(1)(g) of the Land Act mandates the payment of full, fair, and prompt compensation to any person whose right of occupancy or recognized long-standing customary tenure is revoked or interfered with.

Under Section 14 of the Land Acquisition Act and subsidiary regulations, compensation assessments must incorporate comprehensive valuation elements rather than mere construction costs. The assessment framework established under the Land (Assessment of Value of Land for Compensation) Regulations (G.N. No. 78 of 2001) and Land (Compensation Claims) Regulations (G.N. No. 79 of 2001) includes:

  • Market value of the real property based on recent comparable sales, income approach, or replacement cost.
  • Disturbance allowance, calculated using commercial bank fixed deposit interest rates over 12 months.
  • Transport allowance, covering actual costs of transporting 12 tons of luggage within a 20-kilometer radius.
  • Accommodation allowance, determined by multiplying monthly market rent by 36 months.
  • Loss of profits, calculated using audited net monthly business profits multiplied by 36 months.

Prompt payment is statutorily defined as settlement within six months of land acquisition. Where the acquiring authority fails to pay compensation within this period, commercial interest rates accrue on the outstanding sum until full settlement is achieved.

Topic 9: Settlement of Land Disputes

Introduction

From the discussions across all preceding chapters, numerous circumstances arise that entitle an aggrieved party to seek redress before a judicial or quasi-judicial body. This chapter surveys the land dispute settlement machinery and the jurisdiction of land courts in Mainland Tanzania.

Land is a fundamental resource and a primary factor of production, making it a frequent arena of protracted disputes. In African societies, land is not merely an economic asset; it is a symbol of wealth, identity, and social cohesion—binding lineage members together through clan, family, and communal holdings, and serving as a sacred site of birth and burial. Economically, because all industrial, agricultural, and commercial developments take place on land, increasing scarcity intensifies competition and triggers conflicts between:

  • Investors and local land occupiers;
  • Wealthy landowners and smallholder peasants;
  • Farmers and pastoralists;
  • Neighboring communities; and
  • Private individuals.

Politically, land denotes territoriality and facilitates authority. During the German and British colonial eras, economic control and political sovereignty were merged to consolidate power over colonial subjects. Similarly, in pre-colonial societies, chiefs exercised land allocation powers that reinforced political allegiance.

Because competing interests over land are inevitable, an accessible, independent, and expeditious dispute settlement system is essential for sustainable land management. In 1992, the Presidential Commission of Inquiry into Land Matters (the Shivji Commission) documented a massive backlog of land cases and recommended specialized Circuit Land Courts integrated within the judiciary alongside public participation. Subsequently, the National Land Policy (1995) observed that ordinary courts were overburdened with general civil and criminal matters and devoted insufficient time to land disputes, calling for a dedicated land adjudication structure and the strengthening of existing quasi-judicial bodies.

The enactment of the Land Act, Cap. 113 and the Village Land Act, Cap. 114 in 1999 laid the statutory foundation for this specialized hierarchy. Section 167 of the Land Act (and Section 62 of the Village Land Act) established the framework of courts vested with jurisdiction to hear and determine land disputes:

  1. The Court of Appeal of Tanzania;
  2. The High Court (historically the Land Division, now exercised by the High Court and its registries);
  3. The District Land and Housing Tribunal (DLHT);
  4. Ward Tribunals; and
  5. Village Land Councils.

Because the 1999 Acts only named these institutions without detailing their composition, procedures, or pecuniary limits, Parliament enacted the Land Disputes Courts Act, No. 2 of 2002 (Cap. 216) (originally titled the Courts (Land Disputes Settlements) Act) to operationalize the land court system.

1. Applicable Laws in Land Courts

Section 180(1) of the Land Act, Cap. 113 prescribes the hierarchy of laws that courts must apply when interpreting the Land Acts and adjudicating land disputes in Mainland Tanzania, subject to the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania, 1977:

  • Customary Laws of Tanzania: Applied to disputes involving customary tenure, provided such rules are not repugnant to natural justice, statutory law, or constitutional guarantees of equality (particularly the rights of women, children, and persons with disabilities under Section 20(2) of the Village Land Act).
  • Common Law and Doctrines of Equity: The substance of the common law and the doctrines of equity as applied from time to time in Commonwealth countries, insofar as they are relevant to the circumstances of Tanzania.

Under Section 180(3) of the Land Act, courts are mandated to use their best endeavors to develop a common law of Tanzania applicable in equal measure to all land, employing a purposive interpretation guided by the fundamental principles of the National Land Policy (Section 3 of the Land Act). Furthermore, under Section 180(2), English Statutes of General Application in force on 22 July 1920 that had not been declared part of Tanzanian law prior to the commencement of the Land Act cannot be applied to any land matter. Under Section 181, the Land Act prevails over any other written law in the event of conflict or inconsistency regarding land.

The Land Disputes Courts Act, Cap. 216

The Land Disputes Courts Act, Cap. 216 is divided into eight parts governing the establishment, composition, jurisdiction, and procedures of land dispute settlement organs. Under Section 2 of the Act, a "dispute" includes any case where a person complains of and is aggrieved by the actions of another person, or any case in which a complaint is made in an official capacity or against an official act.

The Village Land Council

Established under Section 60 of the Village Land Act, Cap. 114 and Section 5 of the Land Disputes Courts Act, Cap. 216, the Village Land Council is a community-based mediation forum—not a formal court of adjudication—tasked with assisting parties to reach a mutually acceptable settlement on matters concerning village land.

Composition, Qualifications, and Quorum

  • Membership: Consists of seven (7) members nominated by the Village Council and approved by the Village Assembly, of whom at least three (3) must be women (Section 60(2), Village Land Act).
  • Qualifications (Section 60(5)): A nominee must be ordinarily resident in the village, a citizen of Tanzania, at least 18 years of age, of sound mind, not a Member of Parliament, not a Magistrate having jurisdiction in that district, and not convicted of a criminal offence involving dishonesty or moral turpitude.
  • Tenure and Quorum: Members hold office for a term of three (3) years and are eligible for re-appointment (Section 60(7)). The quorum for any meeting is four (4) members, of whom at least two (2) must be women (Section 60(9)). In the event of an equality of votes, the Chairperson exercises a casting vote (Section 60(10)).

Functions and Mediation Procedure

Under Section 7 of Cap. 216, the functions of the Village Land Council are to receive complaints, convene meetings for hearing disputes, and mediate between parties. Under Section 61(4) of the Village Land Act, mediation is guided by customary principles of mediation, principles of natural justice, and mediation training received by members.

Additionally, under Regulations 77 and 78 of the Village Land Regulations, 2001 (G.N. No. 86 of 2001), the Council must:

  • Observe objectivity, fairness, and justice, paying special regard to the land rights of women, children, and persons with disabilities;
  • Conduct hearings publicly in an informal atmosphere, administering substantive justice without undue regard to technicalities;
  • Exclude any member who has a personal interest in the proceedings;
  • Allow parties to appear in person or by representative (excluding advocates, who are barred from appearing before the Council);
  • Inspect the disputed land where necessary, accepting customary boundary evidence such as natural features, trees, footpaths, cattle trails, and community testimony;
  • Formulate and explain proposed terms of settlement to the parties; and
  • Maintain written records and prepare a signed report containing party details, evidence summaries, findings, and recommendations.

Because the Council's role is strictly mediatory, it cannot impose a binding judgment. Where parties do not accept the outcome of mediation, either party may refer the dispute to the Ward Tribunal under Section 62 of the Village Land Act and Section 9 of Cap. 216.

The Ward Tribunal

Ward Tribunals, originally established under the Ward Tribunals Act, Cap. 206, are designated as courts for the settlement of land disputes under Section 10 of the Land Disputes Courts Act, Cap. 216. Each tribunal exercises jurisdiction within the area of the Ward for which it is established.

Composition and Quorum

Under Section 11 of Cap. 216, a Ward Tribunal consists of not less than four (4) and not more than eight (8) members elected by the Ward Committee, of whom at least three (3) must be women. When mediating or adjudicating a land dispute, the Tribunal is duly constituted by three (3) members, at least one (1) of whom must be a woman (Section 14(1)). In the event of an equality of votes, the presiding member has a casting vote (Section 14(2)).

Functions, Jurisdiction, and Powers

  • Dual Mandate (Section 13): The primary function of the Ward Tribunal is to secure peace and harmony by mediating between parties to achieve an amicable settlement. However, as affirmed by the High Court in John Mtawali v. Tatu Said Kuumba (Misc. Land Application No. 19 of 2006, DSM), where mediation fails under Section 14(3), the Tribunal must proceed to hear evidence and deliver a binding judicial determination.
  • Pecuniary Jurisdiction (Section 15): Limited to civil land matters where the value of the subject matter does not exceed TZS 3,000,000.
  • Remedial Powers (Section 16): The Tribunal may order the recovery of possession of land, order specific performance of a contract, grant injunctions, award compensation or monetary claims, order payment of costs and witness expenses, or make any other order required by the justice of the case.
  • Legal Representation and Appeals: Advocates are prohibited from appearing or acting for any party before a Ward Tribunal (Section 18). Any party aggrieved by a decision of a Ward Tribunal may appeal to the District Land and Housing Tribunal within 45 days from the date of the decision (Section 19).

The District Land and Housing Tribunal (DLHT)

Under Section 22 of Cap. 216, the Minister responsible for lands is empowered to establish a District Land and Housing Tribunal in each district, region, or zone. (Note: Subsequent statutory reforms under the Written Laws (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act, 2016 and 2021 transferred administrative oversight and appointment of Chairpersons of DLHTs to the Judiciary of Tanzania to strengthen judicial independence and address resource bottlenecks.)

Composition and Role of Assessors

Under Sections 23 and 24 of Cap. 216, a DLHT is composed of a Chairperson and not less than two (2) assessors. (Note: Following amendments introduced by the Written Laws (Miscellaneous Amendments) (No. 3) Act, 2021, the requirement for assessors in land tribunals was abolished in Mainland Tanzania so that Chairpersons now sit alone, bringing DLHTs in line with ordinary subordinate courts and eliminating delays caused by assessor absenteeism.)

Historically, under Section 27 of Cap. 216, assessors had to be ordinary residents of the area, Tanzanian citizens, mentally fit, free of convictions involving violence, dishonesty, or moral turpitude, and not members of Parliament, local councils, Village Land Councils, or Ward Tribunals. Where assessors sat, the Chairperson was required to consider their opinions before writing judgment, though not bound by them, provided reasons were recorded for any departure.

Jurisdiction, Representation, and Appeals

  • Original Pecuniary Jurisdiction (Section 33(2)): The DLHT exercises original jurisdiction in all proceedings under the Land Act, Cap. 113 and the Village Land Act, Cap. 114 where the value of the subject matter does not exceed:
    • TZS 50,000,000 for immovable property (or up to TZS 100,000,000 / TZS 300,000,000 as subsequently revised by statutory amendments to Section 33 of Cap. 216); and
    • TZS 40,000,000 (subsequently revised upward) for other property whose value can be estimated in monetary terms.
  • Appellate and Revisional Jurisdiction: Hears appeals from Ward Tribunals within its area of jurisdiction and exercises revisional powers over Ward Tribunal records (Section 33(1) & Section 43).
  • Execution and Representation: The DLHT possesses power to execute its own orders and decrees (Section 33(3)). Unlike lower forums, advocates may appear and represent parties before the DLHT (Section 34).
  • Further Appeals: Any party aggrieved by a decision of the DLHT in the exercise of its original, appellate, or revisional jurisdiction may appeal to the High Court within 45 days (Section 41).

The High Court and the Court of Appeal

Originally established as a specialized "Land Division of the High Court" under Section 167(b) of the Land Act and Section 3(2)(d) of Cap. 216, Parliament later abolished the standalone administrative division via the Written Laws (Miscellaneous Amendments) (No. 2) Act, 2010 (Act No. 11 of 2010). Consequently, High Court land jurisdiction is now vested directly in the High Court of Tanzania across all its zonal registries.

Original Jurisdiction of the High Court (Section 37, Cap. 216)

The High Court exercises original jurisdiction in:

  • Proceedings for the recovery of possession of immovable property where the value exceeds the pecuniary ceiling of the DLHT (originally TZS 50,000,000; subsequently raised to TZS 300,000,000 under recent amendments);
  • Other land proceedings where the subject matter value exceeds the DLHT threshold (originally TZS 40,000,000; subsequently raised to TZS 200,000,000);
  • Proceedings involving public corporations under the Rent Restriction legislation (note: the Rent Restriction Act, Cap. 339 was repealed in 2005) and other disputes of national interest;
  • Proceedings under the Tanzania Investment Act (now replaced by the Tanzania Investment Act, No. 10 of 2022), the Land Act, Cap. 113, and the Land Acquisition Act, Cap. 118 in respect of disputes involving the Government; and
  • Any other land proceedings under any written law where jurisdiction is not restricted to a specific subordinate court or tribunal.

Appellate Jurisdiction, Customary Law Reference, and Final Appeals

  • Appellate and Supervisory Powers: The High Court hears appeals from decisions of the District Land and Housing Tribunal (Section 41) and exercises general supervisory and revisional powers over all DLHTs (Section 43).
  • Reference on Customary Law (Section 39): Where an appeal or proceeding before the High Court raises a question of customary law, the Court may refer the question to a panel of experts constituted under the Magistrates' Courts Act, Cap. 11, though the Court is not bound by the panel's opinion.
  • Appeals to the Court of Appeal (Section 47): Any person aggrieved by a decision or order of the High Court in the exercise of its original, appellate, or revisional jurisdiction may appeal to the Court of Appeal of Tanzania, subject to the Appellate Jurisdiction Act, Cap. 141.
