Introduction to the Law of Torts

1. Definition and Etymology

The word Tort is derived from the French word "Tortum" (originally Latin tortum), which means "wrong" or "injury".

Law of Tort deals with civil wrongs resulting from a breach of a duty imposed by law. This breach gives rise to a civil right of action for remedies (typically damages).

2. Main Objectives of Tort Law

  • To provide redress to parties whose legal rights have been infringed, violated, or affected by a civil wrong committed by a specific person.
  • To shift the losses from the person affected (plaintiff) to the person who caused the loss (tortfeasor/defendant).

3. Actionability and Legal Remedies

Not every injury automatically entitles a party to compensation. For a tort to be actionable in court, the plaintiff must establish that they have a valid cause of action against the defendant.

In tort law, the court typically awards remedies in the form of unliquidated damages (damages where the exact monetary amount is not pre-determined by agreement but is evaluated by the court).

4. Key Legal Terms & Parties

Judgment Creditor:
The person in whose favor a court judgment is awarded.
Judgment Debtor:
The person against whom a court judgment is made (the party obligated to pay or perform).
Tortfeasor:
A wrongdoer who commits a tort. If committed individually, they are a tortfeasor; if committed by multiple persons together, they are joint tortfeasors.

5. Definitions by Legal Scholars

Salmond’s Definition

"A tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract, or the breach of a trust, or other merely equitable obligation."

Underhill’s Definition

"A tort is an act or omission which, independent of contract, is authorized or unauthorized by law, resulting in the infringement of a legal right or substantial injury to a person's health, property, or reputation, remediable by an action for damages."

6. Essential Elements of Tortious Liability

To establish liability, the plaintiff must prove three core conditions:

  1. Wrongful Act or Omission: There must be a wrongful act or omission committed by the defendant.
  2. Legal Damage (Injuria): The act or omission must cause an infringement of a legal right of the plaintiff.
  3. Legal Remedy: The wrongful act or omission must give rise to a valid legal remedy under common law.

7. Basic Maxims in Law of Torts

Damnum Sine Injuria

"Damage without legal injury."

Refers to actual loss or damage suffered without the infringement of a legal right. In such cases, no compensation or cause of action exists in court.

Key Cases:

  • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)
  • Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (1892)
  • Bradford Corporation v. Pickles (1895)

Injuria Sine Damno

"Legal injury without actual financial loss or damage."

Refers to the infringement of an absolute legal right without causing physical or monetary damage. The action is actionable per se (in itself).

Key Case:

  • Ashby v. White (1703)

8. Theories of Tortious Liability

1. General Theory / Wider Theory (Winfield)

Proposed by Sir Frederick Pollock and supported by Winfield. It asserts that every wrongful act is a tort unless there is a specific legal justification or defense. It promotes the foundation of new torts as society evolves (e.g., Donoghue v. Stevenson establishing duty of care, or Rylands v. Fletcher establishing strict liability).

2. Pigeonhole Theory (Salmond)

Advocated by Salmond. It posits that tortious liability is limited to a set number of specific, well-defined civil wrongs (e.g., Assault, Battery, Trespass to Land, Defamation). If an injury cannot fit into an existing "pigeonhole," it is not actionable as a tort.

9. Classification of Torts (Pollock)

  • Torts against Persons (e.g., Assault, Battery, False Imprisonment)
  • Torts against Property (e.g., Trespass to Land, Conversion)
  • Torts against both Person and Property

LAW OF TORTS: GENERAL PRINCIPLES & SPECIFIC TORTS

Transcribed Notes, Essential Elements, Legal Maxims, Theories, Defences & Case Law Authorities

1. Essential Elements of a Tort

In tortious liability, the plaintiff is under an obligation to make his case by convincing the court and showing that there is a cause of action.

Once a cause of action is established by the plaintiff, the matter is considered actionable. Therefore, for the plaintiff to make his case relating to tortious liability in a court of law against the defendant, the following elements or conditions must be considered for him to be compensated:

  1. A wrongful act or omission on the part of the defendant.
  2. Such wrongful act or omission should result in legal damage.
  3. The wrongful act or omission committed by the defendant must be of such a nature that it gives rise to a legal remedy.

2. Classification of Torts

According to Pollock, torts are classified into:

  1. Torts against persons.
  2. Torts against causation and properties (property).
  3. Torts against both person and property.

3. Basic Principles in the Law of Torts

These maxims are used by courts to decide whether a matter is actionable and compensable, even if the plaintiff proves a cause of action:

A. Damnum Sine Injuria

Definition: Refers to damage/loss without legal injury. According to this rule, no compensation can be awarded if the plaintiff has failed to prove the infringement of any legal right vested in him.

  • Key Examples: Defamation, business competition.
  • Case Authorities:
    • Mogul Steamship Company Ltd v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (1889)
    • Bradford Corporation v. Pickles (1895)
    • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)

B. Injuria Sine Damno

Definition: Infringement of an absolute legal right without actual loss or damage. The matter is actionable per se, and compensation will be awarded.

  • Case Authority: Ashby v. White (1703)

4. Theories on the Nature of Tort Law

There are two primary conflicting theories on whether it is the "Law of Tort" or "Law of Torts":

A. Wider Theory / General Theory (Winfield)

Winfield argues that all injuries committed by one person to another are torts. It is not necessary to have a specific or named injury to justify that such injury creates tortious liability in law. This theory allows the creation of new tortious liabilities by the courts.

  • Strict Liability: Developed in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868).
  • Duty of Care: Developed in Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932).

B. Pigeon-hole Theory (Salmond)

Advocated by Salmond, this theory states that an injury amounts to a tort only when it fits into well-defined, pre-existing specific categories (pigeon-holes), such as Assault, Battery, Trespass to Land, or Defamation.

5. Defences in Tortious Liability

1. Volenti Non-Fit Injuria (Consent)

Where a person consents to the infliction of harm or risk, no injury is done in the eyes of the law, and no compensation/remedy can be awarded by the court. It acts as a complete defence.

  • Examples: Participating in sports, inviting a trespasser, consenting to publication.
  • Case Authorities:
    • Hall v. Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1932)
    • Padmavati v. Dugganaika (1975)

2. Inevitable Accident

An accident that could not have been avoided by the exercise of ordinary care, caution, and skill.

  • Case Authority: Stanley v. Powell (1891)

3. Act of God / Force Majeure (Vis Major)

Natural disasters or occurrences operating without human intervention (e.g., extraordinary floods, earthquakes).

  • Case Authority: Nichols v. Marsland (1876)

4. Self-Defence (Private Defence)

Force used against an attacker is justified provided it is reasonable and necessary solely for self-protection.

  • Case Authorities:
    • Madali v. M-Gangan (1984)
    • Bird v. Holbrook (1828)

5. Necessity

An act done to prevent a greater harm is justified even if it causes intentional damage.

  • Case Authorities:
    • Cope v. Sharpe (1912)
    • Carter v. Thomas (1893)

6. Torts Against Persons: Battery

Torts against persons involve violations or infringements over rights relating to safety, bodily integrity, and personal freedom (e.g., Assault, Battery, False Imprisonment, Defamation, Malicious Prosecution).

Definition of Battery: The intentional and direct application of force to another person without lawful justification, no matter how slight the touch may be.

Examples & Application of Force:

  • Slapping, pushing, or pulling a person.
  • Accidental touch vs. hostile touch.
  • Medical operation performed on an unconscious patient without prior consent to save life (justified/emergency).
  • Force-feeding a hunger-striking prisoner to preserve life.

Essential Elements to Prove Battery:

  1. Use of force: Direct or indirect (e.g., throwing an object, applying force in anger).
  2. Without lawful justification: The act must lack legal excuse or consent.

Key Case Authorities for Battery:

  • Innes v. Wylie (1844)
  • Stanley v. Powell (1891)
  • Pratap Daji v. B.B. & C.I. Ry (1875)

LAW OF TORTS: SPECIFIC TORTS

Transcribed Notes, Legal Principles, Statutory Foundations, Defences & Case Law Authorities

1. The Tort of Assault

Definition (Underhill): Assault is an attempt or threat to apply force to the person of another, directly or indirectly. If the person making the attempt causes another to reasonable grounds to believe that he has the present ability to execute his purpose, an assault is committed.

In other words, assault is an act of the defendant that causes the plaintiff to apprehend reasonable immediate personal violence.

Key Characteristics:

  • Assault is an attempt or threat to commit a wrong; even though it is an inchoate act, it is actionable and punishable in law.
  • Generally speaking, assault is an inchoate battery (an uncompleted battery).

Leading Case Authorities for Assault:

  • Stephens v. Myers (1830): The defendant advanced towards the chairman at a meeting with a clenched fist, stating he would rather pull the chairman out of his chair than be turned out, but was stopped by the churchwarden before reaching him. The court held him liable for assault because he had created a reasonable apprehension of immediate force.
  • Bovisetti Venkata Surya Rao v. Nandipati Mathayya (1964)

Remedies for Assault:

  1. Action for damages (Compensation).
  2. Self-help (Private defence).

2. The Tort of False Imprisonment

False imprisonment refers to the total restraint of a person's liberty for any duration of time, however short, without lawful justification.

General Principles:

  • It does not necessarily require a formal detention or court order, nor is it required that the person be locked inside a prison or jail.
  • Any unlawful restriction of a person's liberty is actionable in court.
  • The right to freedom of movement and bodily restraint is a fundamental right. For instance, in Tanzania, the right to freedom of movement is protected under Article 17 of the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (CURT), 1977.

Elements of False Imprisonment:

  1. Total Restraint / Restriction: There must be a total restraint of the plaintiff's liberty to move from one place to another. Partial restriction (e.g., blocking one path while leaving another reasonable avenue of escape open) does not constitute false imprisonment.
    • Bird v. Jones (1845): Partial obstruction of a public footpath where the plaintiff could go in another direction did not constitute false imprisonment.
    • Mee v. Cruikshank (1902)
    • Herring v. Boyle (1834)
    • Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation Co. Ltd (1919)
  2. Without Lawful Justification: The total restriction must be unlawful and without legal authority or excuse.
    • Rudal Sah v. State of Bihar (1983)
    • Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry Co. Ltd (1910)
    • Archibold v. Arazu (1990)

Remedies for False Imprisonment:

  1. Action for damages.
  2. Self-help (Reasonable force used by the plaintiff to escape).
  3. Writ of Habeas Corpus (A judicial remedy against unlawful administrative/police detention).

3. The Tort of Malicious Prosecution

Malicious prosecution consists of instituting unsuccessful criminal or legal proceedings against another person maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause.

It constitutes a civil wrong arising from the misuse and abuse of judicial procedures.

Case Authority:

  • West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2007): Defined malicious prosecution as a judicial proceeding instituted by one person against another from an improper motive and without probable cause.

Essential Elements to Prove Malicious Prosecution:

  1. The plaintiff must have been prosecuted by the defendant (i.e., the defendant was actively instrumental in instigating the proceedings).
  2. There was an absence of reasonable and probable cause.
  3. The defendant acted maliciously (improper or indirect motive).
  4. The proceedings terminated in favor of the plaintiff (e.g., acquittal or dismissal).
  5. The plaintiff suffered damage (to reputation, person, or property).

4. The Tort of Defamation

Defamation involves the publication of a false statement about a person that tends to lower them in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or cause them to be avoided or shunned.

Legal Foundations & Constitutional Context:

  • Defamation protects the right to honor, dignity, and reputation.
  • Constitutional & International Basis:
    • Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania, 1977: Article 18 guarantees freedom of expression, while Articles 12, 16, & 24 protect personal dignity and individual rights. Freedom of speech is not absolute; it is subject to limitations preventing the infringement of others' dignity.
    • Cybercrimes Act, 2015 (Section 16).
    • Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR): Article 12.
    • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR): Articles 17 & 19(3)(a).

Forms of Defamation:

  1. Libel: Defamation in a permanent form (e.g., written text, printed material, pictures, internet posts). Actionable per se.
    • John Peter Zenger Case (1735)
  2. Slander: Defamation in a transient form (e.g., spoken words, gestures, sign language, physical acts).
    • Generally requires proof of special (actual financial/material) damage, subject to statutory exceptions.

Essential Elements of Defamation:

  1. The statement must be defamatory and false.
    • John Peter Zenger Case (1735)
    • Cullen v. White (2003)
  2. The statement must refer to the plaintiff.
    • E. Hulton & Co. v. Jones (1909)
    • Newstead v. London Express Newspaper Ltd (1940)
  3. The statement must be published (communicated to at least one third party other than the plaintiff).
    • Bernard Membe v. Cyprian Musiba (2020): Statements made purely in private without third-party publication do not constitute actionable public defamation.
    • Hamis v. Abdi Ali (1971): Defamation involves communicating matters to the mind of a third party that disparage the plaintiff's reputation.

Class & Group Defamation:

Where a defamatory statement is directed at a large class or group of individuals (e.g., "all lawyers are thieves"), no individual member can sue unless the statement can be reasonably understood to refer specifically to them.

  • Knupffer v. London Express Newspaper Ltd (1944): Lord Atkin observed that the crucial question in class defamation is whether the statement can be reasonably construed as referring to the individual plaintiff.
  • Eastwood v. Holmes (1858): Willes J. noted that where an article reflects generally on a broad class, no individual member can maintain an action.

Remedies & Case Authorities on Damages:

The court calculates damages based on the extent, medium, and scope of publication across society.

  • Mussa Yafum v. Manilal (1904)
  • Antarajami Sharma v. Padma Bowa (2007)
  • Bank of India v. Lakshmi Das

LAW OF TORTS: DEFAMATION (CONT.) & TORTS AGAINST PROPERTY

Transcribed Notes, Legal Principles, Statutory Provisions, Defences & Case Law Authorities

1. Defamation: Reference & Publication (Elements 2 & 3)

Where a statement indicates certain characteristics that identify the plaintiff, that will constitute a defamatory statement actionable in a court of law.

  • Le Fanu v. Malcomson (1848)

Exceptions to the General Rule on Group Defamation:

  1. Small Group Exception: When the class or group is so small, it is easy to ascertain that the defendant intended to make a defamatory statement against that specific group of persons. In that regard, there is a possibility to file a lawsuit in a court of law, and such a statement becomes actionable.
    • Browne v. Thomson & Co. (1912)
    • Foxcroft v. Lacey (1613)
  2. Specific Application Exception: Where circumstances make it reasonable to infer that the statement made by the defendant refers to a specific individual or entity, even if framed as a statement about a group, it is actionable.

Element No. 3: Publication to the Public

To constitute the tort of defamation, the defamatory statement must be published to a third party or the public. In defamation actions, compensation awarded to the plaintiff corresponds to the extent to which the information was circulated.

Case Authorities:

  • Nyabagaya Mtani v. Nyakanyi Kabeka (1983) TLR 332
  • Amosi Jonathan v. J.S. Masuka and Others (1983) TLR 201
  • Theaker v. Richardson (1962)
  • Huth v. Huth (1915)

Element No. 4: Proof of Damage

It must be proved that the plaintiff suffered damage as a consequence of the publication.

  • Tito Peter Mwaliposa v. Juma Abdallah Kapikalira

Defences Against Defamation:

  1. Justification / Truth.
  2. The statement did not refer to the plaintiff.
  3. The statement was not published.
  4. Volenti Non-Fit Injuria / Consent.
  5. Privilege:
    • Absolute Privilege (e.g., Article 100 of CURT, 1977 regarding Parliamentary immunity).
    • Qualified Privilege.

Remedies Awarded by Courts in Defamation:

  • Compensation (Damages).
  • Injunction.
  • Specific Performance / Order for Apology/Retraction.

2. Torts Against Property

Torts against property refer to the law enacted by the state to maintain the right of enjoyment of property by the owner. Unlawful, jealous, or malicious interference with another's property rights is prohibited by law.

Property Tort Definition: An intentional interference with property rights committed by one person against another. It is a civil wrong committed by the defendant against the plaintiff (e.g., trespass to land, trespass to chattels, or conversion).

Ways in Which Torts Against Property Are Committed:

  1. Trespass to Land: Entering someone's land without permission.
  2. Trespass to Chattels: Handling, interfering with, or taking a particular item owned by another person without permission.

Illustration: Mr. X is a farmer, and his farm is next to land owned by Mr. Y, who owns cows. One day, Mr. Y's cows enter Mr. X's farm and cause damage. In this scenario, Mr. Y, as the owner of the cows that entered Mr. X's farm, will be held liable for committing trespass, establishing tortious liability regarding immovable property.

Forms of Trespass:

  • Trespass Quare Clausum Fregit: Unlawful entry upon land.
  • Trespass De Bonis Asportatis: Wrongful taking or carrying away of goods.
  • Conversion: Where a person takes possession of someone else's property with the intention of asserting rights inconsistent with the true owner's rights.

Key Characteristics & Elements of Proof:

To establish a tort of trespass, the plaintiff does not need to prove:

  • The use of force.
  • Actual financial or physical damage.
  • Breach of an enclosure.

Under the principle of Actionable Per Se, trespass is actionable without proof of actual damage caused to the property.

Required Elements to Establish Liability:

  1. There must be a valid right to hold or possess the property.
  2. There must be proof of disturbance, interference, or seizure of that right.
  3. Such disturbance or seizure restricts the plaintiff's peaceful enjoyment of the property.

Ways Trespass Can Be Committed:

  1. By wrongful entry upon the land of the plaintiff by the defendant. The law states that even the slightest entry amounts to trespass.
    • Smith v. Stone (1647)
  2. By remaining on the land after authority/licence has expired.
  3. By placing objects or structures on the land (continuing trespass).
  4. Re-entry on land where both claimants dispute ownership.
  5. Easement violations (Refer to Section 146 of the Land Act, Cap 113 regarding interests granted over land).

3. Doctrine of Trespass Ab Initio

Where a person enters land under authority of the law (ad initio) but subsequently commits an act or misconduct that abuses that authority, they become a trespasser from the very beginning (trespass ab initio).

Conditions Required for Trespass Ab Initio to Apply:

  1. The initial authority to enter must be given by law (not by private permission/licence of the owner).
  2. The subsequent act must constitute a positive act of misfeasance (doing a lawful act wrongfully or abusing authority), rather than mere nonfeasance (omission).

Case Authorities for Trespass Ab Initio:

  • Six Carpenters' Case (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a: Six carpenters entered a tavern, ordered bread and wine, and paid for it. They later ordered more wine and bread but refused to pay. The court held that non-payment was mere nonfeasance, so they were not trespassers ab initio.
  • Chic Fashions (West Wales) Ltd v. Jones [1968] 2 QB 299: Expressed doubts on the strict ratio of original trespass principles, adapting police authority rights when executing search warrants in good faith to protect stolen goods.

4. Defences in Trespass to Property

  1. Leave and Licence: Permission granted under land law (e.g., Land Act provisions) allowing the defendant to enter or enjoy rights over the land without payment.
  2. Public or Private Necessity: Entry onto land to preserve life, prevent greater harm, or escape imminent peril.
  3. Self-Defence / Defence of Property: Reasonable force used to protect possession or eject a trespasser.

5. Criminal Trespass under Tanzanian Law

In Tanzania, criminal trespass is defined under Section 199 of the Penal Code (Cap 16).

Essential Elements of Criminal Trespass:

  1. Intent (Mens Rea): Intent to commit an offence, intimidate, insult, or annoy.
  2. Warning / Notice Required: Explicit notice (e.g., "No Trespassing" signs, boundary fences, or locked gates/doors).

Public Property Rule: A person can be convicted of criminal trespass even in a public place (e.g., a store or park) if they remain on the premises after closing hours or refuse to leave when ordered to do so.

Related Penal Code Provisions:

  • Penal Code, Section 294: Housebreaking and Burglary.
  • Penal Code, Sections 295, 296, & 297.
Dual Liability Principle: Criminal liability does not exempt a person from civil liability. A trespasser may face criminal charges under the Penal Code as well as civil suits for damages under civil procedure laws.

LAW OF TORTS: TRESPASS TO GOODS & NEGLIGENCE

Transcribed Notes, Legal Principles, Statutory Foundations, Theories & Case Law Authorities

1. Trespass to Goods

Trespass to goods is a tort that deals with movable property. It is a civil wrong actionable in a court of law; if a person is found liable for committing trespass to goods, they will be ordered by the court to pay compensation.

Core Concept: Trespass to goods is a wrong committed against the possession or ownership of movable goods. Movable goods are statutorily governed by the Sale of Goods Act [Cap. 214 R.E. 2002]. Under the law, there is a clear legal distinction between ownership and possession.

Essentially, trespass to goods protects an individual's right to possession or ownership of goods from wrongful deprivation or interference by another person.

Forms of Trespass to Goods:

Trespass to goods can take various forms, such as throwing a stone at an animal or killing a dog by poisoning.

Proving Trespass to Goods:

To establish trespass to goods, the plaintiff must prove direct interference with the goods without lawful justification. Such direct interference can be committed either intentionally or by honest mistake.

Case Authorities for Trespass to Goods:

  • Cresswell v. Sirl [1948] 1 KB 241: The defendant shot the plaintiff's dog while it was attacking the defendant's livestock. The court held that the shooting was justified because the defendant acted reasonably to protect his property.
  • National Coal Board v. J.E. Evans & Co. (Cardiff) Ltd [1951] 2 KB 861: The defendants engaged independent contractors to excavate land. Unknown to them, the plaintiff's predecessors had placed an underground cable beneath the land, which was damaged during excavation.
  • Kirk v. Gregory (1876) 1 EX D 55: Direct interference with goods, even if done out of a well-meaning motive or honest mistake, constitutes trespass to goods.

Related Torts Dealing with Deprivation of Goods:

  1. Detinue: The wrongful detention of goods, demanding the return of specific chattels or their value.
  2. Conversion: An intentional act of dealing with goods in a manner inconsistent with the owner's rights, amounting to a total deprivation of those rights.

Both detinue and conversion deal with the wrongful deprivation of a person's rights over their property.

Remedies:

  • Compensation / Damages.

2. The Tort of Negligence

Definition (Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab, 2005): Negligence is the breach of a duty caused by omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided by those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do.

Theories of Negligence:

  1. Subjective Theory:
    • Advocated by Salmond, this theory states that negligence depends on a particular mental state or condition of mind.
    • Under this view, a person is responsible for negligent misconduct because it is an intentional act or failure to act according to the best of their ability. Salmond argues that if a person acts to the best of his ability, he should not be held accountable.
  2. Objective Theory:
    • Supported by scholars like Underhill, Pollock, and modern jurisprudence, this theory emphasizes that negligence is measured objectively against the standard of a reasonable person.
    • It does not require proving an intentional or subjective state of mind; rather, human conduct itself is evaluated to establish whether negligent misconduct occurred.

Leading Judicial Definitions & Authorities:

  • Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856) 11 Exch 781: Formulated the classic objective standard of the "reasonable man". Before doing something, a person should apply their skills apart from imprudence.
  • Underhill's Definition: Negligence consists of an act or omission to do something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would do, causing damage to the plaintiff.

Essential Elements to Establish Liability in Negligence:

To establish a claim of negligence against the defendant, the plaintiff must prove the following three elements:

  1. Duty of Care: The defendant owed a legal duty of care to the plaintiff.
  2. Breach of Duty: The defendant breached that duty of care through an act or omission.
  3. Resulting Damage / Causation: The plaintiff suffered actual damage or loss as a direct consequence of that breach.
