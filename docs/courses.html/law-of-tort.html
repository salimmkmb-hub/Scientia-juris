Introduction to the Law of Torts

1. Definition and Etymology

The word Tort is derived from the French word "Tortum" (originally Latin tortum), which means "wrong" or "injury".

Law of Tort deals with civil wrongs resulting from a breach of a duty imposed by law. This breach gives rise to a civil right of action for remedies (typically damages).

2. Main Objectives of Tort Law

  • To provide redress to parties whose legal rights have been infringed, violated, or affected by a civil wrong committed by a specific person.
  • To shift the losses from the person affected (plaintiff) to the person who caused the loss (tortfeasor/defendant).

3. Actionability and Legal Remedies

Not every injury automatically entitles a party to compensation. For a tort to be actionable in court, the plaintiff must establish that they have a valid cause of action against the defendant.

In tort law, the court typically awards remedies in the form of unliquidated damages (damages where the exact monetary amount is not pre-determined by agreement but is evaluated by the court).

4. Key Legal Terms & Parties

Judgment Creditor:
The person in whose favor a court judgment is awarded.
Judgment Debtor:
The person against whom a court judgment is made (the party obligated to pay or perform).
Tortfeasor:
A wrongdoer who commits a tort. If committed individually, they are a tortfeasor; if committed by multiple persons together, they are joint tortfeasors.

5. Definitions by Legal Scholars

Salmond’s Definition

"A tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract, or the breach of a trust, or other merely equitable obligation."

Underhill’s Definition

"A tort is an act or omission which, independent of contract, is authorized or unauthorized by law, resulting in the infringement of a legal right or substantial injury to a person's health, property, or reputation, remediable by an action for damages."

6. Essential Elements of Tortious Liability

To establish liability, the plaintiff must prove three core conditions:

  1. Wrongful Act or Omission: There must be a wrongful act or omission committed by the defendant.
  2. Legal Damage (Injuria): The act or omission must cause an infringement of a legal right of the plaintiff.
  3. Legal Remedy: The wrongful act or omission must give rise to a valid legal remedy under common law.

7. Basic Maxims in Law of Torts

Damnum Sine Injuria

"Damage without legal injury."

Refers to actual loss or damage suffered without the infringement of a legal right. In such cases, no compensation or cause of action exists in court.

Key Cases:

  • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)
  • Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (1892)
  • Bradford Corporation v. Pickles (1895)

Injuria Sine Damno

"Legal injury without actual financial loss or damage."

Refers to the infringement of an absolute legal right without causing physical or monetary damage. The action is actionable per se (in itself).

Key Case:

  • Ashby v. White (1703)

8. Theories of Tortious Liability

1. General Theory / Wider Theory (Winfield)

Proposed by Sir Frederick Pollock and supported by Winfield. It asserts that every wrongful act is a tort unless there is a specific legal justification or defense. It promotes the foundation of new torts as society evolves (e.g., Donoghue v. Stevenson establishing duty of care, or Rylands v. Fletcher establishing strict liability).

2. Pigeonhole Theory (Salmond)

Advocated by Salmond. It posits that tortious liability is limited to a set number of specific, well-defined civil wrongs (e.g., Assault, Battery, Trespass to Land, Defamation). If an injury cannot fit into an existing "pigeonhole," it is not actionable as a tort.

9. Classification of Torts (Pollock)

  • Torts against Persons (e.g., Assault, Battery, False Imprisonment)
  • Torts against Property (e.g., Trespass to Land, Conversion)
  • Torts against both Person and Property
