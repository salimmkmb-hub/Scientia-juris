Topic 1: General Principles & Definitions

2.1 Meaning of Tort and Cause of Action

For any tort committed by a person, the aggrieved party may bring an action in a court of law. This imposes an obligation on the plaintiff to establish that the matter brought before the court constitutes a valid cause of action.

Considering the nature of the suit filed in a court of law, the compensation awarded by the court is primarily in the form of unliquidated damages (damages whose amount is determined at the discretion of the court rather than fixed by prior agreement).

2.2 Key Parties in Litigation

  • Plaintiff / Claimant: Any party whose right has been violated or infringed upon and who initiates a civil suit in a court of law.
  • Defendant: The party alleged to have committed the civil wrong.
  • Tortfeasor: An individual who commits a tort. If a tort is committed by multiple individuals acting together, they are referred to as Joint Tortfeasors.
  • Judgment Creditor: The person in whose favour a judgment or court decree has been made.
  • Judgment Debtor: The person against whom a judgment or court decree has been delivered.

2.3 Actionability and Moral Norms

It is important to note that not every violation of a moral right or duty can be compensated by a court of law. The court must examine whether the act is legally actionable under the law of tort or merely a breach of moral norms. Generally, the Law of Tort determines rights between parties that are legally actionable in a court of law. Where the plaintiff establishes a valid cause of action, the court will issue a pronouncement in favour of the plaintiff and award appropriate damages.

2.4 Classic Definitions of Tort

According to Salmond: "A tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation."

According to Underhill: "A tort refers to an act or omission independent of contract which is unauthorized by law, resulting either in the infringement of a right to which another person is entitled or in the infliction upon some person of substantial loss in money, health, or material comfort, and which is remediable by an action for damages."

2.5 Essential Elements of a Tort

To establish tortious liability and receive compensation, the plaintiff must convince the court by proving the following three essential conditions:

  1. A wrongful act or omission: There must be a wrongful act or omission committed by the defendant.
  2. Legal Damage: Such wrongful act or omission must result in legal damage or injury to the plaintiff.
  3. Legal Remedy: The wrongful act or omission must be of such a nature as to give rise to a legal remedy (ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy).

2.6 Classifications of Torts (Pollock)

According to Sir Frederick Pollock, torts can be classified into three main categories:

  • Torts against persons (e.g., assault, battery, false imprisonment, defamation).
  • Torts against property and rights (e.g., trespass, conversion, nuisance).
  • Torts against both persons and property.

Topic 2: Key Maxims & Fundamental Principles

Courts rely on core legal maxims to determine whether a matter is actionable and compensable.

2.1 Damnum Sine Injuria

Definition: Financial, physical, or material damage without the infringement of a legal right.

Rule: No compensation can be awarded if the plaintiff fails to prove the violation of a legally recognized right, even if substantial financial or physical loss was suffered.

Key Cases:

  • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410): A schoolmaster opened a competing school near the plaintiff's school, causing the plaintiff's school fees to drop. Held: No legal right was violated; mere economic loss due to competition is non-actionable.
  • Mogul Steamship Co. Ltd. v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (1889): Lawful trade competition causing financial loss to a rival trader does not constitute a legal injury.
  • Mayor of Bradford v. Pickles (1895): The defendant percolated underground water through his land to prevent it from reaching the plaintiff's reservoir, hoping to force the plaintiff to buy his land. Held: The act was lawful regardless of malicious intent; no legal right was infringed.

2.2 Injuria Sine Damno

Definition: Infringement of an absolute legal right without actual physical or financial damage.

Rule: Whenever a legal right is infringed, the action is maintainable and actionable per se, even if the plaintiff suffered no pecuniary or actual harm.

Key Case:

  • Ashby v. White (1703): The plaintiff was a qualified voter who was wrongfully prevented from casting his vote by a returning officer. Even though the candidate he intended to vote for won the election and he suffered no pecuniary damage, the court held that the deprivation of his legal right to vote was an actionable wrong.

3. Theories of Tortious Liability

3.1 Winfield’s Theory (The Wider / Foundation Theory)

Developed by Sir Percy Winfield, this theory asserts that all injuries done to another person are torts unless there is some lawful justification recognized by law. Under this approach, the law of tort is open-ended, encouraging and allowing courts to create new torts whenever a new form of unjustified injury arises (e.g., the creation of Strict Liability in Rylands v. Fletcher or the general Duty of Care principle in Donoghue v. Stevenson).

3.2 Salmond’s Theory (The Pigeonhole Theory / Narrow View)

Developed by Sir John Salmond, this theory states that there is no general principle of liability; rather, the law consists of a neat set of specific, well-defined, and closed categories of civil wrongs ("pigeonholes"). For an injury to be actionable as a tort, it must fit into one of these specific recognized boxes (e.g., Assault, Battery, Trespass, Defamation). If an act does not fall into a recognized pigeonhole, it is not a tort unless Parliament or established precedent declares it to be one.

Topic 3: General Defences in Tortious Liability

3.1 Volenti Non-Fit Injuria (Consent / Assumption of Risk)

Where a person voluntarily consents to the infliction of harm or agrees to run the risk of harm, no legal injury is done and no remedy can be awarded by a court of law. It serves as a complete defence.

  • Defamation: No claim lies if the plaintiff consented to the publication of the statement.
  • Trespass: No claim lies if the plaintiff invited or licensed the defendant to enter the land.
  • Hall v. Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1932): Spectator at a motor race injured when a car crashed. Held: Spectators assume the ordinary risks associated with attending high-speed events.
  • Padmavati v. Dugganaika (1975): Two strangers took a lift in a jeep; an unexpected wheel failure caused an accident. Held: The passengers had voluntarily assumed the risk.

3.2 Self-Defence (Private Defence)

A defendant may use reasonable force to defend their person, family, or property against an unlawful attack or threat, provided the force used is proportionate to the threat faced.

  • Cope v. Sharpe (1891): Entry onto land to put out a fire and prevent its spread was held justified as a necessary act of protection.

3.3 Necessity

An act that causes harm may be justified if it was committed to prevent a greater harm to persons or property, provided the danger was imminent and real.

  • Carter v. Thomas (1891): Volunteer firemen entered private property to extinguish a fire when professional firefighters were already managing it effectively. Held: Defence of necessity failed as the intervention was unnecessary.
  • Medical Emergency: Performing an urgent operation on an unconscious person to save their life.
  • Prisoners: Forcibly feeding a hunger-striking prisoner to prevent immediate risk to life.

3.4 Inevitable Accident

An accident that could not have been avoided by the exercise of reasonable care, caution, and skill on the part of the defendant.

  • Stanley v. Powell (1891): The defendant fired at a pheasant; a pellet ricocheted off a tree trunk and injured the plaintiff. Held: The injury was the result of an inevitable accident; defendant was not liable.

3.5 Act of God / Vis Major (Force Majeure)

An extraordinary, unforeseen event caused directly by natural forces without human intervention, which could not have been guarded against by any reasonable foresight or care (e.g., unprecedented floods, earthquakes, lightning strikes).

  • Nichols v. Marsland (1876): Unprecedented torrential rainfall caused artificial lakes to burst their banks and destroy bridges. Held: The escape was caused by an Act of God.

Topic 4: Specific Torts Against Persons

4.1 Battery

Definition: The intentional and direct application of force to another person without lawful justification.

Essential Elements:

  • Use of force (direct like slapping/pushing, or indirect like striking with an object or throwing water).
  • The force must be applied without lawful justification.
  • Hostile or non-consensual nature (even touching someone in anger constitutes battery).

Key Cases: Innes v. Wylie (1844); Stanley v. Powell (1891); Pratap Daji v. B.B. & C.I. Ry (1875).

4.2 Assault

Definition: An act of the defendant that causes the plaintiff to reasonably apprehend the immediate application of unlawful force (battery) upon their person.

Key Distinction: Assault is an intentional attempt or threat to commit a battery. Battery is the actual execution of that physical contact. Thus, an assault is an inchoate battery.

Key Cases:

  • Stephens v. Myers (1830): The defendant advanced toward the chairman of a meeting with clenched fists, saying he would rather pull the chairman out of his chair than be turned out, but was stopped by the churchwarden. Held: Actionable assault because there was an immediate threat and ability to execute it.
  • Bavisetty Venkata Surya Rao v. Nandipati Muthayya (1964): Mere words or threatening gestures without reasonable capacity or intention to execute immediate force do not constitute assault.

4.3 False Imprisonment

Definition: The imposition of a total restraint upon the liberty of a person for any length of time without sufficient lawful justification.

Constitutional Provision: Article 17 of the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (CURT), 1977 guarantees the right to freedom of movement. Violation of this freedom without legal authority constitutes false imprisonment.

Essential Elements:

  1. Total Restraint: The restraint must be total, not partial. Restricting movement in one direction while leaving an reasonable alternative avenue of escape open does not constitute false imprisonment.
  2. Absence of Lawful Justification: The restriction must be without lawful warrant or legal authority.

Key Cases:

  • Bird v. Jones (1845): The defendant enclosed a portion of a public footway to view a regatta. The plaintiff was prevented from moving forward but was free to remain or turn back. Held: Not false imprisonment because the restraint was partial, not total.
  • Herring v. Boyle (1834): A schoolmaster refused to allow a boy to go home with his mother because school fees were unpaid, but the boy was unaware of the restraint. Held: Knowledge of restraint is essential under early common law, though modern cases like Meering show a shift.
  • Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation Co. Ltd. (1919): A person can be falsely imprisoned without knowing it at the time (e.g., while asleep, intoxicated, or unconscious).
  • Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983): The petitioner was detained in prison for 14 years after his acquittal. The Supreme Court awarded compensation for violation of constitutional liberty.
  • Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry Co. Ltd. (1910): A passenger paid 1 penny to enter a wharf to board a ferry, then changed his mind and tried to leave without paying the exit fare required by contract rules. Held: Restraint was reasonable under contractual terms.

Remedies for False Imprisonment:

  • Action for Damages.
  • Self-Help (reasonable force used to escape).
  • Writ of Habeas Corpus (judicial order to produce a detained person before the court).

4.4 Malicious Prosecution

Definition: The institution or continuation of unsuccessful judicial proceedings (usually criminal) against another person maliciously, without reasonable or probable cause, resulting in damage to the plaintiff.

Essential Elements:

  1. Prosecution by Defendant: The defendant must have instituted or instigated criminal legal proceedings against the plaintiff.
  2. Absence of Reasonable and Probable Cause: The defendant acted without an honest or reasonable belief in the guilt or liability of the accused.
  3. Malice: The proceedings were driven by an improper, hostile, or collateral motive rather than a genuine desire to bring an offender to justice.
  4. Termination in Plaintiff's Favour: The prosecution must have ended in favour of the plaintiff (e.g., acquittal, discharge, or quashing of charges).
  5. Damage: The plaintiff suffered harm to their reputation, personal liberty, or financial status.

Key Case: West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2006) — Defined the elements of malicious prosecution in judicial proceedings.

4.5 Defamation

Definition: The publication of a false and derogatory statement concerning a person, without lawful justification, which tends to lower their reputation in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or makes them shun or avoid that person.

Constitutional & Statutory Framework:

  • Freedom of Speech & Limitations: Article 18 of CURT (1977) guarantees freedom of expression. However, this right is subject to limitations under Article 30(1) & (2) to protect the dignity and reputation of others.
  • International Instruments: Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Art. 12); International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, Art. 17 & 19).
  • Criminal Defamation: Section 16 of the Cybercrimes Act of Tanzania criminalizes publishing defamatory or false statements online.
  • Bernard Membe v. Cyprian Musiba (2020): High-profile Tanzanian defamation suit highlighting public injury to political and professional reputation.

Forms of Defamation:

  • Libel: Defamatory matter expressed in permanent form (e.g., written words, printed text, pictures, internet posts, films). Libel is actionable per se (without proof of actual damage).
  • Slander: Defamatory matter expressed in transient/temporary form (e.g., spoken words, gestures, smiles, hissing, sign language). Generally requires proof of special (actual) damage, subject to common-law exceptions.

Essential Elements of Defamation:

  1. The statement must be defamatory: It must tend to lower the plaintiff's reputation.
  2. It must refer to the plaintiff: The statement must be understood by reasonable people as referring to the plaintiff.
    • E. Hulton & Co. v. Jones (1909): Defamation established even where the defendant intended a fictitious character name ("Artemus Jones"), but real persons reasonably associated it with the plaintiff.
    • Newstead v. London Express Newspaper Ltd. (1940): True statement about one person rendered defamatory to another person of the exact same name because of lack of specific details.
  3. Publication to a Third Party: The statement must be communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff. Statements made in confidence solely between the plaintiff and defendant do not constitute publication.
    • Huth v. Huth (1915): An inquisitive butler opening a letter addressed to his master did not constitute foreseeable publication.
  4. Proof of Damage: Required in cases of slander (unless covered by statutory exceptions) and to assess quantum of compensation.
    • Tito Peter Mwakyusa v. Juma Abdallah Kapikulira: Demonstrated the assessment of damages in Tanzanian defamation litigation.

Group Defamation & Exceptions:

General Rule: Where defamatory words are spoken of a large group or class of persons (e.g., "All lawyers are thieves"), no individual member of that class can sue unless there is something pointing directly to that specific individual (Eastwood v. Holmes; Knupffer v. London Express Newspaper Ltd. [1943]).

  • Small Group Exception: If the group is so small that the defamatory statement naturally points to every individual member, any member can sue (e.g., Browne v. Thomson [1912]; Foxcroft v. Lacey [1678]).
  • Specific Application Exception: Where circumstances make it reasonable to conclude that a statement made against a group explicitly targets a particular individual or company within it (Le Fanu v. Malcomson [1848]).
  • Tanzanian Cases: Nyabagaya Mtani v. Nyahanyi Kabeka (1983 TLR 332); Amosi Jonathan v. J.S. Masuka & Others (1983 TLR 201).

Defences to Defamation:

  • Justification (Truth): The statement made was substantially true.
  • Fair Comment: Honest expression of opinion on a matter of public interest based on true facts.
  • Absolute Privilege: Complete protection granted regardless of malice (e.g., Parliamentary proceedings under Art. 100 of CURT 1977, judicial proceedings).
  • Qualified Privilege: Protection granted where the maker has a legal, moral, or social duty to make the statement to a party with a corresponding duty or interest to receive it, provided there is no malice.
  • Unintentional Publication / Lack of Reference: Proving the statement did not refer to the plaintiff or was never published to a third party.
  • Consent (Volenti Non-Fit Injuria).

Topic 5: Torts Against Property

5.1 Overview

Property torts stem from the unlawful or intentional interference with an individual's right to peaceful possession, ownership, and enjoyment of land or chattels.

5.2 Trespass to Land (Trespass Quare Clausum Fregit)

Definition: The direct, intentional, and unlawful entry onto or interference with land in the possession of another person without legal permission.

Key Principles:

  • Actionable Per Se: Trespass to land is actionable without proof of actual damage. Even the slightest unauthorized entry constitutes a trespass.
  • Possession vs. Ownership: Trespass is primarily a wrong against possession, not necessarily legal ownership.

Ways Trespass to Land Can Be Committed:

  • Entering upon the land directly (e.g., walking, driving, putting livestock on another's property).
  • Remaining on the land after permission/licence has been revoked.
  • Constructive Entry: Placing physical objects, debris, or structures on or over another's land.

Trespass Ab Initio:

When a person enters land under authority of law (e.g., a police officer or officer of the court) and subsequently commits a wrongful act or misfeasance (abuses that legal authority), they are deemed a trespasser from the very beginning (ab initio).

Conditions for Trespass Ab Initio:

  1. The initial entry must have been authorized by law (not by private licence or contract).
  2. The subsequent wrongful act must be an affirmative act of misfeasance (doing something wrong), not merely nonfeasance (omission/failure to act).

Key Cases on Trespass Ab Initio:

  • Six Carpenters' Case (1610): Six carpenters entered an inn, bought and paid for bread and wine, then ordered more wine and refused to pay. Held: Refusal to pay was nonfeasance (omission), so they were not trespassers ab initio.
  • Chic Fashions (West Wales) Ltd. v. Jones (1968): Constables executed a search warrant. The court restricted the broad application of trespass ab initio to protect law enforcement officers executing warrants in good faith.

Criminal Trespass in Tanzania:

  • Defined under Section 299 of the Tanzanian Penal Code (Cap 16).
  • Elements: Intent to commit an offence, intimidate, insult, or annoy, accompanied by proper warning or notice (e.g., "No Trespassing" signs, fences, locked doors).
  • Related Offences: Housebreaking and Burglary (Sections 294–297, Penal Code).
  • Civil vs. Criminal: A person may face criminal charges while simultaneously being sued for civil remedies under the Civil Procedure Code.

Defences to Trespass to Land:

  • Leave and Licence: Permission granted by the possessor (e.g., under the Land Act, Cap 113).
  • Necessity: Entry to save lives, preserve property, or prevent catastrophic loss.
  • Self-Defence / Private Defence.
  • Statutory Authority: Lawful entry by government officials executing public duties.
  • Easement: A granted legal right over another's land (Section 146, Land Act).

5.3 Trespass to Goods (De Bonis Asportatis), Detinue, and Conversion

Movable property and chattels are governed in commercial contexts by statutes such as the Sale of Goods Act (Cap 214).

Categories of Wrong Against Goods:

  • Trespass to Goods: Unlawful, direct physical interference with goods in the possession of another (e.g., moving a car, throwing stones at an animal, damaging items). Actionable per se.
    • Kirk v. Gregory (1876): Unlawful moving of jewelry from one room to another without authorization constituted trespass to goods.
    • Cresswell v. Sirl (1948): A farmer shot a dog attacking his livestock. Held: Justified if shooting was reasonably necessary to protect property.
    • National Coal Board v. Evans (1951): Contractors damaged an underground cable they did not know existed. Held: Involuntary/accidental contact without fault or negligence is not trespass.
  • Conversion: An intentional act of dealing with goods in a manner inconsistent with the true owner's rights, exercising control or dominion over them (e.g., selling, destroying, or altering another's goods).
  • Detinue: The wrongful detention and refusal to deliver up goods to the person who has an immediate right to their possession upon demand.

Topic 6: The Law of Negligence

6.1 Definition

Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856): "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do."

See also: Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005).

6.2 Theories of Negligence

  • Subjective Theory (Salmond): Focuses on the defendant's mental state. Negligence consists of a culpable state of mind or lack of care. If a person acts to the absolute best of their individual ability, they should not be held liable.
  • Objective Theory (Pollock / Modern View): Focuses entirely on external conduct. Negligence is assessed by measuring the defendant's actions against the objective standard of a reasonable and prudent person, regardless of their subjective mental state or individual skill limitations.

6.3 Essential Elements of Negligence

To succeed in an action for negligence, the plaintiff must establish three fundamental elements:

  1. Duty of Care: The defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty of care.
  2. Breach of Duty: The defendant failed to conform to the required standard of care.
  3. Causation and Remoteness of Damage: The breach of duty directly caused actionable damage to the plaintiff.

6.4 Duty of Care & Development of Precedents

  • Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932): Established the general Neighbor Principle. Lord Atkin declared: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbor." A neighbor is defined as anyone so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.
  • Bourhill v. Young (1943): A pregnant woman heard a motorcycle crash 50 feet away and later saw blood on the road, suffering psychiatric shock and giving birth to a stillborn child. Held: The motorcyclist owed her no duty of care because she was not within the foreseeable area of physical danger (lack of proximity).
  • Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills Ltd. (1936): The plaintiff purchased woolen underwear containing hidden excess sulphites and contracted severe dermatitis. Held: Manufacturers owe a direct duty of care to ultimate consumers where there is no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination.

6.5 Breach of Duty & Standard of Care

The standard applied is that of the hypothetical reasonable person. However, specialized rules apply to professionals:

Professional Negligence & The Bolam Test:

  • Bolam v. Friern Hospital Management Committee (1957): Formulated the Bolam Test for professionals: A professional (e.g., a medical doctor) is not negligent if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical professionals skilled in that particular field, even if other experts disagree.
  • Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti (1966): Collapse of a clock tower due to failure to carry out periodic structural maintenance established a clear breach of duty.
  • Cates v. Mongini Bros (1917): A restaurant visitor was injured by a falling ceiling fan due to a latent manufacturing defect inside the metal rod. Held: Not liable because the defect could not be discovered by reasonable inspection.
  • Ashutosh Haribhau Khodwa v. State of Maharashtra (1996): Leaving a surgical towel inside a patient's abdomen during surgery constitutes professional negligence.
  • Theodelina Alphaxad (Minor) v. The Medical Officer i/c Iringa Hospital: Tanzanian medical negligence precedent regarding the operational standard of care in regional public hospitals.

6.6 Damage & Special Legal Maxims

  • Remoteness of Damage & Foreseeability: The harm suffered must be a reasonably foreseeable outcome of the breach, not too remote.
  • Res Ipsa Loquitur ("The thing speaks for itself"): A rule of evidence that shifts the burden of proof to the defendant when:
    1. The instrumentality causing the harm was under the exclusive control of the defendant; and
    2. The accident is of a nature that does not ordinarily occur without negligence.

6.7 Composite Negligence vs. Contributory Negligence

Feature Contributory Negligence Composite Negligence
Definition The plaintiff's own failure to exercise reasonable care for their own safety contributes to their harm (e.g., failure to wear a seatbelt in Davies v. Swan Motor Co.). The combined negligence of two or more independent wrongdoers (composite tortfeasors) causes a single indivisible harm to an innocent third party.
Liability Reduces the plaintiff's damages in proportion to their degree of fault. The wrongdoers are jointly and severally liable to the plaintiff for the full extent of the damage (Parsani Devi v. State of Haryana).

6.8 Defences in Negligence Actions

  • Contributory Negligence (apportionment of blame).
  • Inevitable Accident.
  • Act of God (Vis Major).
  • Volenti Non-Fit Injuria.

Topic 7: Vicarious Liability

7.1 General Principle

Vicarious liability is a legal doctrine under which one person is held liable for the tortious acts committed by another, based on a specific legal relationship between them.

Underlying Maxims:

  • Qui facit per alium facit per se: "He who acts through another is deemed in law to have acted himself."
  • Respondeat Superior: "Let the master answer."

7.2 Categories of Relationships

  • Master and Servant (Employer and Employee): Most common form.
  • Principal and Agent.
  • Partners in a Partnership: Every partner is vicariously liable for torts committed by co-partners in the ordinary course of partnership business (Hamlyn v. Houston & Co. [1903]).
  • Parents and Children: General Rule: Parents are not vicariously liable for torts committed by their children, except where:
    1. The child acts as an agent or servant of the parent; or
    2. The parent's own personal negligence provided the opportunity for the child to cause injury (e.g., Bebee v. Sales [1916], where a father allowed his 15-year-old son to keep an airgun after numerous complaints, resulting in an eye injury to another child).

7.3 Principal and Agent

A principal is liable for the torts of their agent committed within the actual, implied, or apparent scope of authority (intra vires). If the agent acts completely outside their authority (ultra vires), the principal is generally not liable.

  • Tirlock Swah Singh v. Kailash Bharti: Unlawful delegation of driving without the owner's permission relieved the owner of vicarious liability when an accident occurred.
  • Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co. (1912): A firm of solicitors was held vicariously liable for the fraud of their managing clerk committed in the course of transacting conveyancing business, even though the clerk acted for his own personal gain.

7.4 Master and Servant (Course of Employment)

To establish master-servant vicarious liability, the plaintiff must prove:

  1. A master-servant (employee) relationship existed; and
  2. The tortious act was committed by the servant in the course of employment.

Unauthorized Acts vs. Giving Lifts:

An act is within the course of employment if it is an authorized act or an unauthorized mode of performing an authorized act. However, giving unauthorized lifts to passengers on commercial vehicles often falls outside the course of employment, relieving the employer of liability:

  • Twine v. Bean's Express Ltd. (1946): A driver gave a lift to an unauthorized passenger despite express prohibitions by the employer. Held: Employer was not vicariously liable.
  • Conway v. George Wimpey & Co. Ltd. (1951): Similar ruling regarding explicit bans against carrying unauthorized staff on transport lorries.

7.5 Independent Contractors

General Rule: An employer is not liable for torts committed by an independent contractor whom they have hired to execute work.

Distinction: A servant is employed under a Contract of Service (subject to detailed control over how the work is executed). An independent contractor works under a Contract for Services (undertakes to produce a given result, exercising independent discretion on how it is accomplished).

Exceptions (Where Employer IS Liable for Independent Contractor):

  1. Where the employer authorized or ordered the specific wrongful act or tort.
  2. Cases involving Strict Liability (e.g., the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher).
  3. Where the employer was negligent in choosing, checking, or retaining an incompetent contractor.
  4. Breach of non-delegable statutory or common law duties (e.g., inherently dangerous operations on or near highways).

Cases: Morgan v. Incorporated Central Council; Govindaraju v. Govindarajulu.

Topic 8: The Law of Nuisance

8.1 Definition and Origins

Derived from the French word nuire and Latin nocere (meaning to hurt, harm, or annoy). Nuisance refers to an unlawful or unreasonable act, condition, or omission that causes annoyance, harm, or inconvenience to another person's enjoyment of land or public rights.

Minor, unavoidable everyday discomforts that occur in communal life do not amount to actionable nuisance.

8.2 Distinction Between Nuisance and Trespass

  • Trespass to Land: Direct physical interference with land by a tangible object (actionable per se).
  • Nuisance: Indirect, non-trespassory, and usually continuous interference with the use or enjoyment of land (e.g., noise, offensive odors, smoke, vibrations, water pollution, overhanging tree roots). Requires proof of actual discomfort or damage.

8.3 Categories of Nuisance

1. Public Nuisance

  • An unlawful or unreasonable act that interferes with a right common to the general public (e.g., blocking a public road, digging a trench across a highway, polluting public water sources).
  • Legal Status in Tanzania: Public nuisance is criminalized under Section 170(1) & (2) of the Penal Code (Cap 16).
  • Civil Claim Exception: A private individual can only sue for public nuisance in civil court if they can prove they suffered special / particular damage over and above that suffered by the general public (Rose v. Miles [1815]; Ram Raj Singh v. Babulal; Inua Dandu v. Nigerian National Petroleum Corp.).

2. Private Nuisance

  • Unreasonable or unlawful interference with an individual's use, enjoyment, or interest in private land. Private nuisance is a civil wrong in personam.
  • Essential Elements:
    1. The interference must be continuous and unreasonable.
    2. It must relate directly to the use or enjoyment of land.
    3. The plaintiff must prove actual physical damage or substantial sensory discomfort (Geoffrey Mulungu & Another v. John Doctor Thomas [Civil Appeal 246 of 2021, TZ High Court]; Halsey v. Esso Petroleum Co. Ltd. [1961]).

8.4 Remedies and Defences in Nuisance

Remedies:

  • Injunction (court order to cease or restrict the activity).
  • Damages (monetary compensation).
  • Abatement / Self-Help (removal of the nuisance by the aggrieved party, such as trimming overhanging branches).

Defences:

  • Prescription: Exercising the activity openly and without interruption for 20 continuous years gives rise to a legal right.
  • Statutory Authority: The act complained of was explicitly authorized by an Act of Parliament.
  • Inevitability / Act of Necessity.
  • Invalid Defence: "Coming to the nuisance" (i.e., claiming the plaintiff moved to the area where the nuisance was already operating) is not a valid defence.

Topic 9: Tortious Liability Against the Government

9.1 Historical Context & Common Law Doctrine

In classical English common law, the doctrine of Sovereign Immunity prevailed under the Latin maxim: "Lex non potest peccare" (The King can do no wrong). Under this principle, the Crown could not be sued in tort.

This absolute immunity was gradually altered:

  • Magna Carta (1215): First statutory steps limiting executive authority.
  • Crown Proceedings Act of 1947 (UK): Abolished sovereign immunity in tort, enabling citizens to sue the Crown for civil wrongs committed by its servants in the same manner as a private individual (Section 2).

9.2 Legal Framework in Tanzania

In Tanzania, the government (both central government and local government authorities) can be held vicariously liable for torts committed by its agents or officers acting within the scope of their employment (intra vires).

Governing Statutes:

  • Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania (CURT), 1977: Articles 13, 18, and 30.
  • Government Proceedings Act (Cap 5 R.E. 2019): Section 3(1) governs civil claims against the government; Section 6(4) & (7) dictates jurisdiction.
  • Local Government (District Authorities) Act (Cap 287): Sections 12, 19, 26, and 119.
  • Local Government (Urban Authorities) Act (Cap 288): Sections 14 and 106.
  • Interpretation of Laws Act (Cap 1).

9.3 Mandatory Procedural Requirements

  1. Jurisdiction: All tort actions against the Government of Tanzania must be instituted exclusively in the High Court of Tanzania (Section 6, Government Proceedings Act).
  2. Statutory Notice of Intention to Sue: The plaintiff must serve a mandatory 90 days' notice of intention to sue on the government before filing the lawsuit (Section 6(2), GPA; Section 119, Cap 287; Section 106, Cap 288).
  3. Necessary Party: The Attorney General (AG) must be joined as a necessary party in all civil suits against the central government.

9.4 Exceptions & Immunities

Under Section 3(1) of the Government Proceedings Act, no action lies against the government for acts or omissions committed by any person exercising judicial functions or executing judicial processes in good faith.

Execution of Decrees Against the Government:

Executing court decrees against government assets differs from executing against private individuals. Public funds and government property cannot be attached or auctioned directly through ordinary court execution orders (Aloyce Chacha Kenganya v. Mwita Chacha Wambura, Commissioner of Mines Musoma & AG [Civil Case 7 of 2019]).

Topic 10: Occupiers' Liability

10.1 Overview & Statutory Foundation

Just as a driver owes a duty to road users or a doctor to a patient, an occupier of land or structures (buildings, vehicles, ships, airplanes, elevators) owes a legal duty to ensure that visitors do not suffer harm while on the premises.

In Tanzania, this area is regulated by the Occupiers' Liability Act (Section 3), which imposes a statutory obligation on occupiers to exercise reasonable care to keep visitors safe.

10.2 Definition of an Occupier

Lord Denning's Test (Wheat v. E. Lacon & Co. Ltd. [1966]): "Wherever a person has a sufficient degree of control over premises that he ought to realize that any failure on his part to use care may result in injury to a person lawfully coming there, then he is an occupier... It is not necessary for a person to have exclusive occupation or entire control."

10.3 Legal Status of Persons Entering Premises

1. Lawful Visitors

  • Includes invitees (e.g., customers in a store) and licensees (e.g., social guests, delivery personnel).
  • Duty Owed: The occupier owes a Common Duty of Care — a duty to take such care as is reasonable in all the circumstances to see that the visitor will be reasonably safe in using the premises for the purpose for which they were invited or permitted to be there.

2. Children

  • Occupiers must be prepared for children to be less careful than adults. Premises must be free of hidden traps or "allurements" (e.g., unfenced ponds, dangerous machinery, poisonous berries).

3. Trespassers

  • Historically, occupiers owed no duty to trespassers except not to injure them intentionally or recklessly (Addie v. Dumbreck [1929]).
  • Modern Law (British Railways Board v. Herrington [1972]): An occupier owes a duty of "common humanity" to take reasonable steps to warn or protect trespassers against known severe dangers.

10.4 Occupiers' Liability and Independent Contractors

An occupier is generally not liable for damage caused to a visitor by an unsafe condition created by an independent contractor hired by the occupier, provided that:

  1. It was reasonable in the circumstances for the occupier to entrust the work to an independent contractor in the first place.
  2. The occupier took reasonable steps to satisfy themselves that the contractor was competent.
  3. The occupier took reasonable steps (where feasible) to verify that the work was properly executed.

10.5 Defences in Occupiers' Liability

  • Consent / Volenti Non-Fit Injuria: The visitor willingly accepted the risk.
  • Contributory Negligence: The visitor failed to exercise reasonable care for their own safety despite clear warnings or notices provided beforehand.
  • Sufficient Warning: An occupier is not liable if clear, adequate warning signs were displayed enabling the visitor to remain safe.
