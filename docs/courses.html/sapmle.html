TOPIC 1: THE LAW OF EVIDENCE

Academic Notes & Legal Evaluation Framework

1. Fundamental Concepts of Evidence

Evidence presented in a court of law must be duly proved and legally admissible. For example, a documentary instrument generally requires appropriate certification or stamping where mandated by law to be accepted as evidence.

Statutory Provision: Section 3(1) of the Evidence Act provides the statutory interpretation and legal definition of "evidence".

To establish proof, the law provides procedural mechanisms to summon witnesses and adduce testimony, enabling the court to evaluate whether the evidence is sufficient to meet the required standard of proof.

Legal Evaluation & Correction:

Note on "Have evidence without evidence?": In evidentiary jurisprudence, facts may be established without formal physical or oral evidence through Judicial Notice (facts so notorious that proof is unnecessary) or formal Admissions made by parties prior to or during proceedings.

TOPIC 2: RELEVANCY AND ADMISSIBILITY OF EVIDENCE

Academic Notes, Case Law & Legal Evaluation Framework

1. Foundations of Relevancy and Admissibility

Under Section 3(1) of the Law of Evidence Act [Cap. 6 R.E. 2019], a "fact" is not limited merely to tangible objects that can be seen or touched. It includes any mental condition or state of mind of which any person is conscious, such as feelings, emotions, or intentional actions demonstrated in court.

General Principle: Not all facts are admissible as evidence in court. Only facts that are legally relevant and admissible under the Act may be adduced as evidence. If a fact lacks relevancy or fails the legal test of admissibility, it cannot be received as evidence.

Determination of Relevancy [Section 3(1)]

"Fact in Issue" refers to any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature, or extent of any right, liability, or disability asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding necessarily follows.

"Relevancy": One fact is said to be relevant to another when the one is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of the Evidence Act relating to the relevancy of facts.

Legal Evaluation (Adversarial System & Judicial Discretion):

In an adversarial legal system, the parties initially determine which facts to present as relevant. However, the judge or magistrate retains ultimate judicial discretion to rule on whether a piece of evidence is relevant and legally admissible. When a witness's testimony contradicts previous statements, the court assesses credibility (impeachment of witness under Sections 154–157 TEA) before deciding the weight of the evidence.

2. Admissibility and General Rules of Relevancy

Admissibility is a question of law. Some facts may be logically relevant but legally inadmissible due to exclusionary rules (e.g., hearsay or privileged communications). Conversely, facts must first be relevant to be admitted.

General Rule of Relevancy (Section 7 TEA)

  1. Evidence may be given in any suit or proceeding of the existence or non-existence of every fact in issue and of such other facts as are declared to be relevant by the Act, and of no others.
  2. Parties can argue the existence or non-existence of facts in issue or relevant facts.
  3. Evidence adduced must be strictly confined to the relationship with the fact in issue or facts declared relevant.
  4. Only evidence recognized and permitted by law is admissible.

3. Facts Forming Part of the Same Transaction (Res Gestae - Section 8 TEA)

Facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the **same transaction** are relevant, whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places.

  • Contemporaneity Test: Spontaneous statements or acts must be substantially contemporaneous with the fact in issue to exclude the possibility of fabrication or afterthought.
  • Hearsay Exception: Statements forming part of the res gestae are admissible as exceptions to the rule against hearsay, provided they are uttered while the act is occurring or immediately thereafter.
Case Citation Key Legal Principle / Holding
R. v. Bedingfield (1879) Strict contemporaneity rule: Statement made after the act was completed ("Oh dear, see what Bedingfield has done to me") was rejected as not part of res gestae because the act was already over.
Teper v. R [1952] Words spoken must be so clearly associated with the event in time, place, and circumstance that they form part of the item being proved.
Keji v. R (1940) EACA Acts and threats uttered during an attack on a third party immediately preceding the murder of the deceased were admissible as part of the same transaction (res gestae).
Hassan Ismail v. The Crown (1945) Statements made after a delay (e.g., walking home after a rape) were excluded from res gestae due to lack of immediate contemporaneity, though admissible as corroborative conduct.

4. Occasion, Cause, Effect, Motive, Preparation, and Conduct (Sections 9 & 10 TEA)

Facts Which Are Occasion, Cause, or Effect (Section 9 TEA)

Facts are relevant if they are the occasion, cause, or effect (immediate or otherwise) of relevant facts or facts in issue, or if they constituted the state of things under which they happened, or afforded an opportunity for their occurrence or transaction.

  • Occasion: Circumstances creating the opportunity for an act to occur (e.g., victim being alone).
  • Cause: The direct origin or trigger of the event (e.g., poison administered).
  • Effect: The outcome or result of the action (e.g., marks on the body, bloodstains, fingerprints).
  • Opportunity: Situations establishing that the accused had access/opportunity (e.g., defense of Alibi relies on demonstrating a lack of opportunity).

Motive, Preparation, and Conduct (Section 10 TEA)

Under Section 10 of the Evidence Act, any fact is relevant which shows or constitutes a motive or preparation for any fact in issue or relevant fact.

  • Motive vs. Intention: Intention relates to the immediate purpose or act desired, whereas motive refers to the ultimate ulterior reason or emotion inducing a person to act.
  • Preparation: Acts or measures undertaken prior to committing an offense (e.g., buying a weapon, purchasing poison) are relevant if connected to the fact in issue.
  • Subsequent Conduct: Conduct of any party or accused person following an offense—such as absconding, fabricating false evidence, or destroying clues—is admissible as relevant conduct under Section 10(2).
Relevant Case Authorities:
  • John Makindi v. Republic (1961): Admissibility of previous conduct and similar acts under Section 7 & Section 10 TEA.
  • Makin v. Attorney-General for New South Wales [1894] AC 57: Principles governing similar fact evidence and motive.
  • Mohamed Said Matula v. Republic: Establishing the nexus between previous conduct, motive, and facts in issue.

5. Facts Necessary to Explain or Introduce Relevant Facts (Section 11 TEA)

Facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact are admissible in so far as they are necessary for that purpose. Section 11 covers:

  1. Facts necessary to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  2. Facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  3. Facts establishing the identity of any person or thing whose identity is relevant (e.g., identification parades).
  4. Facts establishing the time or place at which any fact in issue or relevant fact occurred.
  5. Facts showing the relation of parties by whom any such fact was transacted.

Topic Review & Examination Questions

Section 11 of the Evidence Act declares relevant facts that are intended either to introduce or explain a fact in issue or relevant fact (such as names, dates, and places, which may be admissible).

  • Introductory or Explanatory Facts: Names, descriptions, places, and times are admissible under sub-section (1).
  • Supporting or Rebutting Inferences: It admits facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  • Identity of Person or Object: Identity may be established using identification parades, DNA, or direct witness testimony.
  • Fixing Time and Place: Facts which fix the exact time and place when or where an event happened.
  • Relationship of Parties: Facts showing the relationship of parties (e.g., cohabitation, employment, agency).
Key Legal Issue: What happens when there is a wrong identification of the accused in an identification parade? (Affects weight and admissibility; requires strict adherence to identification procedures).

Procedure & Rules Requirement for Identification Parade in Tanzania

Identification parades in Tanzania are governed by police procedural guidelines under P.G.O. 232 (Police General Orders) read together with Form 3 (P.F. 186):

  1. The officer shall register the number of persons attending the parade (minimum 8 persons of similar age, height, and general appearance).
  2. He/she will register the number of witnesses under Form 3 / P.F. 186.
  3. The officer-in-charge of the case shall take no part in conducting the parade.
  4. The officer conducting the parade shall inform the suspect of his right to put on parade identification options (e.g., choice of position or presence of advocate/friend).
  5. The place of the parade must have good light.
  6. The suspect may put on the clothing he was wearing when the offense was committed during the parade.
  7. The officer conducting the parade will finally check entries in the identification parade register and sign in the space provided.

2. Conspiracy & Common Intention (Section 12 TEA)

Section 12 TEA: Statements or actions referring to common intention. Anything said, done, or written by a conspirator in reference to their common design is a relevant fact against each of the persons believed to be so conspiring.

Principle of Agency: This section builds upon the agency principle—treating every individual who is involved in a conspiracy as an agent of another co-conspirator.

Two Essential Conditions Required to Apply Section 12:
  1. There must be prima facie / sufficient evidence of an agreement between those parties (conspiracy).
  2. There was a common intention by the parties to execute their plan at the time the act or statement was made.
Case Citation Key Legal Principle / Holding
Hashim Abdalla @ Simba & Omary Hussein @ Lugendo v. Republic Admissibility of co-conspirator acts and statements under common intention.
Stanislaus alias Kayambo s/o Kitambo v. R (1959) Applicability of statements of conspirators in relation to Section 11/12 TEA.
Leonard Mathias Mahani & Another v. Republic Where two or more persons conspire to commit an offense, anything said or done with reference to common desire is relevant.
R. v. Gokaldas Kanji Karia & Alibhai Mamanji (1949) 16 E.A.C.A. 116 Convicted for conspiracy to export diamond from Tanganyika. Court held agreement to conspire may be deduced from any act raising the presumption.
John Moody Lawrence Brown & Another v. R (1957) Proof of common intention and conspiracy through overt acts.

3. Defense of Alibi & Inconsistent Facts (Section 13 TEA)

Section 13 TEA: Facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact, or if by themselves or in connection with other facts they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable.

Definition of Alibi: A form of defense whereby the accused attempts to prove that he/she was elsewhere when the crime in question was committed.

Procedural Requirements for Alibi Defense in Tanzania

  • Notice of Alibi: Governed by Section 194(4) of the Criminal Procedure Act [Cap. 20 R.E. 2019]. The accused must give notice of intention to rely on an alibi during preliminary hearing or before prosecution closes its case.
  • Failure to Plead Early: Failure to give timely notice allows court to draw adverse inference or disallow late alibi evidence without leave.
  • Examples of Supporting Evidence: Eyewitness testimony, time-stamped pictures/video footage placing the accused at another location, bank/ATM receipts, GPS log data.
Relevant Authorities on Alibi & Appellate Review:
  • Tanginya Kajima v. R: Defense of alibi and standard of proof.
  • Raja v. Kotorra (1965) EA 362: Duties regarding alibi setup.
  • Mbuyi v. Dyer / Mshanga v. Tuani Diwani: Evidential burden regarding physical impossibility of presence.
  • Petter Mtingo & 4 Others v. R: Court's duty to evaluate alibi defense alongside prosecution evidence.
  • Peter v. Sunday Post Ltd (1958) EA 424: Appellate court entitlement to reassess evidence where lower court misapprehended substance or quality of evidence.

4. Damages and Customary Rights (Sections 14 & 15 TEA)

Facts Relevant in Suits for Damages (Section 14 TEA)

In suits in which damages are claimed, any fact which will enable the court to determine the amount of damages which ought to be awarded is relevant (whether to mitigate or aggravate damages).

Facts Affecting Existence of Right or Custom (Section 15 TEA)

Where the existence of any right or custom is in question, the following are relevant:

  • Any transaction by which the right or custom was created, claimed, modified, recognized, asserted, or denied.
  • Particular instances in which the right or custom was claimed, recognized, exercised, or disputed.
7 Requirements for Custom to be Valid in Law:
  1. Ancient: Must have existed from time immemorial / long standing.
  2. Continuous & Uniform: Exercised without interruption.
  3. Reasonable: Not contrary to natural justice or good conscience.
  4. Certain: Clear and definite in its scope and application.
  5. Compulsory: Obligatory and not optional.
  6. Peaceable: Enjoyed peaceably without force.
  7. Not Immoral / Not illegal: Not against statutory law or public policy.

5. Facts Showing State of Mind / Body & System (Sections 16 & 17 TEA)

State of Mind or Feeling (Section 16 TEA)

Facts showing the existence of any state of mind (such as intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, rashness, ill-will, or good-will) or state of body/bodily feeling are relevant when the existence of such state of mind or body is in issue.

John Makindi v. Republic: Evidence of similar transactions/previous conduct is admissible to show knowledge, intention, or state of mind, but cannot be used as sole proof of guilt for the specific charged offense.

System / Similar Occurrences (Section 17 TEA)

When there is a question whether an act was accidental or intentional, or done with particular knowledge, the fact that such act formed part of a series of similar occurrences is relevant.

  • Must be read together with Section 16 (state of mind).
  • Requires a specific line of defense alleging lack of intention, accident, or mistake.
  • The similar acts must be closely related in nature and time to show a systematic course of conduct.

6. Course of Business & Judicial Presumptions (Sections 18, 122 & 34B TEA)

Section 18 TEA: When there is a question whether a particular act was done, the existence of any course of business, according to which it naturally would have been done, is a relevant fact.

Section 122 TEA: The Court may presume the existence of any fact which it thinks likely to have happened, regard being had to the common course of natural events, human conduct, and public/private business in relation to the facts of the particular case.

Section 34B TEA: Written statements in criminal proceedings (Proof by written statement under Section 34B of Evidence Act / Section 289 CPA).

Key Examination Question & Summary

QN: Why do we need to study facts that are generally deemed to be relevant?

  • It helps the court decide matters efficiently based on proven logical connections.
  • It helps establish liability/guilt or assists the accused person to raise valid legal defenses (e.g., Alibi, lack of intention).
  • Excludes non-relevant/excluded facts such as pure uncorroborated hearsay or facts not forming part of the same transaction.

LAW OF EVIDENCE: ADMISSIONS & HEARSAY (TOPIC 3)

Transcribed Notes, Statutory Alignment & Case Authorities under the Law of Evidence Act [Cap. 6 R.E. 2019]

1. The General Rule of Direct Evidence & Hearsay

General Rule (Section 62 TEA): Evidence must be direct. Oral evidence must, in all cases whatever, be direct (i.e., given by a witness who saw, heard, or perceived the fact himself).

Exceptions: Where evidence is permitted or given by someone else, it constitutes an exception to the rule against hearsay evidence (e.g., Admissions, Confessions, and Res Gestae).

Definition of Admission (Sections 19–27 TEA): An admission is a statement—oral, electronic, or documentary—which suggests any inference as to any fact in issue or relevant fact, made by any of the persons and under the circumstances specified under the Act.

Note on Admissions: For a statement to qualify as a relevant admission under Sections 19–27 of the TEA, it should generally not be in the self-serving interest of the maker when tendered.

2. Proof and Usage of Admissions (Section 23 TEA)

General Rule under Section 23 TEA: An admission may be proved against the person who makes it (or his representative in interest), but it cannot be proved by or on behalf of the person who makes it, except in specific statutory instances.

  • Prohibition against Self-Serving Statements: A party is not permitted to bring evidence of his or her own admission to support their own case.
  • Legal Maxim / Rationale: No person should be allowed to benefit from their own wrongdoing or self-fabricated statements (Nemo ex suo dolo interdictur).

Statutory Exceptions under Section 23(a)–(c) TEA

An admission may be proved by or on behalf of the person making it under the following exceptions:

  1. Section 23(a): When the statement is of such a nature that, if the person making it were dead, it would be relevant as between third persons under Section 34 TEA (Statements by persons who cannot be called as witnesses).
  2. Section 23(b): When the admission consists of a statement of the existence of any state of mind or body, made at or about the time when such state of mind or body existed, and is accompanied by conduct rendering its falsehood improbable.
  3. Section 23(c): If the statement in question is relevant otherwise than as an admission (i.e., admissible under any other provision of the law).

3. Who Can Make Admissions? (Sections 20–22 TEA)

According to Section 20 of the Evidence Act, admissions can be made by:

  • Parties to the proceeding.
  • Agents authorized by parties.
  • Parties suing or being sued in a representative character (when the statement was made while holding that character).
  • Persons who have a proprietary or pecuniary interest in the subject matter.
  • Persons from whom the parties to the suit have derived their interest.
Specific Statutory Provisions:
  • Section 21 TEA: Statements made by persons whose position must be proved as against a party to the suit are admissions if they would be relevant in a suit brought by or against them.
  • Section 22 TEA: Statements made by persons to whom a party to the suit has expressly referred for information in reference to a matter in dispute are admissions.

4. Oral Admissions, Written Documents & Civil Cases

Oral Admissions as to Contents of Documents (Section 24 TEA)

General Rule: Oral admissions as to the contents of a document are not relevant, unless and until the party proposing to prove them shows that he is entitled to give secondary evidence of the contents under statutory rules, or unless the genuineness of a document produced is in question.

Admissions in Civil Cases (Section 25 TEA)

In civil cases, no admission is relevant if it is made either upon an express condition that evidence of it is not to be given (e.g., "without prejudice" negotiations), or under circumstances from which the court can infer that the parties agreed together that evidence of it should not be given.

Evidential Effect of Admissions (Section 26 TEA)

Section 26 TEA: Admissions are not conclusive proof of the matters admitted, but they may operate as an estoppel to preclude a party from denying what they previously stated.

5. Summary of Key Case Authorities

Case Citation Statutory Relevance & Legal Principle
Erna Akbarali Shariff & Anor v. Bashir Pirosha Sethna & Ors (1963) Application of admissions made by representative parties or interested persons.
Duncan v. Gibson (1968) Application and scope of Section 20 TEA regarding admissions by agents/parties.
Slatterie v. Pooley (1840) Common law principles governing oral admissions as to document contents (Context of Section 22 / 24 TEA).

Core Takeaway: Hearsay vs. Admissions / Res Gestae

If evidence is brought into court for the sole purpose of establishing the truth of the matter stated, it will generally be excluded as hearsay. However, if it is introduced for another legal purpose—such as proving an admission, confession, or res gestae—it is admissible as an exception to the hearsay rule.

TOPIC 4: CONFESSIONS S. 27 TEA | Date: 15/01/2024

Overview: Meaning, Rule of proving it, Different types of Confession and their implication, the Rule of the Court.

1. Meaning of Confession

Refers to the statement that is voluntarily made by the accused person which admits or incriminates the criminal liability by the accused, and this statement is made in the presence of a person authorized to administer confession.

(Constitutes Admission of actus reus and element of mens rea).

2. Admissibility & Evidentiary Value

S. 27(3)(b) of TEA: Confession is considered to be one of the best evidence in criminal proceedings because an accused freely agreed that they were liable.

Principle: No reasonable person would make a statement against their own interest unless it is a truthful matter.

Generally, confession statements are admissible if they were made voluntarily (S. 27 of TEA). However, in instances where the law permits the court to admit a confession obtained through threat, if it is in the opinion of the court that the confession contains nothing but the truth of the matter.

Relevant Cases:

  • Yusuph Svester v. R C.A
  • Josiphat Somisho Maziku v. R 1992

3. Voluntariness and Court Position

While it is true to law that the condition precedent for the admissibility of confession is its voluntary nature, the confession is not automatically admissible simply because it resulted from threat or promise where the inducement or threat was of such a nature as was likely to cause an untrue admission of guilt.

Plea-bargain in Criminal Procedure Act is similar to confession, but this does not constitute the amount of threat or promise which influences the accused to accept or to admit the liability of the offence.

"It is the duty of prosecution to prove that the Confession was voluntarily made."

4. Things to Consider When Dealing with Confession

  1. Persons who confess are considered to be the best witnesses. Thus, the court accepts the liability as voluntary and to be believed because nobody who is in their right sense would accept liability were they not liable in truth.
    DPP v. Nsiru Automatical
  2. There is no mandatory requirement for a confession to be reduced into writing even where it is made orally, it can be admissible if it meets the legal requirements.
  3. The onus of proving that the confession was voluntary rests on the prosecution (S. 27(2) of TEA).
    Jacob Mayan v. Republic Cr. 259 2016 (unreported).
  4. Exculpatory Statement in Confession: Statement that tends to clear a person of blame or guilt (S. 27(1) paragraph 3 of TEA).

    Where the accused made an exculpatory statement, it may as well reduce the statement not to be a confession. Any confession statement which contains exculpatory matter is not a confession.

    Mussa Bakari Ngoronji v. Republic
  5. Confession Leading to Discovery: It is a rule of law that where a confession leads to the discovery of a material object connected to the crime, it is one of the most reliable evidence because the discovery confirms the truthfulness of what was confessed.
    Chamulihu Kirenge v. Republic
  6. Implicating / Including Co-accused: As a general rule, a confession can be used against a co-accused when applied in instances where 2 or more persons are tried jointly either for the same offence or different offences arising in the same transaction. In such instances, if one of the accused persons makes a confession, it is considered against the remaining co-accused. The law, however, requires that where a confession is to be used to secure conviction of a co-accused, there should be corroborating evidence that supports the conviction because a co-accused's confession is among the weakest evidence available (S. 33 of TEA).
    • Simon v. Republic 1974
    • Karanya Simon & Others v. Republic
    • Babe Hamisi & 2 Others v. Republic 2014
    • Jamali Msombo & Another v. R

5. Guiding Principles in Confessions

Case law has established several rules that assist the court when dealing with confession statements:

  • Tuamsi v. Uganda 1967 E.A 91
  • Nyorero Nyague v. R
  • Jumanne Issa and another v. R

Core Guiding Principles:

  1. A confession is presumed to be obtained voluntarily except where an objection is made by an accused.
  2. Where an accused makes an objection to the admissibility of a confession, they may do so on the ground that the confession was not made voluntarily, or it was not made at all, or it was made contrary to the Law.
  3. An objection to the admissibility of a confession must be done when or before a confession was admitted in court.
  4. The court cannot conduct an inquiry to test the voluntariness of a confession on its own motion.
  5. Whenever a confession is objected to at the right moment on the issue of voluntariness or whether the statement was recorded or not, the court must conduct a trial-within-a-trial or inquiry to determine the objection.
  6. In the trial-within-a-trial or inquiry, the court should only deal with whether the accused person made the statement at all or whether it was obtained voluntarily.

6. Repudiated and Retracted Confessions

  • Retracted Confession: Refers to a statement where a person admits making the recorded statement, but claims it was not made voluntarily.
  • Repudiated Confession: Refers to a confession where the accused person argues that they never made such a statement at all.
When there is retraction or repudiation, a conviction can be ruled using the repudiation or retraction, but the court should only do so after it has warned itself on the danger of acting on such confessions. It also requires that the court should satisfy itself that the confession is nothing but the truthfulness of the matter.

Case Law:

  • Kashidijemeri v. Republic (2002) - Stolen goods
  • Muganyizi Peter Michael & 3 Others v. Republic CR 144, 2020 - Murder
  • Kashinge Case - "Doctrine of recent possession"

7. Practical Aspects of Confessions & Cautioned Statements

Date: 29/01/2024

Confession / Cautioned Statement

"Person must make statement after caution"

Refers to the statement made by the suspect of the crime during interrogation or investigation under the rule of Law that the statement given after the statement has been warned as to whether about the fact that their statement may be used as evidence against them. Considering legal provisions and decided cases, the following are guiding principles on Cautioned Statements:

"The principle would not guide the accused to make the conviction against himself that is a matter of principle."

Voluntariness Requirements: A person who makes a statement to police must do so without torture, intimidation, or violation of any means in order to get that statement. TEA mandates the prosecution to prove that even the confession of an accused person was obtained voluntarily, particularly when obtained by the police.

Leonard Mathias Makani & Another v. R Cr.A [2017]:
Observed that if a statement is recorded in the presence of many police officers (even 2 officers), it will not be admissible because ordinary people are often intimidated by police even when they did not commit a crime.

Time Factors & Rights of Suspect During Interrogation

  1. Time factors: Requirement under S. 50(1)(a) of C.P.A: The total time available to interview the suspect is 4 hours, counted from the moment a suspect is arrested. S. 51 provides the procedure and conditions under which that period can be extended.
  2. Rights of suspect during interrogation: In the course of interrogation, an accused person is entitled to certain rights including:
    1. Right to know the identity of the officer who is interrogating them.
    2. Right to know the offence for which they are suspected before the statement is taken.
    3. Right to be warned that the statement provided will be used as evidence against them (Cautioned Statement).
    4. Right to choose whether to answer questions or not to state in court proceedings.
    5. Right to communicate and to have a presence in the interrogation room of either a lawyer, friend, or trusted relative.
  3. Satisfaction by Recording Officer: At the end of interrogation, the law requires that the recording officer must be satisfied that a statement given by the suspect was free and truthful (S. 57 of C.P.A).
    Juma Omari v. Republic Cr. 568 (2020) Court of Appeal.
  4. Right to Comment before Admission: Refers to the accused's right to comment on the statement before it is admitted into evidence. This procedural requirement requires the court to give an opportunity to the accused to make any observation regarding their statement before it is used as evidence. The accused can challenge it on grounds of voluntariness or clarity. Failure to give an accused person that opportunity is said to be a violation of fair trial.
    Jumampili Mrote v. R Cr 14
  5. Requirement to Read Statement After Submission: The accused should be informed about the contents of the statement. This requirement seeks to ensure that an accused person is aware of the evidence used by the prosecution so they can prepare their defense. The reading should be after it is admitted, not before. Reading it before admission may lead to prejudice, treating an extra-judicial statement as evidence before the court decides to admit it.
    Samwel Henry Juma v. Republic Cr.A 207 [2011]
    (Reading the statement before admitting it in fact means that statement may be expunged/taken out from the record of the statement).

8. Extra-Judicial Statements

A generic title addressing a statement made in the presence of a Justice of the Peace or a Magistrate. S. 28 of TEA provides that a confession statement contained in an extra-judicial statement is admissible as a matter of procedural requirement. The accused person or suspect must be brought before a Justice of the Peace or Magistrate, and signed along with the Justice of the Peace/Magistrate's signature.

The requirement to sign was emphasized by the Court of Appeal in Pomba Mathenena v. R.

Conditions for Extra-Judicial Statements:

  1. Time and date of the arrest.
  2. The place of the arrest.
  3. The place the accused person was kept before being taken to the Justice of the Peace.
  4. It must show whether there was any threat or promise that persuaded the accused to give the statement.
  5. A caution that the statement can be used as evidence against the maker.

9. Trial-Within-a-Trial (Enquiry)

Refers to a mini court process conducted when determining the admissibility of evidence of a confession that has been objected to. In a Subordinate Court, it is called an Enquiry; in the High Court, it is called a Trial-within-a-Trial.

As a matter of procedure, the accused person must be given an opportunity before evidence is admitted to object to the admissibility of that statement on grounds of voluntariness or repudiation. The main trial will stop to allow an enquiry or trial-within-a-trial.

Nyaura Patrice v. Republic Cr. 703 of 2015:
Failure to conduct an enquiry or trial-within-a-trial where the objection was on voluntariness or repudiation is a fatal error by the appellate court.

  1. Persons who confess are considered to be the best witnesses. Thus, the court accepts the liability as voluntary and to be believed because nobody who is in their right sense would accept liability were they not liable in truth.
    DPP v. Nsiru Automatical
  2. There is no mandatory requirement for a confession to be reduced into writing even where it is made orally, it can be admissible if it meets the legal requirements.
  3. The onus of proving that the confession was voluntary rests on the prosecution (S. 27(2) of TEA).
    Jacob Mayan v. Republic Cr. 259 2016 (unreported).
  4. Exculpatory Statement in Confession: Statement that tends to clear a person of blame or guilt (S. 27(1) paragraph 3 of TEA).

    Where the accused made an exculpatory statement, it may as well reduce the statement not to be a confession. Any confession statement which contains exculpatory matter is not a confession.

    Mussa Bakari Ngoronji v. Republic
  5. Confession Leading to Discovery: It is a rule of law that where a confession leads to the discovery of a material object connected to the crime, it is one of the most reliable evidence because the discovery confirms the truthfulness of what was confessed.
    Chamulihu Kirenge v. Republic
  6. Implicating / Including Co-accused: As a general rule, a confession can be used against a co-accused when applied in instances where 2 or more persons are tried jointly either for the same offence or different offences arising in the same transaction. In such instances, if one of the accused persons makes a confession, it is considered against the remaining co-accused. The law, however, requires that where a confession is to be used to secure conviction of a co-accused, there should be corroborating evidence that supports the conviction because a co-accused's confession is among the weakest evidence available (S. 33 of TEA).

    • Karanya Simon & Others v. Republic
    • Babe Hamisi & 2 Others v. Republic 2014
    • Jamali Msombo & Another v. R

5. Guiding Principles in Confessions

Case law has established several rules that assist the court when dealing with confession statements:

  • Tuamsi v. Uganda 1967 E.A 91
  • Nyorero Nyague v. R
  • Jumanne Issa and another v. R

Core Guiding Principles:

  1. A confession is presumed to be obtained voluntarily except where an objection is made by an accused.
  2. Where an accused makes an objection to the admissibility of a confession, they may do so on the ground that the confession was not made voluntarily, or it was not made at all, or it was made contrary to the Law.
  3. An objection to the admissibility of a confession must be done when or before a confession was admitted in court.
  4. The court cannot conduct an inquiry to test the voluntariness of a confession on its own motion.
  5. Whenever a confession is objected to at the right moment on the issue of voluntariness or whether the statement was recorded or not, the court must conduct a trial-within-a-trial or inquiry to determine the objection.
  6. In the trial-within-a-trial or inquiry, the court should only deal with whether the accused person made the statement at all or whether it was obtained voluntarily.

6. Repudiated and Retracted Confessions

  • Retracted Confession: Refers to a statement where a person admits making the recorded statement, but claims it was not made voluntarily.
  • Repudiated Confession: Refers to a confession where the accused person argues that they never made such a statement at all.
When there is retraction or repudiation, a conviction can be ruled using the repudiation or retraction, but the court should only do so after it has warned itself on the danger of acting on such confessions. It also requires that the court should satisfy itself that the confession is nothing but the truthfulness of the matter.

Case Law:

  • Kashidijemeri v. Republic (2002) - Stolen goods
  • Muganyizi Peter Michael & 3 Others v. Republic CR 144, 2020 - Murder
  • Kashinge Case - "Doctrine of recent possession"

7. Practical Aspects of Confessions & Cautioned Statements

Date: 29/01/2024

Confession / Cautioned Statement

"Person must make statement after caution"

Refers to the statement made by the suspect of the crime during interrogation or investigation under the rule of Law that the statement given after the statement has been warned as to whether about the fact that their statement may be used as evidence against them. Considering legal provisions and decided cases, the following are guiding principles on Cautioned Statements:

"The principle would not guide the accused to make the conviction against himself that is a matter of principle."

Voluntariness Requirements: A person who makes a statement to police must do so without torture, intimidation, or violation of any means in order to get that statement. TEA mandates the prosecution to prove that even the confession of an accused person was obtained voluntarily, particularly when obtained by the police.

Leonard Mathias Makani & Another v. R Cr.A [2017]:
Observed that if a statement is recorded in the presence of many police officers (even 2 officers), it will not be admissible because ordinary people are often intimidated by police even when they did not commit a crime.

Time Factors & Rights of Suspect During Interrogation

  1. Time factors: Requirement under S. 50(1)(a) of C.P.A: The total time available to interview the suspect is 4 hours, counted from the moment a suspect is arrested. S. 51 provides the procedure and conditions under which that period can be extended.
  2. Rights of suspect during interrogation: In the course of interrogation, an accused person is entitled to certain rights including:
    1. Right to know the identity of the officer who is interrogating them.
    2. Right to know the offence for which they are suspected before the statement is taken.
    3. Right to be warned that the statement provided will be used as evidence against them (Cautioned Statement).
    4. Right to choose whether to answer questions or not to state in court proceedings.
    5. Right to communicate and to have a presence in the interrogation room of either a lawyer, friend, or trusted relative.
  3. Satisfaction by Recording Officer: At the end of interrogation, the law requires that the recording officer must be satisfied that a statement given by the suspect was free and truthful (S. 57 of C.P.A).
    Juma Omari v. Republic Cr. 568 (2020) Court of Appeal.
  4. Right to Comment before Admission: Refers to the accused's right to comment on the statement before it is admitted into evidence. This procedural requirement requires the court to give an opportunity to the accused to make any observation regarding their statement before it is used as evidence. The accused can challenge it on grounds of voluntariness or clarity. Failure to give an accused person that opportunity is said to be a violation of fair trial.
    Jumampili Mrote v. R Cr 14
  5. Requirement to Read Statement After Submission: The accused should be informed about the contents of the statement. This requirement seeks to ensure that an accused person is aware of the evidence used by the prosecution so they can prepare their defense. The reading should be after it is admitted, not before. Reading it before admission may lead to prejudice, treating an extra-judicial statement as evidence before the court decides to admit it.
    Samwel Henry Juma v. Republic Cr.A 207 [2011]
    (Reading the statement before admitting it in fact means that statement may be expunged/taken out from the record of the statement).

8. Extra-Judicial Statements

A generic title addressing a statement made in the presence of a Justice of the Peace or a Magistrate. S. 28 of TEA provides that a confession statement contained in an extra-judicial statement is admissible as a matter of procedural requirement. The accused person or suspect must be brought before a Justice of the Peace or Magistrate, and signed along with the Justice of the Peace/Magistrate's signature.

The requirement to sign was emphasized by the Court of Appeal in Pomba Mathenena v. R.

Conditions for Extra-Judicial Statements:

  1. Time and date of the arrest.
  2. The place of the arrest.
  3. The place the accused person was kept before being taken to the Justice of the Peace.
  4. It must show whether there was any threat or promise that persuaded the accused to give the statement.
  5. A caution that the statement can be used as evidence against the maker.

9. Trial-Within-a-Trial (Enquiry)

Refers to a mini court process conducted when determining the admissibility of evidence of a confession that has been objected to. In a Subordinate Court, it is called an Enquiry; in the High Court, it is called a Trial-within-a-Trial.

As a matter of procedure, the accused person must be given an opportunity before evidence is admitted to object to the admissibility of that statement on grounds of voluntariness or repudiation. The main trial will stop to allow an enquiry or trial-within-a-trial.

Nyaura Patrice v. Republic Cr. 703 of 2015:
Failure to conduct an enquiry or trial-within-a-trial where the objection was on voluntariness or repudiation is a fatal error by the appellate court.
