HEARSAY EVIDENCE & DYING DECLARATION S. 62(1) & S. 34 TEA

1. Definition & Fundamental Nature of Hearsay Evidence

Is the piece of evidence Hearsay? That is a kind of evidence which is provided by a third party to the matter, which was not present within the event which occurred, but heard it from another person. Under s. 62(1) of TEA, direct oral evidence is required.

Impact on Legal Proceedings: When the court entertains hearsay evidence, it leads to an unfair hearing or proceeding, which may result in the conviction of an innocent person or the acquittal of a guilty person, because there is a possibility of providing untrue information in the matter.

The general rule is against hearsay. An out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement is hearsay under s. 62(1).

2. Definition under Section 3 of TEA & Core Elements

According to s. 3 of TEA, oral evidence means "all statements which the Court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses being physically present at the time of making the statement or by use of other means of communication including teleconference or video conference, in relation to matters of fact under inquiry."

From the definition, we get 3 main elements of hearsay:

  1. An out-of-court statement.
  2. A statement made by a human being.
  3. Offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted.

Detailed Breakdown of Elements

i. Out of Court: Anything said outside the four walls of a courtroom amounts to being hearsay. It includes what happens out of the trial or the matter brought into court (e.g., statements made in another courtroom, said in the past, or said in a different case).

ii. Statement: An assertion made by a human being. It can be:

  • Orally made
  • Written (letter, postcard, email, fax, etc.)
  • Non-verbal (head nod, head shake, thumbs up or down)

iii. Truth of the Matter Asserted: Occurs when the presenter uses the statement provided by the assertor/declarant to prove the truth of the matter in the court of law. It is hearsay and otherwise inadmissible unless it constitutes an Admission, Confession, or Res Gestae.

Key Consideration: What purpose is the party seeking to achieve by bringing this piece of evidence to the court?

3. Exceptions to Hearsay Evidence (Part B)

Is there an exception that would allow hearsay evidence to be admissible? Yes, the Law of Evidence Act (TEA) contains exceptions, such as Confessions, Admissions, and statements under s. 34.

Under s. 34 of TEA, oral or written statements of relevant facts made by a person are admissible when the person:

  1. Is dead.
  2. Has become incapable of giving evidence.
  3. Cannot be found.
  4. Whose attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay or expense.

4. Dying Declaration (D.D)

A statement of a person who has died is admissible as evidence if it relates to the cause of death of that person.

  • D.D is the generic name given to such a statement, and it can serve to prove the facts of the matter in cases including murder or manslaughter.
  • The law provides specific conditions before a D.D can be admitted in evidence.

Conditions for Admissibility of Dying Declaration

  1. Cause of Death Requirement: The subject matter of the statement must be the cause of death, and the person who made the statement must now be deceased.
  2. Direct Relationship: It must be a statement that directly relates to the cause of that death.
  3. Issue of Death: When the cause of death is the main issue, the statement should explain how the deceased met their death.
  4. Identity of the Maker: The D.D must relate to the cause of death of the declarant themselves, not somebody else's death. If proof shows the declarant died from other unrelated causes, such a statement is not a valid D.D (R. v. Abed Kalonga (1938)).

Jurisdictional Variations (Common Law vs. Tanzania)

Under Common Law, D.D is only admissible in cases of murder or manslaughter. In Tanzania, courts have interpreted D.D to apply in civil matters involving the cause of death (e.g., partition/succession context).

At Common Law, a D.D can only be admitted if the maker was in actual expectation of death. In Tanzania, a D.D is admissible irrespective of whether the person was or was not in expectation of death at the time of making it.

Key Case Law on Dying Declarations

  • Pius Nsangu v. R. (1954) 21 EACA 387: The court observed that a D.D taken by asking leading questions is admissible; however, the court must exercise caution when using it.
  • R. v. Mitchell: The court held that the declaration must be recorded in the actual words of the deceased.

5. Reliability, Corroboration & Prudence

As a general rule, an incomplete or uncorroborated D.D requires careful judicial scrutiny:

  • Factors Affecting Weight: The court will consider any abnormal factors such as the declarant's capacity to remember facts, physical or mental impairment, or external influences acting upon the maker at the time of making the statement.
  • Rule of Prudence: As a matter of prudence, a Dying Declaration requires corroborating evidence to support a conviction, except where the declaration is complete, clear, and the maker is shown to have been fully certain of what they stated.
